# Phonologically Informed Tokenization for German Speech Recognition: A Cross-Domain Study

Christopher Witzl    Tobias Bocklet    Korbinian Riedhammer
Affiliation: Technische Hochschule Nürnberg Georg Simon Ohm, Nürnberg,
Germany
Email: [{christopher.witzl, tobias.bocklet, korbinian.riedhammer}@th-nuernberg.de](mailto:)

###### Abstract

German is a morphologically rich language whose syllable structure is
exceptionally well-predicted by the Knuth–Liang hyphenation algorithm.
We ask whether phonologically informed tokenization can serve as a
competitive target for end-to-end speech recognition. We compare three
tokenizer families on the Omnilingual ASR wav2vec 2.0 backbone
fine-tuned with CTC: the pretrained multilingual character inventory, a
data-driven Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) over orthography, and
phonologically informed units from Pyphen syllabification and
grapheme-to-phoneme conversion. Across 40 fine-tunes, we evaluate on
three German test sets spanning orthogonal shifts: in-domain read
speech, dialectal spontaneous speech, and standard-German spontaneous
speech. In-domain, all phonologically informed tokenizers match BPE and
the multilingual character baseline on both WER and CER. Under domain
shift the picture splits along vocabulary size rather than the
linguistic axis of variation: at small vocabularies, syllable-aware
tokenization improves on dialectal speech, where phonetic surface forms
vary but syllable structure is preserved, and stays ahead on spontaneous
speech, where new word-forms violate vocabulary closure. A phoneme-level
confusion analysis further shows that all tokenizers commit the same
canonical function-word errors, indicating that the acoustic encoder,
not the tokenizer, dominates the error topology. Our findings suggest
that tokenizer choice may depend on the vocabulary budget as much as on
the distribution shift expected at deployment rather than reducing to a
single universal optimum.

## 1 Introduction

Modern end-to-end automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems built on
self-supervised pretraining (Baevski et al., 2020) couple an acoustic
encoder with an output tokenizer that determines the units the network
must predict. The de facto default has converged on two choices: a
data-driven Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) (Sennrich et al., 2016) fitted to
the training transcripts, or a fixed multilingual character inventory
inherited from the pretrained ASR model (Omnilingual ASR team et al.,
2025). Both are phonologically agnostic. The first treats character
$`n`$-grams as merge candidates without regard to syllable or morpheme
structure; the second models every character of every supported script
as an independent unit, leaving word-level structure to the acoustic
encoder. The choice is not cosmetic: the tokenizer fixes which strings
the network can emit, how many target units per word the CTC objective
must align, and how the model degrades on word-forms unseen in training.

This convergence is convenient, but it hides a methodological question
with real downstream consequences: do _phonologically informed_
tokenizers¹¹ 1 I.e., tokenizers whose boundaries derive from linguistic
knowledge (syllabification, grapheme-to-phoneme mappings) rather than
data-driven frequency statistics., those that align with the syllabic
and phonemic structure of the target language, offer something that
data-driven and multilingual defaults cannot? German is a natural test
case: it is morphologically rich (productive compounding, inflection),
it has substantial dialectal variation, and large-scale German ASR
benchmarks (Common Voice (Ardila et al., 2020), Spoken Wikipedia
Corpus (Köhn et al., 2016)) coexist with structurally different
out-of-distribution (OOD) corpora, namely dialectal spontaneous speech
(BAS RVG 1, Burger and Schiel, 1998) and standard-German spontaneous
dialogues (Verbmobil, Burger et al., 2000). The language thus offers two
distinct OOD axes against which tokenizer behavior can be probed: RVG 1
varies the _phonetic surface_ (a largely shared lexicon realized across
nine dialect regions), while Verbmobil varies the _lexical inventory_
(standard-German phonetics but negotiation-dialogue vocabulary including
hesitations, discourse particles, and colloquial verb forms unseen at
training time).

#### Contributions.

We make three contributions. (1) We assemble and train 40 CTC fine-tunes
of the Omnilingual ASR wav2vec 2.0 encoder (Omnilingual ASR team et al., 2025) (31 tokenizers at $`300\,`$M, nine at $`1\,`$B) that share
encoder, training data, and training schedule, varying only the
tokenizer: 13 orthographic tokenizers (character, BPE-512/1024/2048,
syllable-512/1024/2048, multi-syllable with and without syllable
separators) and 18 phoneme-track tokenizers (raw IPA, phoneme-BPE,
phoneme-syllable, phoneme-multi-syllable; vocabulary sizes
$`43`$–$`3{,}840`$). The matrix is large enough to dissociate vocabulary
size from tokenization strategy. (2) We evaluate every model on the
in-domain test set _and_ on two structurally orthogonal OOD test sets:
dialectal spontaneous speech (RVG 1) and standard-German negotiation
dialogues (Verbmobil), quantifying _when_ the phonological bias helps,
_where_ it stays competitive, and _how_ its payoff depends on vocabulary
size. (3) We complement aggregate WER with paired-bootstrap significance
testing (Bisani and Ney, 2004) and confusion-matrix analyses; we focus
on WER and CER rather than token-level metrics, since the latter
conflate recognition quality with tokenizer compression rate and are not
directly comparable across the very different vocabulary sizes (43 to
10,288 tokens) in our design space.

#### Headline result.

Phonologically informed tokenizers cluster within the confidence
intervals of BPE and character baselines in-domain. Under
dialectal-phonetic OOD they show a small advantage that sits at the
Bonferroni boundary ($`-1.09`$ pp WER on RVG 1-ORT, $`p{=}0.019`$,
threshold $`\alpha/k{\approx}0.017`$ for $`k{=}3`$ tests on this
dataset), and under spontaneous-lexical OOD a small lead ($`-0.89`$ pp
WER for syllable-512 on Verbmobil), giving a characterization of the
phonological-bias trade-off rather than a single “best tokenizer”. That
trade-off runs along vocabulary size rather than along the type of
shift: the advantage reverses on both corpora at $`|V|{=}2048`$ (§4.5).

## 2 Related Work

The choice of output unit in ASR has recurred as a design question
through every architectural paradigm. Classical large-vocabulary systems
combined pronunciation lexica, context-dependent triphones, and language
models (Lee et al., 1990; Young et al., 1994), later composed through
weighted finite-state transducers (Mohri et al., 2002). For German
specifically, Killer et al. (2003) found that graphemes match phonemes
in GMM-HMM systems, while Mousa et al. (2012) reported that
morpheme-level language models lower out-of-vocabulary rates. Both
findings are early evidence that linguistic units remain competitive
in-domain, with conditional advantages elsewhere.

The unit question survived the neural transition. Hybrid NN/HMM systems
(Bourlard and Morgan, 1994) retained phonemic states as targets;
end-to-end CTC reopened the question, with Graves et al. (2013)
reestablishing phoneme-CTC on TIMIT. Sennrich et al. (2016) introduced
Byte-Pair Encoding for neural machine translation, and Zenkel et al.
(2018) adapted it to CTC ASR with a key contrast between _Subword_ units
(merges constrained to within words) and _Crossword_ units (merges
allowed across word boundaries). Their distinction maps directly onto
our _syllable_ vs. _multisyl_ contrast at the syllable rather than word
level.

Irie et al. (2019) compared graphemes, phonemes, and wordpieces on
LibriSpeech and found graphemes and wordpieces dominate without an
external language model; for German, Khosravani et al. (2020) found BPE
and unigram subwords to outperform character- and word-level outputs
(with $`n`$-gram LMs). Sainath et al. (2018) found that graphemes
largely obviate the lexicon, whereas Zeineldeen et al. (2020) responded
that phoneme-BPE with homophone disambiguation remains competitive. A
separate line of work derives subword vocabularies from pronunciation
information (Xu et al., 2019; Papadourakis et al., 2021; Zhou et al.,
2021). Most directly related to our phoneme track, Klumpp et al. (2022)
released the Common Phone corpus and trained a wav2vec 2.0 phoneme
recognizer covering six languages including German, achieving $`18.1`$ %
PER across $`101`$ phonetic symbols; our phoneme-track models follow the
same acoustic-modeling setup but vary the tokenizer.

Syllable-based ASR has a thinner literature. Ganapathiraju et al. (2001)
demonstrated syllabic acoustic units in HMM-LVCSR on Switchboard; Qu
et al. (2017) trained syllable-level CTC for Mandarin; and Manohar
et al. (2023) applied Syllable-BPE to Malayalam, the closest
cross-linguistic precedent to our German Pyphen-based syllable
tokenizers (Liang, 1983; Pyphen developers, 2023). None of these works
evaluated tokenizers under structured domain shift, which is the gap our
paper addresses.

A complementary literature on ASR robustness holds the tokenizer fixed
and varies the acoustic side. Likhomanenko et al. (2021) established
that single-corpus WER is a poor robustness proxy; Hsu et al. (2021)
showed that encoder-side adaptation closes $`66`$–$`73`$ % of the OOD
gap in wav2vec 2.0; and Radford et al. (2023) framed robustness through
weak-supervision scale. Our results are consistent with the
encoder-as-primary-lever finding (confusion topology is largely
tokenizer-invariant in our setup), but tokenizer choice acts as a
complementary lever whose payoff depends on the OOD axis and vocabulary
size. We extend this lineage by holding the encoder fixed and varying
only the output tokenizer, isolating the tokenization effect from the
lexicon, decoder, and training-scale variables that confounded earlier
comparisons.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Datasets

Training data is a $`961`$-hour mixture of German Common Voice (Ardila
et al., 2020) and the Spoken Wikipedia Corpus (Köhn et al., 2016;
Baumann et al., 2019, Oct. 2017 release;). Evaluation uses three
structurally distinct test sets: the held-out portion of the same
mixture (CV+SWC test, in-domain), BAS RVG 1 (Burger and Schiel, 1998),
and BAS Verbmobil VM1 (Burger et al., 2000; Schiel, 1999). Table 1
summarizes the training mixture and the three evaluation sets.

Table 1: Corpora. Training uses the CV 24 + SWC mixture; the in-domain
test split is held out from the same mixture; RVG 1 and Verbmobil are
entirely unseen and pooled across their train/dev/test splits (§3.8).
Both OOD corpora carry parallel ORT (canonical orthography) and TR2
(dialect-/prosody-annotated transliteration) tiers. Verbmobil VM1 also
contains English negotiation dialogues; we evaluate on its German subset
only (§3.8).

The two OOD corpora are intentionally orthogonal. RVG 1 is spontaneous
speech recorded across nine German dialect regions; it varies the
phonetic realization while the underlying lexicon remains largely shared
across regions. Verbmobil VM1 is spontaneous standard-German negotiation
speech; it keeps the phonetic norm but introduces unfamiliar word-forms
(hesitations, repairs, colloquial shortenings, discourse particles).
Each corpus therefore stresses a different tokenizer property: phonetic
robustness for RVG 1, lexical coverage for Verbmobil. Both provide
parallel ORT (canonical orthography) and TR2 (dialect transliteration
with prosody markers) reference tiers, which we report separately.

### 3.2 Tokenizer design space

We compare three classes of tokenizers, deliberately matched for
vocabulary size where possible. Table 2 shows what the smallest variant
of each family produces on a single illustrative example, which we
discuss in §3.7.

### 3.3 Multilingual character inventory

Omni-vocab is the publicly released omniASR_tokenizer_written_v2 of
Omnilingual ASR (Omnilingual ASR team et al., 2025), declared as
tokenizer_family: char_tokenizer in the model card: a character-level
tokenizer with $`\approx 10{,}288`$ multilingual code-points covering
1,600+ languages, shipped as the default for all v2 architectures (CTC
and LLM checkpoints from $`300\,`$M to $`7\,`$B). On our German training
data, only $`59`$ of these code-points occur more than $`100`$ times
each (top-$`50`$ coverage $`>`$$`99.99`$ % of all token occurrences); a
long tail of $`\sim`$$`600`$ further code-points appears as rare
orthographic artifacts. Omni-vocab serves as the “no phonological bias”
comparator inherited from the pretrained ASR model.

### 3.4 Data-driven subword

German-BPE is a standard SentencePiece BPE (Sennrich et al., 2016; Kudo
and Richardson, 2018) fitted to the German training transcripts at
vocabularies $`\{512,1024,2048\}`$. It serves as the data-driven
comparator and is the de facto default in many modern German ASR
systems.

### 3.5 Phonologically informed orthographic tokenizers

We construct three orthographic families on top of SentencePiece BPE
(Sennrich et al., 2016; Kudo and Richardson, 2018), differing only in
which symbols are protected as user-defined symbols (UDS) and whether
the training text is pre-syllabified. Syllable-$`k`$ uses the
Knuth–Liang hyphenation algorithm (Liang, 1983) via Pyphen (Pyphen
developers, 2023) to syllabify the training transcripts; the syllable
separator - is registered as a UDS so that no BPE merge crosses a
syllable boundary. Vocabularies are $`k\in\{512,1024,2048\}`$.
Multisyl-$`k`$ is trained on the same syllabified text, but the
separator - is _not_ protected, allowing merges to cross syllable
boundaries; in addition, the $`100`$ most frequent German syllables are
pre-registered as UDS so the model retains the most useful
multi-character atoms even at small vocabulary sizes. This corresponds
conceptually to Zenkel et al.’s (2018) _Crossword_ variant but operates
at the syllable rather than word level. Multisyl-nosep-$`k`$ keeps the
same $`100`$-syllable UDS list as Multisyl but is trained on _raw_,
non-syllabified text, so no syllable-separator token ever appears in the
inputs. It isolates the effect of having syllables as atomic vocabulary
entries from the effect of an explicit syllable-boundary marker.

### 3.6 Phonologically informed phoneme tokenizers

The phoneme track applies the same four merge strategies (raw, BPE,
syllable, multi-syllable) to G2P-derived X-SAMPA phoneme sequences
(Bisani and Ney, 2008). The G2P model is a joint-sequence WFST built
with Phonetisaurus (Novak et al., 2012) and trained on the German
pronunciation lexicon shipped with MaryTTS (Schröder and Trouvain, 2003)
(de.txt, $`\sim`$$`31`$k entries), giving canonical SAMPA transcriptions
for every word in our training and test transcripts. Out-of-lexicon
words are handled by Phonetisaurus’ default joint-sequence backoff
(beam size $`20`$); compound forms are not pre-decomposed and are mapped
as whole tokens. The 18 resulting phoneme-token configurations span
vocabulary sizes from $`43`$ (the smallest character-level raw variant)
to $`3{,}840`$ (the largest multi-syllable BPE). The phoneme target
inherits the German MAUS/BAS tradition (Schiel, 1999) and parallels
Common Phone’s wav2vec 2.0 phoneme recognition setup (Klumpp et al.,
2022).

### 3.8 Evaluation protocol

We report token-weighted WER and CER for orthographic models and PER and
CER for phoneme-track models, both with $`95`$ % paired-bootstrap
confidence intervals (CIs; $`n_{\mathrm{boot}}{=}10{,}000`$,
seed=$`42`$, percentile method) (Bisani and Ney, 2004). For the
orthographic track, references and hypotheses are normalized before
scoring (lowercase, ß $`\!\to\!`$ ss, punctuation stripped, whitespace
collapsed); this matches the standard Whisper-style protocol and ensures
that capitalization conventions of the reference (Verbmobil
business-style transcripts use Sie/Termin/Mai capitalization, while
CV+SWC are lowercase) do not contaminate the recognition metric.
Phoneme-track (X-SAMPA) evaluation is not normalized, since SAMPA
symbols would be destroyed by punctuation stripping. Pairwise
comparisons apply per-dataset Bonferroni correction. For the OOD corpora
we pool train+dev+test (none seen at training time, which justifies
pooling for tighter intervals at no leakage cost). Verbmobil VM1
contains English as well as German negotiation dialogues; we score its
German subset ($`13{,}633`$ utterances, $`33.8`$ h), identified from the
reference transcripts by a German/English function-word criterion. Audio
is $`16\,`$kHz mono and capped at $`60\,`$s for CV+SWC and Verbmobil
(matching the external baselines); RVG 1’s full-length recordings, the
only ones routinely exceeding $`60\,`$s, are instead segmented at
silence boundaries into $`{\leq}60\,`$s chunks so the complete utterance
is scored rather than truncated. Decoding is greedy CTC without an
external language model: a token-level LM would tie the decoding
vocabulary to the tokenizer under study and conflate its
language-modeling contribution with the acoustic comparison. Every
WER/CER/PER reported here therefore reflects the encoder plus
tokenizer-specific head in isolation; LM rescoring, and the tokenizer–LM
interaction it opens up, is left to future work (§5).

## 4 Experiments and Results

### 4.1 Setup

We train 40 CTC fine-tunes (Graves et al., 2006) of the Omnilingual ASR
wav2vec 2.0 backbone (Baevski et al., 2020; Omnilingual ASR team et al.,
2025): all 31 tokenizers (13 orthographic, 18 phoneme-track) at
$`300\,`$M, and a nine-tokenizer subset at $`1\,`$B. The subset
comprises the full vocabulary sweep of Syllable and German-BPE plus one
variant per remaining family (omni-vocab, multisyl-512,
multisyl-nosep-512). All runs use the Adam optimizer with a fixed
learning rate of $`5{\times}10^{-5}`$, bfloat16 mixed precision, no
encoder freezing, $`20\,`$k optimization steps, and a batched dynamic
budget of $`\approx`$$`2.3{\times}10^{7}`$ audio elements per gradient
step (min/max audio $`2`$/$`60\,`$s). Batching is audio-element-based
rather than token-based: every run sees the same audio in the same order
(identical seed) for the same $`20\,`$k updates, and the batch size is
never adjusted per tokenizer. The number of target tokens therefore
varies with tokenizer fertility; we deliberately do not match it, since
fixing the token count would unfix the audio budget and confound the
comparison with the amount of acoustic evidence. Appendix C reports the
resulting target-token counts per run. The CTC output head is a single
linear projection from the final encoder layer to the tokenizer’s
vocabulary size (no layer selection was performed), with Xavier-uniform
weight initialization and zero-initialized bias; no final-layer dropout
is applied. Augmentation, label smoothing, validation schedule, and
early-stopping criterion are held identical across all runs so that any
cross-tokenizer difference is attributable to the output unit and its
head alone. We organize the empirical comparison around three research
questions, each probing one of our three test sets, complemented by
paired significance tests for nine focal comparisons.

### 4.4 Q3: Spontaneous-lexical OOD (Verbmobil)

Table 6 (right half) shows a much flatter picture. On the ORT tier,
syllable-512-1b reaches the lowest WER at $`\mathbf{21.27}`$ %, ahead of
multisyl-512-1b ($`22.05`$ %) and omni-vocab-1b ($`22.16`$ %), which are
statistically indistinguishable from each other (C1-vm in Table 7);
syllable-1024 leads on CER ($`13.51`$ vs. $`14.08`$ for omni-vocab). All
German-BPE variants and syllable-2048 fall between $`24.17`$ and
$`26.95`$ %, at least $`2`$ pp behind these leaders. The TR2 tier
mirrors ORT throughout ($`0.5`$ to $`1`$ pp higher, unchanged ordering).
The systems in front are therefore the phonologically informed
tokenizers at $`|V|{\leq}1024`$ together with the character baseline,
and syllable-2048 falls $`4.3`$ pp behind syllable-512, the same
direction as on RVG 1 (§4.5).

The shift on Verbmobil is driven by new lexical material rather than
phonetic variation: hesitations (“m”, “naja”), phonetic-shorthand verb
conjugations (“müsten” for “müssten”), and discourse particles. A
character tokenizer has no out-of-vocabulary problem, whereas a
syllable- or BPE-based vocabulary fails on these unseen forms via
cascading substitutions (aligned examples in Appendix B). One consistent
(though confounded with vocabulary type) interpretation is that
finer-grained inventories simply have more recovery paths when no whole
word matches: omni-vocab emits $`\approx`$ $`6.9`$ tokens per word in
our test data, multisyl-512 only $`\approx`$ $`4.5`$. In aggregate WER
this advantage is offset by the character inventory’s own failure mode
of transcribing reduced function words literally, so on Verbmobil the
two error types cancel rather than one dominating.

large $`|V|`$ BPE rediscovers syllable-like units on its own and the
hard syllable constraint becomes a liability. C3 replicates on a second,
architecturally different backbone, as do the directions of C1 and C2 on
RVG 1 (mHuBERT-147; Appendix A).

### 4.6 Analysis: error patterns are tokenizer-invariant

We compute confusion matrices for all $`18`$ phoneme tokenizers across
all three corpora. The pattern is striking: across every tokenizer the
top-$`5`$ in-domain substitution pairs are the same bidirectional
function-word swaps (“der”$`\leftrightarrow`$“die”,
“und”$`\leftrightarrow`$“der”, “und”$`\leftrightarrow`$“die”), with
maximum count differences between tokenizers $`\leq`$$`10\,\%`$ for the
$`17`$ well-converged runs (the $`40`$ %-PER outlier
phone-multisyl-v2-$`3840`$ shows the same ranking at inflated absolute
counts). This invariance holds across representative extremes of
vocabulary size and merge strategy (e.g., phone-char-raw,
phone-bpe-1024, phone-multisyl-lite-512). One important caveat remains:
the confusion counts are computed at the whitespace-separated SAMPA-word
level rather than at the atomic-IPA-segment level (see Limitations).
Atomic-phoneme confusion analysis is left for future work; we therefore
read the present observation as indirect evidence that the encoder
determines _which_ errors occur on these data, while the tokenizer
modulates _how many_. In §4.7 we show that this redistribution
materially affects deployment WER under domain shift.

### 4.7 Discussion

The opening question, whether _phonologically informed_ tokenizers built
from German syllabification and grapheme-to-phoneme mappings might offer
something that data-driven and multilingual defaults do not, admits a
nuanced answer in three positive components.

Phonologically informed tokenizers are fully competitive in-domain. All
top tokenizers cluster within their confidence intervals on CV+SWC, so
adopting syllable- or phoneme-derived units incurs no in-domain accuracy
cost while adding practical advantages: more interpretable confusion
analyses, smaller embedding tables in production (relative to the
$`10{,}288`$-token multilingual character inventory), and a
linguistically transparent design space.

Under domain shift, the choice of output unit becomes a useful tool
whose payoff depends on vocabulary size rather than on the type of
shift. When the shift is phonetic (RVG 1-ORT), the syllable-aware
multisyl-512 takes the WER lead while omni-vocab stays ahead on CER;
when it is lexical (Verbmobil), syllable-512 takes the lead with
omni-vocab about one point behind and every German-BPE variant further
back. The item-level mechanisms still move in the direction phonological
theory would predict: syllable-aligned units anchor the model in
intra-syllabic acoustic variation, and finer-grained inventories absorb
out-of-vocabulary lexical material. On Verbmobil, however, they offset
each other in aggregate WER. What separates the systems there is the
size of the vocabulary: against German-BPE, the syllable prior wins by
$`4`$ to $`8`$ pp at $`|V|{=}512`$ and loses on both corpora at
$`|V|{=}2048`$. Tokenization thus emerges as a deployment-time lever,
but one tuned against the vocabulary budget as much as against the
expected mode of variation.

The acoustic encoder and the output tokenizer act as complementary, not
competing, design axes. Across all 18 phoneme-track tokenizers, the
dominant error patterns are broadly similar bidirectional function-word
swaps, indicating that tokenization redistributes errors along a shared
encoder-fixed backbone, and this redistribution is large enough on
domain-shifted data to materially change deployment outcomes. We
therefore see linguistic structure not as an alternative to encoder-side
improvements such as domain-adaptive pretraining (Hsu et al., 2021) but
as an orthogonal lever that adds value on top of them.

## 5 Conclusion

Across 40 CTC fine-tunes of the Omnilingual ASR wav2vec 2.0 backbone for
German (31 tokenizers at $`300\,`$M, nine at $`1\,`$B), evaluated on
three orthogonal corpora, the phonologically informed tokenizers (Pyphen
syllables, multi-syllable merges, phoneme-derived units) are competitive
with BPE and character baselines in-domain, provide the best system on
both OOD sets at small vocabulary sizes while losing that lead at larger
ones, and complement rather than replace the acoustic encoder, since the
dominant error patterns are broadly similar across all 18 phoneme-track
variants. Linguistic structure thus remains a useful design axis for
German ASR, and combining linguistic priors with encoder-side adaptation
is a natural next step, as is LM rescoring, whose interaction with the
output unit we leave open.

## Limitations

Our largest models are $`1\,`$B parameters trained on $`961`$ h of
audio; external baselines trained on two to three orders of magnitude
more data sit $`5`$–$`20`$ pp ahead cross-domain (although in-domain WER
is statistically tied), so the trade-offs we report could be partially
compressed at production scale. We probe two structurally orthogonal OOD
axes, dialectal-phonetic (RVG 1) and spontaneous-lexical (Verbmobil),
and leave combined modes (e.g., dialectal negotiation speech), noise
robustness, and code-switching to future work. All $`1\,`$B results in
Tables 5, 6, and 7 use the continued-training checkpoints; the in-domain
delta between initial and continued training is small
($`\leq`$$`0.7`$ pp WER across the nine $`1\,`$B tokenizers), so the
reported cross-domain effects are not dominated by undertraining of any
single variant. The second-backbone replication (Appendix A) uses a
$`95\,`$M base model with much higher absolute error rates, and the
direction of the vocabulary-size interaction C2 depends on encoder
capacity. The phoneme-track confusion analysis operates on
whitespace-separated SAMPA word transcriptions rather than atomic IPA
segments, and the phoneme track itself emits phoneme sequences; a
deployed system would additionally require a phoneme-to-grapheme decoder
(Lee et al., 2023). Findings are reported for German; the
dialectal-vs-spontaneous trade-off is plausible for typologically
similar languages (dialectally rich, syntactically free), but
cross-linguistic generalization remains an open question we leave to
future work.

## Ethical Considerations

The training data (Common Voice, Spoken Wikipedia Corpus) is released
under permissive licenses and consists of recordings by consenting
volunteer speakers. The OOD corpora (RVG 1, Verbmobil) are licensed via
the Bavarian Archive for Speech Signals (BAS) for research use. The
dialectal split of RVG 1 covers a balanced regional sample of German
speakers; our evaluation does not infer or release any speaker identity.
ASR systems can amplify existing inequities in recognition quality
across speaker groups; our findings suggest that tokenizer choice can be
tuned to mitigate this for particular OOD axes (dialectal speech in our
case), and we recommend that practitioners audit their tokenizer choice
for the specific deployment population.

To test whether the interaction structure of §4.5 is specific to the
wav2vec 2.0 encoder, we repeat the experiment with mHuBERT-147
(Zanon Boito et al., 2024), a $`95\,`$M-parameter multilingual HuBERT
base model that differs from Omnilingual ASR in self-supervised
objective, architecture, and pretraining mixture. The encoder is the
only change: the CTC recipe, training data, tokenizer artifacts,
optimizer, learning-rate schedule, and audio budget of §4.1 are reused
unchanged. Because mHuBERT-147 is a raw self-supervised checkpoint
rather than an ASR-adapted one, the encoder is frozen for the first
$`10\,`$k steps while the CTC head is trained (the standard HuBERT
fine-tuning recipe; the setting is tokenizer-independent), and training
runs for $`30\,`$k steps so that the encoder receives the same $`20\,`$k
fine-tuning updates as in the main experiments. We train the eight
systems that enter the contrasts C1–C3 and evaluate them with the
protocol of §3.8.

Table 9 reports the three paired-bootstrap contrasts on both backbones;
Table 10 gives the per-system WER. Absolute error rates are two to three
times higher than at $`1\,`$B, as expected for a $`95\,`$M base model;
the test is whether the sign structure replicates. C3 does so on both
corpora, and C1 on RVG 1: multisyl-512 again beats omni-vocab on
dialectal speech, and the granularity-matched pair again differs
significantly on RVG 1 and on Verbmobil. C2 replicates on RVG 1 with a
similar magnitude ($`+8.7`$ vs. $`+10.0`$ pp) but is negative on
Verbmobil, where the same contrast is $`-0.9`$ pp on the $`300\,`$M
wav2vec 2.0 fine-tunes as well: its direction tracks encoder capacity
rather than the encoder family. Per-system rankings transfer poorly
between backbones (Spearman $`\rho`$ between the mHuBERT-147 and
$`300\,`$M wav2vec 2.0 rankings of the eight systems: $`0.00`$ on RVG 1,
$`+0.57`$ on Verbmobil, $`+0.38`$ in-domain), which is what the
interaction reading predicts: a ranking is not a property of the
tokenizer alone. An interaction that survives an encoder swap cannot be
a property of the wav2vec 2.0–tokenizer match.

Tables 11 and 12 illustrate the error types behind the cross-domain
results, using the $`1\,`$B systems omni-vocab, multisyl-512, and
German-BPE-512 (one per tokenizer class). Examples are selected
automatically rather than by hand: we align reference and hypothesis at
the word level, slide windows of 4–10 reference words over every
utterance, and discard windows in which any system’s hypothesis
collapses (hypothesis length outside 0.6–1.5 times the window length).
On RVG 1 we keep windows in which multisyl-512 is error-free while every
omni-vocab error is a near-miss substitution (character distance
$`{\leq}2`$), i.e., a phonetic rendering of a dialectal realization; on
Verbmobil (German utterances) we keep windows in which omni-vocab is
error-free while both subword systems err on a word absent from the
$`6.14`$ M-word training text. Windows are ranked by the number of such
errors, at most one window per utterance; one counterexample with the
opposite pattern is shown per corpus.

The two corpora show different error types. On RVG 1 the character
inventory transcribes reduced dialectal forms literally
(_habe_$`\to`$_hab_, _ist_$`\to`$_is_, _und_$`\to`$_un_) and drops
consonants inside function words, whereas syllable-constrained units
restore the canonical form. On Verbmobil the subword systems fail on
out-of-vocabulary compounds and names by producing plausible but wrong
forms (_arbeitsstreffen_, _anderthalbägiges_), while the character
inventory spells them correctly; the most frequent substitutions
(Table 12), colloquial contractions such as _wäre_$`\to`$_wär_ and
_es_$`\to`$_s_, are shared by all systems.
