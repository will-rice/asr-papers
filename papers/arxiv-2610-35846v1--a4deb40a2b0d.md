###### Abstract

Expanding multilingual ASR systems to new languages is challenging when
adaptation must preserve previously supported languages without
replaying source-language training data. We study this problem in
connector-based Speech LLMs, where a trainable connector maps speech
representations into a frozen large language model. We evaluate
target-update construction and merging strategies for adapting the
connector while preserving source-language performance. In single-target
expansion, target-only fine-tuning and standard TIES merging learn new
languages but cause severe source-language forgetting, while
SSU-Wanda+TIES gives the best source-preserving trade-off. In
multi-target expansion, update merging provides stable source retention,
whereas recursive expansion can improve target adaptation under
favorable orders but is highly order-sensitive. These results show that
multilingual ASR expansion requires balancing target acquisition, source
retention, and interactions among target-language updates.

## 1 Introduction

Recent speech foundation models have substantially improved multilingual
automatic speech recognition (ASR) through larger training corpora,
increased model capacity, and improved training objectives Babu et al.
(2022); Radford et al. (2023); Barrault et al. (2023). However,
extending an existing multilingual ASR model to new languages remains
challenging. Expansion often needs to be performed with limited data and
computation while preserving performance on previously supported
languages, creating a trade-off between target-language adaptation and
source-language retention.

A common expansion strategy is to fine-tune the existing model on the
new language, but this can cause catastrophic forgetting. Similar
degradation has been observed in connector-based Speech LLMs for ASR,
where multilingual projectors are easy to adapt but may substantially
harm original-language performance Concina et al. (2026a); Concina et
al. (2026b). Replay-based continual learning can mitigate forgetting by
interleaving previously supported languages during adaptation, but it
requires storing and reusing replay data. When such data is unavailable
or impractical to reuse, expansion must rely on replay-free
target-language adaptation without gradient-based source adaptation.

Model merging offers an alternative by combining learned parameter
updates without additional training Yang et al. (2026). Methods such as
weight averaging and task arithmetic integrate capabilities from
different training runs or tasks in weight space Wortsman et al. (2022);
Ilharco et al. (2022), but independently learned updates may interfere.
TIES-Merging mitigates this interference by removing small parameter
changes and resolving sign conflicts between task vectors Yadav et al.
(2023). Language-specific merging has also been explored to reduce
training and maintenance costs when adding or updating languages in
multilingual models Dmonte et al. (2026). However, how target-language
updates behave in connector-based multilingual ASR expansion remains
unclear, particularly without source-language replay during adaptation.

In this work, we study source-aware multilingual ASR expansion for
connector-based Speech LLMs Ma et al. (2025); Ma et al. (2024). Starting
from a multilingual base connector Zhang et al. (2026a), our goal is to
adapt the connector to new target languages while preserving performance
on the source languages, without replaying source-language training data
or performing gradient-based source adaptation during expansion. We
focus on two questions: RQ1: How can adaptation and merging balance
target acquisition and source retention? and RQ2: How effectively can
independent target updates be merged to support multiple new languages?

To answer these questions, we conduct controlled experiments with three
multilingual base connectors and six target languages. We compare direct
target fine-tuning, source-aware parameter-selective adaptation, and
TIES-based target-update merging under the same target-language
adaptation budget. Our experimental results suggest that source-aware
merging substantially improves the adaptation–retention trade-off in
single-target expansion, while multi-target expansion requires managing
interactions among target-language updates. Joint update merging
provides stable source retention, whereas recursive adaptation can
achieve stronger target performance under favorable target orders.

Our main contributions are as follows. First, we introduce a
source-aware multilingual ASR expansion setting for connector-based
Speech LLMs, where new target languages are added while preserving
existing source-language performance without source-language replay or
gradient-based source adaptation. Second, we systematically compare
target-update construction strategies, including direct fine-tuning,
SSU-based parameter-selective adaptation, SSU+FT, and TIES-based
target-update merging, to characterize the trade-off between target
adaptation and source retention. Third, we extend the study from
single-target to multi-target expansion and analyze how target-update
interactions and recursive target order shape multilingual expansion.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Multilingual ASR Expansion

Large-scale speech foundation models have advanced multilingual ASR
through cross-lingual self-supervised learning, weakly supervised
recognition, and unified multilingual speech modeling across diverse
languages and tasks Babu et al. (2022); Radford et al. (2023); Barrault
et al. (2023). Recent LLM-based ASR systems further connect pretrained
speech encoders with large language models through lightweight
connectors, showing that frozen speech and language backbones can
support multilingual recognition and benefit from auxiliary multimodal
context Fathullah et al. (2023); Ma et al. (2024); Yang et al. (2024).
These developments motivate connector-based Speech LLMs as a
parameter-efficient ASR framework, but leave open how to expand them to
new languages while preserving existing multilingual capabilities.

Multilingual ASR systems often need to be expanded after the initial
model has been trained, making new-language acquisition and
source-language retention a central challenge. Prior work on
multilingual ASR expansion has largely framed new-language acquisition
and forgetting as a continual-learning problem, covering replay-based
retention, parameter-regularized adaptation, and decoder-specific
optimization strategies Della Libera et al. (2024); Kwok et al. (2024);
Concina et al. (2026b). In contrast, our work does not rely on
source-language replay or gradient-based source adaptation during
expansion. We instead study how target-language updates can be
constructed and combined under lightweight source-language calibration,
comparing parameter-selective adaptation and target-update merging in
both single-target and multi-target connector expansion.

### 2.2 Model Merging for Adaptation

Model merging provides an alternative to continued fine-tuning by
combining parameters or parameter updates from separately trained
models. Model soups show that averaging the weights of multiple
fine-tuned models can improve performance and robustness without
increasing inference cost, particularly when the models are fine-tuned
from the same initialization Wortsman et al. (2022). Task arithmetic
represents fine-tuning effects as task vectors and shows that these
vectors can be added, subtracted, or composed to alter model behavior in
parameter space Ilharco et al. (2022). These approaches suggest that
independently learned capabilities can sometimes be integrated without
additional training.

However, merging fine-tuned models separately can introduce interference
between parameter updates. TIES-Merging addresses this issue by trimming
small parameter changes, resolving sign conflicts among task vectors,
and merging only sign-aligned updates Yadav et al. (2023). In
multilingual settings, Dmonte et al. (2026) study language-specific
model merging as a way to reduce training and maintenance costs when
individual languages are added or updated in multilingual task-specific
LLMs. In the speech domain, model merging is explored for adding new
tasks Zhao et al. (2026); Lin et al. (2025), domains Carvalho et al.
(2026), and fairness Lin et al. (2026); Bravo-Bravo et al. (2026).
Nevertheless, existing model merging work has not fully examined
connector-based multilingual ASR expansion under a source-free setting,
where target-language updates must be integrated while preserving
source-language performance without access to source-language data
during expansion.

### 2.3 Parameter-Selective Adaptation

Parameter-selective adaptation aims to reduce forgetting by restricting
which parameters are updated during task or language adaptation Liu et
al. (2021); Zhang et al. (2024); Pan et al. (2024). Instead of updating
the full model, these methods identify parameters or structural units
that are important for previously learned capabilities and protect them
during subsequent fine-tuning. This idea has been explored in continual
learning through masking, pruning, and parameter-isolation strategies,
where different subsets of model parameters are assigned to different
tasks or protected from future updates Mallya and Lazebnik (2018);
Mallya et al. (2018).

Source-Shielded Updates (SSU) apply this idea to target-language
adaptation of LLMs by estimating source-language parameter importance
and freezing source-important units during target-language fine-tuning
Yamaguchi et al. (2026). This is particularly relevant to multilingual
ASR expansion, where adapting to a new language can interfere with
previously supported source languages. In our work, we adopt SSU-style
source-aware masking for connector adaptation, using a lightweight
source-language calibration set only to construct masks.

## 3 Methodology

Figure 1 provides an overview of the connector-based ASR architecture
and the single-target and multi-target expansion strategies studied in

### 3.1 Connector-Based ASR Backbone

Figure 1: Overview of source-aware multilingual ASR expansion.

We study multilingual expansion in a connector-based ASR architecture Ma
et al. (2025); Ma et al. (2024); Zhang et al. (2026a), where only a
lightweight connector is adapted while both the speech encoder and the
language model remain frozen. Given an input speech signal $`S`$, a
frozen speech encoder $`\mathcal{E}`$ produces acoustic representations:

```math
\mathbf{H}=\mathcal{E}(S)\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times T\times E}, \tag{1}
```

where $`B`$ is the batch size, $`T`$ is the number of acoustic frames,
and $`E`$ is the encoder hidden dimension. To reduce the frame rate
before passing the representations to the LLM, we apply a
stack-downsampling operator $`\mathcal{D}_{K}(\cdot)`$ that concatenates
every $`K`$ consecutive frames:

```math
\mathbf{H}^{(K)}=\mathcal{D}_{K}(\mathbf{H})\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times(T/K)\times(EK)}. \tag{2}
```

A trainable connector $`\mathcal{C}_{\theta}`$ maps the downsampled
acoustic sequence into the LLM embedding space:

```math
\mathbf{Z}=\mathcal{C}_{\theta}(\mathbf{H}^{(K)}), \tag{3}
```

where $`\theta`$ denotes the connector parameters. The frozen
decoder-only LLM $`\mathcal{D}`$ then generates the transcription
autoregressively:

```math
p(\mathbf{y}\mid S)=\prod_{t=1}^{|\mathbf{y}|}p(y_{t}\mid y_{<t},\mathbf{Z}). \tag{4}
```

All adaptation and merging operations in this work are applied only to
the connector parameters, while the speech encoder and LLM decoder
remain frozen.

### 3.2 Target Adaptation with Source Calibration

We begin with a multilingual base connector trained on a set of source
languages. Let $`\theta_{\mathrm{base}}`$ denote the resulting base
connector. Given a new target language, our goal is to adapt the
connector to the target language while retaining performance on the
original source languages.

We first construct a target-adapted connector by fine-tuning the base
connector on target-language data:

```math
\theta_{\mathrm{tgt}}=\mathrm{FT}(\theta_{\mathrm{base}},\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tgt}}), \tag{5}
```

where $`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tgt}}`$ denotes the target-language
adaptation data. Direct fine-tuning uses $`\theta_{\mathrm{tgt}}`$
itself as the expanded model. In merging-based settings, we view target
adaptation as an update from the base connector:

```math
\tau_{\mathrm{tgt}}=\theta_{\mathrm{tgt}}-\theta_{\mathrm{base}}. \tag{6}
```

We also use SSU as a parameter-selective target adaptation strategy
Yamaguchi et al. (2026). SSU uses a small source-language calibration
set to estimate which columns of each weight matrix are important for
source-language behavior, and then freezes those columns during
target-language adaptation. In our work, the calibration set is drawn
from the source languages of the base connector.

Let $`\rho`$ denote the column-freezing ratio. For each connector weight
matrix
$`\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}`$,
SSU first assigns an element-wise importance score $`s_{ij}`$ to each
weight $`W_{ij}`$. Following SSU, we use the Wanda criterion to compute
these scores from source-language calibration data. Specifically,

```math
s_{ij}^{\mathrm{Wanda}}=|W_{ij}|\cdot\|\mathbf{x}_{j}\|_{2}, \tag{7}
```

where $`\mathbf{x}_{j}`$ is the activation of the $`j`$-th input feature
estimated from source-language calibration data.

We also evaluate two alternative masking criteria. In SSU-Mag, the
element-wise score is based only on weight magnitude:

```math
s_{ij}^{\mathrm{Mag}}=|W_{ij}|. \tag{8}
```

In SSU-Rand, columns are selected uniformly at random, without using an
importance score.

For score-based variants, SSU aggregates element-wise scores into column
scores:

```math
S_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}}s_{ij}. \tag{9}
```

The protected column set is selected by taking the top-$`\rho`$
proportion of columns:

```math
\mathcal{P}_{\rho}=\operatorname{Top}_{\rho}\left(\{S_{j}\}_{j=1}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}}\right). \tag{10}
```

For SSU-Rand, $`\mathcal{P}_{\rho}`$ is instead sampled uniformly from
the columns of $`\mathbf{W}`$ with the same cardinality.

The binary mask for $`\mathbf{W}`$ is defined as

```math
B_{ij}=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }j\in\mathcal{P}_{\rho},\\
1,&\text{if }j\notin\mathcal{P}_{\rho}.\end{cases} \tag{11}
```

Thus, protected columns are frozen during target-language adaptation:

```math
W_{ij}\leftarrow W_{ij}-\eta B_{ij}\nabla_{W_{ij}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tgt}}, \tag{12}
```

where $`\eta`$ is the learning rate and $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tgt}}`$
is the target-language training loss. For one-dimensional parameters
such as biases, each parameter is treated as its own column.

The resulting SSU-adapted connector is denoted as

```math
\theta_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{\mathrm{SSU}}=\mathrm{FT}_{\mathbf{B}}(\theta_{\mathrm{base}},\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tgt}}), \tag{13}
```

with update

```math
\tau_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{\mathrm{SSU}}=\theta_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{\mathrm{SSU}}-\theta_{\mathrm{base}}. \tag{14}
```

Finally, we include an SSU+FT variant. In this setting, SSU is first
used to obtain $`\theta_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{\mathrm{SSU}}`$, which is then
further adapted with standard unmasked target-language fine-tuning:

```math
\theta_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{\mathrm{SSU+FT}}=\mathrm{FT}(\theta_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{\mathrm{SSU}},\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tgt}}). \tag{15}
```

This variant tests whether source-calibrated masked adaptation provides
a better initialization for subsequent target-language optimization.

### 3.3 Target-Update Model Merging

For single-target expansion, the target update is

```math
\tau_{\mathrm{tgt}}=\theta_{\mathrm{tgt}}-\theta_{\mathrm{base}}, \tag{16}
```

where $`\theta_{\mathrm{tgt}}`$ may be obtained from standard
fine-tuning, SSU-based adaptation, or SSU+FT. We instantiate the merging
operator using TIES-Merging Yadav et al. (2023). Given a set of target
updates

```math
\mathcal{T}=\{\tau^{(1)},\ldots,\tau^{(n)}\}, \tag{17}
```

where each $`\tau^{(j)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}`$ is defined relative to
$`\theta_{\mathrm{base}}`$, TIES produces a merged update $`\tau_{m}`$
through trim, elect sign, and disjoint merge.

First, TIES trims each update by retaining only the largest $`k`$% of
entries by absolute value:

```math
\hat{\tau}^{(j)}_{i}=\begin{cases}\tau^{(j)}_{i},&\text{if }|\tau^{(j)}_{i}|\geq q_{k}(|\tau^{(j)}|),\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases} \tag{18}
```

Here, $`q_{k}(|\tau^{(j)}|)`$ is the threshold corresponding to the
top-$`k`$% absolute values in $`\tau^{(j)}`$. This removes small
parameter changes before aggregation.

Second, TIES elects a sign for each parameter dimension based on the
aggregate signed mass:

```math
\gamma_{i}=\operatorname{sgn}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\hat{\tau}^{(j)}_{i}\right). \tag{19}
```

When the signed mass is zero, we set $`\gamma_{i}=0`$ and the merged
update for that dimension to zero.

Finally, TIES performs a disjoint merge by averaging only updates whose
signs agree with the elected sign. Let

```math
\mathcal{A}_{i}=\left\{j\;\middle|\;\operatorname{sgn}(\hat{\tau}^{(j)}_{i})=\gamma_{i}\;\text{and}\;\hat{\tau}^{(j)}_{i}\neq 0\right\}. \tag{20}
```

The merged update is

```math
(\tau_{m})_{i}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{|\mathcal{A}_{i}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{A}_{i}}\hat{\tau}^{(j)}_{i},&\text{if }|\mathcal{A}_{i}|>0,\\
0,&\text{if }|\mathcal{A}_{i}|=0.\end{cases} \tag{21}
```

The final merged connector is obtained by scaling the merged update and
adding it to the base connector:

```math
\theta_{m}=\theta_{\mathrm{base}}+\lambda\tau_{m}, \tag{22}
```

where $`\lambda`$ is a scaling coefficient.

For conventional single-target merging,
$`\mathcal{T}=\{\tau_{\mathrm{tgt}}\}`$. In this case, there are no
cross-update sign conflicts, and TIES reduces to trimming the target
update before scaling:

```math
\theta_{m}=\theta_{\mathrm{base}}+\lambda\operatorname{TopK}(\tau_{\mathrm{tgt}};k). \tag{23}
```

For multi-target expansion, let
$`\tau_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{(1)},\ldots,\tau_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{(m)}`$ denote
target-language updates independently obtained from the same base
connector. We merge these target updatesly:

```math
\tau_{m}=\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{TIES}}\left(\{\tau_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{(1)},\ldots,\tau_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{(m)}\}\right), \tag{24}
```

and obtain

```math
\theta_{\mathrm{multi}}=\theta_{\mathrm{base}}+\lambda\tau_{m}. \tag{25}
```

In this setting, TIES can resolve sign conflicts among multiple
target-language updates before aggregation. This allows us to test
whether independently learned target-language updates can be combined
without recursive fine-tuning on multiple target languages.

Table 3 reports macro-averaged WER for each base group. The results show
a clear adaptation–retention trade-off. Target-only fine-tuning learns
the new language effectively, but severely damages the original source
languages. For example, on the Germanic base, Target FT reduces target
WER from 102.70% to 27.86%, but increases source WER from 22.57% to
69.73%. The same pattern appears for Romance and Slavic, where source
WER increases by +55.54% and +54.49%, respectively.

Standard TIES merging does not solve this problem in the single-target
setting. Although it preserves the target-language gains of fine-tuning,
it retains most of the source-language degradation, behaving similarly
to Target FT across base groups. SSU-Wanda+FT is also insufficient on
its own, even with source-important parameters frozen during target
adaptation, the resulting connector still suffers substantial source
degradation. These results suggest that neither conventional merging nor
protected fine-tuning alone is enough to recover a balanced
source–target solution.

In contrast, SSU-Wanda+TIES gives the strongest overall trade-off. It
keeps source WER close to the base connector while still improving
target performance substantially. For Germanic and Romance, source WER
increases by only +0.24% and +0.69%, while target WER drops from 102.70%
to 35.82% and from 111.16% to 37.36%, respectively. For Slavic, the
harder expansion setting, SSU-Wanda+TIES reduces source degradation from
+53.95% under TIES to +6.68%, while improving target WER from 110.15% to
51.69%. These results show that strong single-target expansion requires
combining source-aware adaptation with update merging.

#### 4.2.2 Effect of Source-Aware Protection

We next compare the three SSU masking criteria used with TIES: random
masking, magnitude-based masking, and Wanda-based masking. Table 3 shows
that all SSU+TIES variants reduce source forgetting compared with Target
FT and standard TIES, but they differ in how they balance target
adaptation and source retention. SSU-Rand+TIES often preserves stronger
target-language gains than SSU-Wanda+TIES, but leaves more source
degradation. For example, on the Slavic base, SSU-Rand+TIES reaches
41.69% target WER, but increases source WER by +22.54%. SSU-Wanda+TIES
gives a higher target WER of 51.69%, but reduces source degradation to
+6.68%. This suggests that random protection can limit update damage,
but does not reliably select the directions most important for
preserving source-language behavior.

SSU-Mag+TIES provides a more structured mask than random selection, but
it is generally worse than SSU-Wanda+TIES in source retention and
overall WER. Across all three base groups, SSU-Mag+TIES causes larger
source degradation than SSU-Wanda+TIES, indicating that weight magnitude
alone is not sufficient to identify source-important connector
directions. By incorporating source-language activations, SSU-Wanda
produces a more conservative mask that better preserves the original
multilingual capability.

Overall, SSU-Wanda+TIES gives the most reliable source-preserving
trade-off. It achieves the lowest source WER and lowest overall WER for
all three base groups, although it sometimes gives up part of the
target-language gain achieved by SSU-Rand or SSU-Mag. We therefore use
SSU-Wanda as the default SSU criterion in the multi-target experiments.

The results show that base–target compatibility affects expansion
behavior, but not through a simple ranking of conditions. Serbian is the

and script as the Slavic base, and is comparatively easy to add.
SSU-Wanda+TIES reaches 27.63% target WER with only +4.17% source
degradation. However, same-group targets are not uniformly easy. Polish
and Slovenian are both Slavic languages using Latin script in our
setting, but they are harder to add than Serbian, and Slovenian causes
much larger source degradation.

The comparison between Slovenian and Kyrgyz further shows that
language-group match alone does not determine expansion difficulty.
Slovenian is same-group but different-script, while Kyrgyz is
different-group but same-script. Yet SSU-Wanda+TIES preserves source
languages much better for Kyrgyz than for Slovenian, with +2.26% versus
+21.11% source degradation, even though Kyrgyz remains difficult in
target WER. This suggests that source retention and target learnability
are related but not identical. A target can be hard to recognize while
causing limited forgetting, or linguistically closer while producing a
more disruptive update.

These mixed patterns suggest that group and script compatibility
influence expansion, but their effect depends on how the target update
interacts with source-important connector directions.

#### 4.3.1 Joint Update Merging versus Recursive Adaptation

Joint update merging provides a stable source-preserving solution, while
recursive adaptation can learn target languages more aggressively but is
more order-sensitive. TIES keeps source WER close to the base connector
for all three base groups and substantially improves target WER,
reducing it from 102.70% to 64.93% for Germanic, from 111.16% to 67.22%
for Romance, and from 110.15% to 97.41% for Slavic. This shows that
independently trained target updates can be merged jointly without
severe source-language forgetting.

Source-aware variants of joint merging do not uniformly improve target
performance in the multi-target setting. Hard SSU+TIES and Incremental
dynamic SSU further protect source languages, but often retain less
target-language improvement than TIES. This suggests that source
protection can become too restrictive when multiple target updates are
combined.

Recursive methods show a different trade-off. Recursive FT then SSU
achieves the best target WER for Germanic and Slavic, and the best
overall WER for Slavic under the configured order. However, its Romance
result is much worse, indicating that recursive adaptation can produce
stronger target learning but depends heavily on target order. Recursive
rebased SSU is even less stable. Although it improves target WER in some
settings, it causes large source degradation for Romance and Slavic,
suggesting that recursively applying base-anchored target updates can
accumulate source-language interference. Thus, unlike single-target
expansion where SSU-Wanda+TIES is consistently strongest, multi-target
expansion exposes a sharper trade-off between stable joint merging and
order-sensitive recursive adaptation.

For Romance, the configured order NO $`\rightarrow`$ IT $`\rightarrow`$

These results show that recursive adaptation is not simply worse than
update merging. It is a higher-variance strategy that can achieve
stronger target adaptation under favorable orders, but can also
accumulate interference under poorly matched orders. This is consistent
with the target-update conflict analysis in Appendix B.4, where target
updates show low cosine similarity and substantial sign disagreement.
Overall, update merging is more stable and source-preserving, while
recursive adaptation can achieve better overall WER when the target
sequence is favorable.

## 5 Conclusion

In this paper, we studied source-aware expansion for connector-based
multilingual ASR, where new languages are added while preserving the
original base connector. Our results show that target fine-tuning and
standard TIES merging learn new languages but often overwrite
source-language performance, while SSU-Wanda+TIES gives the strongest
single-target adaptation–retention trade-off. For multi-target
expansion, update merging is stable and source-preserving, whereas
recursive adaptation can achieve stronger target performance under
favorable orders but is highly order-sensitive. Overall, effective
multilingual ASR expansion requires both source-aware preservation and
careful coordination among target-language updates.

## 6 Limitations

This work has two main limitations. First, our experiments focus on
connector-based Speech LLMs, where the speech encoder and LLM are kept
frozen and only the connector is adapted. This setting allows us to
study parameter-selective adaptation and model merging in a controlled
way, but the conclusions may not directly generalize to systems that
update the speech encoder, the LLM, or the full ASR model during
language expansion. Second, our multi-target experiments cover a limited
number of target languages and target orders. Although the results show
clear order sensitivity in recursive expansion, we do not propose an
automatic method for selecting the target order. A larger-scale study
over more languages and principled order-selection strategies would be
needed to fully characterize recursive multilingual expansion.

## 7 Ethical Considerations

Our experiments use publicly available speech data from Common Voice,
without collecting new recordings or additional speaker metadata. We do
not disclose any personally identifiable information about the speakers.
Multilingual ASR expansion may broaden access to speech technologies,
but improvements for newly added languages can come at the expense of
previously supported languages, potentially reducing service quality for
their speakers. ASR adaptation methods may affect languages and speaker
groups differently, and improvements in average WER can obscure these
disparities. To make differences across languages visible, we report
per-language performance on both source and target languages.

## Appendix A Additional Experimental Setup

### A.1 Data Allocation and Calibration

Each multilingual base contains five source languages, with 5 hours of
training speech per language, giving a total of 25 hours per base. Each
target-language adaptation run uses 5 hours of speech. In single-target
experiments, every run starts independently from the corresponding
multilingual base connector.

The Germanic and Romance bases are each expanded to Norwegian, Italian,
and Serbian. The Slavic base is expanded to Italian, Serbian, Polish,
Slovenian, and Kyrgyz.

For SSU-Wanda, the calibration set contains 5 minutes of speech from the
validation split of each source language, totaling 25 minutes per base.
Calibration is therefore balanced by speech duration across source
languages. It is used only for forward activation collection and mask
construction. Source-language examples are not replayed or used for
gradient-based adaptation during expansion. Random and magnitude-based
masking do not require activation collection.

### A.2 Expansion Protocols

We evaluate both single-target and multi-target expansion. In
single-target expansion, each target language is added independently to
a base connector, with base–target pairs covering language-group and
script match/mismatch conditions (Table 2). We compare direct target
fine-tuning, TIES without source-aware protection, and SSU variants
using random, magnitude-based, and Wanda-based masking.

In multi-target expansion, we study how multiple target-language updates
can form a single expanded connector. For joint update merging,
target-specific connectors are independently adapted from the same base
connector. Each target update is computed relative to that shared base,
and the updates are merged without further gradient-based training.
Recursive adaptation instead processes targets sequentially, using the
connector produced by the preceding stage as the starting point for the
next target. Each stage uses only the current target language for
gradient-based training.

The configured order is NO $`\rightarrow`$ IT $`\rightarrow`$ SR for the
Germanic and Romance bases, and IT $`\rightarrow`$ SR $`\rightarrow`$ PL
$`\rightarrow`$ SL $`\rightarrow`$ KY for the Slavic base. We
additionally evaluate Recursive FT then SSU under the orders listed in
Table 6. These constitute the evaluated subset of possible orders rather
than an exhaustive enumeration.
