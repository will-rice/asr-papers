###### Abstract

Search is a central component of Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR)
systems and has been deeply investigated for classical ASR modeling
based on Hidden Markov Models (HMMs). However, in work on current
sequence-to-sequence ASR architectures it is often simplified to a small
fixed-size beam search without external Language Model (LM). In this
work, we present a comparative analysis of search space complexity and
efficiency for Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) and neural
transducer models under different pruning strategies and LM
configurations. We show that these end-to-end (E2E) models work with a
small search space most of the time but exhibit large temporary peaks in
hypothesis count during periods of uncertainty. Score-based pruning
dynamically adapts the search to model uncertainty, leading to a
significantly lower Real-Time Factor (RTF). Our results highlight the
limitations of search with low fixed-size beam pruning and provide
practical guidelines for efficient search in E2E ASR.

###### Index Terms: 

ASR, search space, pruning strategies, CTC, neural transducer

## I Introduction & Related Work

Search (or decoding) is the central process that brings together the
components of an ASR system in order to turn acoustic evidence into a
transcription by navigating a space of intermediate hypotheses over
time. In the large vocabulary continuous speech recognition (LVCSR) era
of the 1990s-2000s, search spaces often contained thousands or tens of
thousands of hypotheses per time step, which drove the need for
efficient search strategies and methods for search space reduction \[1,
2, 3, 4\]. Modern neural ASR models such as CTC \[5\], neural
transducers \[6\] and Attention Encoder-Decoder (AED) \[7\] models have
changed this setting. They often exhibit sharp posterior distributions
where most of the time the probability mass is concentrated on only one
output class \[8\]. Additionally, these models are commonly defined on
an orthographic (e.g. character or Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) \[9\])
rather than a phonetic subword vocabulary, making search possible
without a pronunciation lexicon or external LM. Due to this development,
more sophisticated search methods have been largely disregarded in
recent literature, and many works just report fixed beam-sizes of 1-8
without external LM integration. Dynamic score-threshold pruning and
lexical prefix-tree based search organization are known techniques from
classical ASR, but their practical role for modern time-synchronous E2E
architectures has been underrepresented.

Most publicly available toolkits reflect this shift of focus away from
search and only offer limited search capabilities for modern model
architectures. Flashlight \[10\] offers lexicon-constrained and
lexicon-free search as well as word- and subword-level LM integration,
but separate pruning of within-word and word-end hypotheses is not
possible and it does not provide support for transducer models. ESPnet
\[11\] provides transducer search with neural LMs at the subword-level
but does not provide lexicon- or word-level capabilities and only
fixed-size beam pruning instead of dynamic score-based pruning.
k2/icefall \[12\] also offers many options for CTC search, including a
lexicon and n-gram LM but only implements neural LMs via rescoring and
again lacks dynamic pruning based on a score threshold for transducer
search. NeMo \[13\] and recent publications with it \[14, 15, 16\] aim
to optimize the transducer search process fully on GPU in order to take
advantage of parallelization and avoid inter-device communication.
However, this approach again prohibits word-level processing and dynamic
pruning.

In this paper, we challenge the assumption that small fixed-size beam
search is sufficient for optimal performance with modern model
architectures, in terms of both accuracy and speed. In our open-source
toolkit RASR \[17, 18, 19\] ¹¹ 1
[https://github.com/rwth-i6/rasr](https://github.com/rwth-i6/rasr) we
implemented a generic search module which supports various search
strategies for various E2E ASR models, allowing a comprehensive study of
their behavior and search space under comparable conditions. We restrict
the analysis in this work to time-synchronous CTC and monotonic
transducer models because AED, Speech-Large Language Model (LLM) and
related sequence-to-sequence models search label-synchronously which
means that hypotheses at one search step may correspond to different
input positions and finished hypotheses may have different lengths. This
makes active-hypothesis-count and pruning statistics not directly
comparable and introduces the length-normalization problem which is out
of scope for this discussion.

The main contributions of our work are as follows:

- •
  We systematically compare the performance of BPE and phoneme-based CTC
  and neural transducer models under various search conditions such as
  with and without lexicon-constraint as well as with and without
  subword- or word-level LMs.
- •
  We analyze search space behavior across different models and task
  difficulty levels by computing search errors and hypothesis counts.
- •
  We quantify the impact of dynamic vs. fixed-size beam pruning on the
  search accuracy (Word Error Rate (WER)) and efficiency (RTF) across
  datasets and search conditions.
- •
  We give compact algorithmic descriptions and tuned decoding parameters
  for reproducibility.

Our setups are published on GitHub²² 2
[https://github.com/rwth-i6/returnn-experiments/tree/master/2026-search-space](https://github.com/rwth-i6/returnn-experiments/tree/master/2026-search-space)
.

In modern ASR, the search maps a sequence of encoder states
$`h_{1}^{T}`$ which are computed from acoustic features
$`x_{1}^{T^{\prime}}`$ to the output sequence with maximum probability.
This can be conducted in a lexicon-free (open-vocabulary) or a
lexicon-constrained (closed-vocabulary) manner. In the lexicon-free
case, the maximization is carried out directly over subword sequences
$`a_{1}^{S}`$:

```math
h_{1}^{T}\rightarrow\argmax_{a_{1}^{S}}\bigg\{p_{\mathrm{SubLM}}(a_{1}^{S})^{\lambda}\cdot\max_{y_{1}^{T}:a_{1}^{S}}p_{\mathrm{ASR}}(y_{1}^{T}\mid h_{1}^{T})\bigg\}. \tag{1}
```

Here, $`y_{1}^{T}:a_{1}^{S}`$ denotes the set of alignments allowed by
the label topology that map or collapse to the subword sequence
$`a_{1}^{S}`$. For each subword sequence there is a score contribution
from a subword LM $`p_{\mathrm{SubLM}}`$ scaled by a factor $`\lambda`$
(which may be 0) and an ASR model $`p_{\mathrm{ASR}}`$ which is broken
down to frame-wise alignments.

Lexicon-constrained search works in a similar way but has an additional
outer maximization over word sequences $`w_{1}^{N}`$:

```math
\begin{split}h_{1}^{T}\rightarrow\argmax_{w_{1}^{N}}&\bigg\{p_{\mathrm{WordLM}}(w_{1}^{N})^{\lambda_{1}}\cdot\max_{a_{1}^{S}:w_{1}^{N}}\Big\{p_{\mathrm{SubLM}}(a_{1}^{S})^{\lambda_{2}}\\
&\cdot\max_{y_{1}^{T}:a_{1}^{S}}p_{\mathrm{ASR}}(y_{1}^{T}\mid h_{1}^{T})\Big\}\bigg\}.\end{split} \tag{2}
```

In the inner maximization, only those label sequences $`a_{1}^{S}`$ that
correspond to a valid word sequence according to the lexicon are
considered. This type of search also allows the integration of a
word-level LM $`p_{\mathrm{WordLM}}`$ in addition to
$`p_{\mathrm{SubLM}}`$. In practice, however, usually only a word-level
LM is used during lexicon-constrained search.

In this work, we focus on CTC and monotonic neural transducer \[20\] ASR
models, since both of them work on a frame-by-frame basis and allow for
a meaningful comparison. In the case of CTC, the alignment probability
in search can be stated as follows, including a frame-wise prior
probability $`p_{\mathrm{Prior}}(y_{t})`$ with scale $`\beta`$:

```math
p_{\mathrm{ASR}}(y_{1}^{T}\mid h_{1}^{T})=\prod_{t=1}^{T}\frac{p_{\mathrm{CTC}}(y_{t}\mid h_{t})}{p_{\mathrm{Prior}}(y_{t})^{\beta}}. \tag{3}
```

For the transducer model, we use a limited context of one history label,
which has previously shown to yield performance equivalent to full
context \[21\]. We also correct by the Internal Language Model (ILM)
probability $`p_{\mathrm{ILM}}(a_{s}\mid a_{s-1})`$ which we estimate by
the zero-encoder method \[22\]. For convenience of notation, we define
$`a(y_{1}^{t-1})`$ as the last non-blank label in $`y_{1}^{t-1}`$ and
set $`p_{\mathrm{ILM}}(y\mid a)=1`$ when $`y`$ is the blank label since
the ILM only contributes for non-blank labels:

```math
p_{\mathrm{ASR}}(y_{1}^{T}\mid h_{1}^{T})=\prod_{t=1}^{T}\frac{p_{\mathrm{Transducer}}(y_{t}\mid a(y_{1}^{t-1}),h_{t})}{p_{\mathrm{ILM}}(y_{t}\mid a(y_{1}^{t-1}))^{\beta}}. \tag{4}
```

Algorithms 1 and 2 implement the global objectives defined in equations
(1) and (2) approximately in negative-log space by accumulating local
ASR and LM scores. Here,
$`\mathrm{score}_{\mathrm{ASR}}=-\log p_{\mathrm{ASR}}`$ is instantiated
by Equation 3 for CTC and Equation 4 for the transducer. Recombination
keeps the best hypothesis among equivalent contexts. For lexicon-free
search, this recombination context consists of prediction network state
(for transducer) and the subword-LM history. For lexicon-constrained
search, the recombination context additionally contains tree state and
the word-LM history.

During the search process, we apply score-based and max-beam-size-based
pruning strategies. In score-based pruning with a threshold $`\Delta`$,
the current best hypothesis is selected from the beam and all hypotheses
whose score (i.e., negative log probability) is worse than the current
best plus the threshold are pruned. Let $`\mathcal{H}`$ be the current
set of hypotheses, then this is formalized as:

```math
\textsc{ScorePrune}(\mathcal{H},\Delta)=\Big\{q\in\mathcal{H}\mid{}\mathrm{score}(q)\leq\min_{q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}}\{\mathrm{score}(q^{\prime})\}+\Delta\Big\} \tag{5}
```

On the other hand, with max-beam-size pruning, we simply keep a fixed
maximum amount $`B`$ of hypotheses while pruning away the rest, i.e.:

```math
\textsc{BeamPrune}(\mathcal{H},B)=\mathrm{Top}_{B}(\mathcal{H}) \tag{6}
```

Pure fixed-beam search disables ScorePrune and only applies BeamPrune.
In our search implementation, we apply score- and max-beam-size-pruning
with the option to use different thresholds after each individual score
contribution, i.e., first after $`\mathrm{score}_{\mathrm{ASR}}`$, then
after $`\mathrm{score}_{\mathrm{SubLM}}`$ and finally after
$`\mathrm{score}_{\mathrm{WordLM}}`$. We also prune within-word
hypotheses separately from word-end hypotheses. We denote the ASR-stage,
subword-LM and word-LM maximum beam sizes by $`B_{\mathrm{ASR}}`$,
$`B_{\mathrm{SubLM}}`$ and $`B_{\mathrm{WordLM}}`$, and their score
thresholds by $`\Delta_{\mathrm{ASR}}`$, $`\Delta_{\mathrm{SubLM}}`$ and
$`\Delta_{\mathrm{WordLM}}`$, respectively.

All decoding experiments use RASR as the implementation framework. It
supports, among others, CTC and transducer scorers, subword- and
word-level LMs, lexicon-free and lexicon-constrained decoding, and the
staged pruning described above. Algorithms 1 and 2 summarize the
corresponding search steps implementations, but omit some optimizations
such as on-the-fly extension pruning. Notably, all results characterize
the model and search procedure, not RASR-specific behavior.

## III Experimental Setup

Experiments are conducted on three corpora: LibriSpeech, Loquacious, and
an in-house Spanish telephony corpus. LibriSpeech \[23\] uses the full
960h training set and is evaluated on dev-other and test-other. For
Loquacious, we use the public medium portion with 2500h of 16kHz
training data \[24\]. Loquacious evaluation is performed on dev.all and
test.all, which aggregate VoxPopuli, LibriSpeech, YODAS, and Common
Voice. The Spanish corpus contains around 12000h of 8kHz telephony
training data and is evaluated on an in-house 4.5h test set, providing a
harder-domain cross-lingual condition.

We train models on BPE and phoneme alphabets. The LibriSpeech BPE
vocabulary contains 185 labels including blank while the end-of-word
(EOW) augmented phoneme vocabulary contains 80 labels including blank.
The Loquacious setup uses BPE/EOW-phoneme vocabularies of 184/80 labels
including blank. On the Spanish task, we use a vocabulary of 63
EOW-phoneme labels.

For acoustic features, we extract 80-dimensional log-mel features from
the 16kHz LibriSpeech and Loquacious data, and 60-dimensional log-mel
features from the 8kHz Spanish data. The features are passed through a
convolutional front-end and subsampled to a step-size of 40 ms.
Afterwards, they are processed by a Conformer \[25\] encoder with 12
layers for LibriSpeech and Loquacious, and 16 layers for Spanish. The
transducer models additionally use a 2-layer feed-forward prediction
network for the limited history and a 1-layer feed-forward joiner
network.

Each LibriSpeech model is trained for 100 epochs, each Loquacious model
for 40 epochs, and the Spanish model for 60 epochs. All models use a
One-Cycle Learning Rate (OCLR) \[26\] schedule and apply SpecAugment
\[27\], speed perturbation \[28\], dropout, and weight decay for
regularization.

For word-level LM experiments, we apply a 4-gram LM; for LibriSpeech,
this is the official 4-gram LM, for Loquacious, this is the 4-gram LM
setup described in \[29\], and for Spanish, it is trained on in-house
data. On LibriSpeech, we additionally present results with a small
2-layer Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) \[30\] LM with 55M parameters at
the BPE level and larger 96-layer Transformer LMs at BPE and word level
with around 300M and 430M parameters respectively \[31\].

Lexicon constraints improve CTC without an external LM (from 7.0/7.1% to

the best setup reaching 3.8/4.2% WER. Overall, the word-level
Transformer LM gives the best WER, while the 4-gram and subword-level
LSTM LMs provide decent accuracy at much higher speed. The subword-level
Transformer LM is expensive in terms of execution time because it needs
to score every (non-blank) subword extension, while not improving WER
over the word-level Transformer LM which only needs to be forwarded at
word ends. The subword-level LSTM LM slightly improves over the 4-gram
LM, but is also slower; combining the LSTM and 4-gram LMs gives results
similar to the LSTM alone. A natural direction for future work is to
extend this comparison to larger ASR models trained on more data, where
integration of external LMs may have lower impact.

The phoneme CTC model is slightly worse than BPE CTC with a 4-gram LM
(5.5/5.7% vs. 5.2/5.6%) and on par with the Transformer LM. Search
without a lexicon and word-LM is not applicable to phoneme models since
phonetically equivalent words cannot be distinguished. The transducer
outperforms the CTC models, especially when no lexicon or LM is present
(6.0/5.9% vs. 7.0/7.1% WER) and remains better by between 0.3% to 0.7%
absolute WER in all other conditions. Compared to CTC, transducer search
is much slower without LM or with only a 4-gram LM, but the gap shrinks
with a neural LM because LM forwarding makes up a large part of the
search cost.

We first investigate the relation between pruning parameters, search
space size, and recognition performance. To optimize the pruning
parameters, we first set a very large maximum beam-size $`B`$ while
tuning the score thresholds and then decrease $`B`$ until performance
starts to degrade. Tables III and IV show LibriSpeech
lexicon-constrained BPE CTC and BPE transducer search with a word-level
Transformer LM. Search errors count segments that were transcribed
incorrectly although the ground-truth transcription has a better score
than the recognized one, i.e., the ground-truth was erroneously pruned
away during search. The tables also include percentile statistics of the
within-word hypothesis counts at each search step.

Fig. 1: Illustrative number of active within-word hypotheses at each
search step for a selected LibriSpeech dev-other utterance with BPE
CTC/transducer using lexicon-constrained search including a word-level
4-gram LM.

The p50 value shows that the median active hypothesis set is small while
the p90 and p99 statistics show that in some rare difficult regions the
signal causes locally increased hypothesis counts. This effect is
visualized in Figure 1 which shows the hypothesis count by timestep for
a selected utterance with a CTC and transducer model. The search space
stays small for most search steps and exhibits one large temporary peak
between steps 300 and 400.

Table V reports the corresponding analysis for Loquacious with BPE
transducer decoding and a word 4-gram LM. Overall, the hypothesis counts
are lower on Loquacious. This effect can be explained by the fact that
the experiments on Loquacious only use a 4-gram LM and a much lower LM
scale (see Table X). Since the 4-gram only relies on a three-word
history, the recombination step can recombine many more hypotheses and
with the low LM scale, score jumps from the LM score contributions are
much smaller and need a lower score threshold to cover for them. Still,
the same non-uniform behavior can be observed on this dataset: the
median active set remains small, while p99 reflects rare high-complexity
regions and is comparatively much higher.

To test more difficult acoustic conditions, we collected similar
statistics for a phoneme CTC model on the in-house Spanish 8kHz
telephony test set, as shown in Table VI. This task has a much higher
WER and a low hypothesis count in the tens is not nearly enough to reach
full performance in this case. Even at the median, several hundred
hypotheses are required and the p90 and p99 values go into the thousands
in order to reach optimal performance.

These results confirm that small fixed beams are insufficient when model
uncertainty is higher and when external LM contribution makes up a large
part of the score. Dynamic pruning adapts to the model uncertainty and
LM score contributions and keeps the hypothesis counts comparatively low
in the median.

## VI Search Efficiency

Search efficiency is tied to the size of the active search space. We
compare fixed max-beam pruning with dynamic score-threshold pruning
while preserving the WER. For each max-beam size $`B_{\mathrm{ASR}}`$,
the fixed baseline uses only max-beam pruning while the dynamic variant
additionally performs score-threshold pruning with the tightest possible
thresholds that do not degrade the WER. We report the relative search
RTF reduction when switching from fixed to dynamic pruning in percent:

```math
\mathrm{RTF\ reduction}\left[\%\right]=100\cdot\left(1-\frac{\mathrm{RTF}_{\mathrm{dynamic}}}{\mathrm{RTF}_{\mathrm{fixed}}}\right). \tag{7}
```

In Table IX we summarize the reduction in average hypothesis count and
search RTF for different ASR models and external LMs on all three
datasets. When no external LM is used, greedy search already gives
optimal results and dynamic pruning provides no advantage in this case.
For all experiments with external LM, however, dynamic pruning provides
significant efficiency improvements to reach the same WER. The average
hypothesis count and search RTF are often reduced by around an order of
magnitude. The relative RTF reduction is largest for CTC and 4-gram LM
setups because their search cost is dominated by hypothesis expansion
and CPU-side bookkeeping, while transducer prediction/joiner networks
and neural LMs use batched GPU computations which do not scale linearly
with the active set.

Table X lists tuned dynamic-pruning parameters. The LM scale weights
external-LM scores; the ILM/prior scale controls transducer ILM or CTC
prior correction. $`B_{\mathrm{ASR}}`$/$`B_{\mathrm{LM}}`$ are maximum

score thresholds pruning at the ASR and LM stages respectively. These
parameters define the pruning settings used in Table IX.

## VII Conclusions

We presented a systematic comparison of search strategies for
time-synchronous BPE- and phoneme-based CTC and monotonic neural
transducer models in E2E ASR. Comparisons of WER and RTF show that
dynamic score-based pruning and external LM combination remain relevant
for decoding with modern E2E ASR models. We have seen that typically,
these models operate with a low active hypothesis count for the majority
of search steps but spike to hundreds or thousands of hypotheses in rare
difficult regions. This behavior is verified on the public LibriSpeech
task as well as the more heterogeneous public Loquacious task and a more
difficult in-house telephony dataset.

The fixed-vs-dynamic pruning experiments show that dynamic pruning can
preserve WER while reducing search RTF by adapting the active hypothesis
set to local uncertainty, especially with external LMs where low
fixed-size beams are insufficient for optimal performance. We provide
tuned decoding parameters and open source implementations for
reproducibility.

This study is limited to time-synchronous CTC and transducer search;
label-synchronous and streaming decoding require some fundamentally
different analysis and remain future work.

Overall, our results suggest that external LM integration and dynamic
pruning are underutilized in modern E2E ASR applications.
