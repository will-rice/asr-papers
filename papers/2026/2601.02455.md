# Diagnostic-Driven Layer-Wise Compensation for Post-Training Quantization of Encoder-Decoder ASR Models

Xinyu Wang xinyu.wang5@mail.mcgill.ca    Ziyu Zhao ziyu.zhao2@mail.mcgill.ca    Yajie Luo yajie.luo@umontreal.ca    Yihong Wu yihong.wu@umontreal.ca    Liheng Ma liheng.ma@mail.mcgill.ca    Jingrui Tian jingrui.tian@mail.mcgill.ca    Lei Ding lei.ding@umanitoba.ca    Xiao-Wen Chang chang@cs.mcgill.ca    Peng Lu peng.lu@umontreal.ca \[ \[ \[ \[

###### Abstract

Deploying Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) models on memory-constrained edge devices requires aggressive low-bit weight quantization. Layer-wise post-training quantization is practical and effective, but it suffers from cross-layer error accumulation. Existing compensation methods typically use a single global strength for all layers, which is ill-suited to encoder-decoder ASR models whose acoustic encoder and linguistic decoder exhibit markedly different sensitivities to quantization noise. We propose FADE, a diagnostic-driven framework that assigns each layer an adaptive compensation coefficient by combining two complementary signals: an intrinsic vulnerability score from weight geometry and a calibration reliability score from the data-driven solution. The resulting layer-wise coefficient balances local quantization fidelity against cross-layer error correction, enabling tailored compensation without retraining or hyperparameter search. Experiments on Whisper, Moonshine, and Qwen3-ASR across four benchmarks show that FADE consistently improves mean Word Error Rate over strong baselines at both 3- and 4-bit precision while substantially reducing run-to-run variance.

## 1 Introduction

Encoder–decoder Transformer architectures now underpin many of the strongest automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems. Recent models such as OpenAI’s Whisper \[whisper\] and the Moonshine family \[moonshine\] achieve strong performance across languages and acoustic conditions, bringing robust speech recognition closer to practical use. Yet this progress has not translated cleanly to edge deployment. On mobile phones, wearables, and other resource-constrained devices, modern ASR models must operate under tight budgets on memory, compute, and energy. Their large parameter footprints strain storage and memory bandwidth \[pope2023efficiently\], and the cost of moving those parameters through the system can itself become a major bottleneck for real-time inference \[dettmers2022llmint8, kwon2023vllm\].

A natural response is to compress the model. Prior work explores this from several directions. Some methods remove redundant parameters through pruning without back-propagation \[efficient_pruning, frantar2023sparsegpt\], while others pursue ultra-low-bit compression via clustering or mixed-precision allocation \[ultra_low_bit\]. These approaches can be effective, but they often rely on structural changes or hardware support for non-uniform precision, which makes them less attractive for broad deployment on commodity edge devices.

Post-training quantization (PTQ), and in particular fixed-precision weight-only quantization (WOQ), offers a more practical alternative. WOQ compresses model weights into uniform low-bit integers (e.g., 3-, 4-, or 8-bit) without the cost of retraining \[gptq, zeroquant, dettmers2022llmint8, awq, omniquant, wang2025potptq\]. This uniform precision is appealing in practice because it preserves a simple computation pattern while substantially reducing storage and bandwidth requirements. Among PTQ methods, Hessian-based algorithms such as GPTQ \[gptq\] quantize each layer by minimizing a local reconstruction error estimated from a small calibration set:

``` math
\min_{\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{l}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n_{l}\times d_{l}}}\|\mathbf{W}_{l}\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{l}-\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{l}\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{l}\|_{F}^{2},
```

where $`\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{l}`$ denotes the quantized weight in the low-bit space $`\mathbb{Q}^{n_{l}\times d_{l}}`$, and $`\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{l}`$ is the activation produced by previously quantized layers.

This local objective, however, leaves an important problem unresolved. Quantization is applied layer by layer, but its errors accumulate globally: once $`\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{l}`$ is corrupted, deeper layers must optimize against an already distorted input. Recent work on Quantization Error Propagation (QEP) addresses this issue through cross-layer error compensation, shifting the reconstruction target toward the clean activation $`\mathbf{X}_{l}`$ and controlling the correction strength with a single global coefficient $`\alpha`$ \[arai2025quantization\]. That strategy works well for decoder-only large language models. For encoder–decoder ASR models, however, we find that a single global coefficient is not enough. It often leads to unstable behavior and large run-to-run variance under aggressive low-bit settings, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

![Refer to caption](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.02455v2/x1.png)

Figure 1: Word error rate versus Whisper model size for different quantization methods. Bubble size denotes the standard deviation across runs under 4-bit weight quantization. FADE achieves both lower mean WER and lower variance across runs.

The difficulty stems from the heterogeneity of ASR models themselves. Although encoder–decoder ASR systems share a Transformer backbone, not all layers respond to quantization in the same way. Layers differ in weight geometry, activation statistics, and tolerance to low-bit noise. The mismatch is especially pronounced across modules: the acoustic encoder processes continuous speech representations, whereas the decoder operates over text-like representations. These differences make calibration-based correction unevenly reliable across the network. A single global compensation strength is therefore too coarse: it can over-correct sensitive layers and overfit the calibration data, while under-correcting layers that require stronger error compensation. What is needed instead is a layer-wise mechanism that adapts correction strength to each layer’s quantization sensitivity.

Our goal is to make error-compensated PTQ better matched to the structure of encoder–decoder ASR models. To this end, we propose FADE, a diagnostic-driven framework that assigns each layer its own compensation coefficient. FADE combines two complementary signals: an *intrinsic weight vulnerability* score derived from weight geometry, and a *calibration reliability* score derived from the data-driven correction itself. Together, these signals estimate both how vulnerable a layer is to low-bit approximation and how trustworthy its calibration-based compensation is. Their fusion yields a layer-wise gate that balances local reconstruction fidelity against cross-layer error correction. The resulting method requires no retraining, no additional calibration data, and no hyperparameter search, and it integrates directly into existing Hessian-based PTQ pipelines.

We evaluate FADE on representative encoder–decoder ASR families, including Whisper, Moonshine, and Qwen3-ASR, across multiple benchmarks and low-bit settings. The results show that fine-grained, layer-wise compensation is important for stable quantization in heterogeneous ASR architectures: FADE consistently improves word error rate over strong baselines while substantially reducing run-to-run variance.

Our contributions are as follows:

- •
  We identify layer-wise sensitivity heterogeneity as a central obstacle for low-bit PTQ in encoder–decoder ASR models, and show that compensation schemes based on a single global coefficient are fundamentally mismatched to this setting.
- •
  We propose FADE, a diagnostic-driven framework that determines a separate compensation coefficient for each layer by combining an intrinsic weight-vulnerability signal with a calibration-reliability signal, without retraining, extra calibration data, or hyperparameter search.
- •
  We conduct extensive experiments on Whisper, Moonshine, and Qwen3-ASR across multiple benchmarks and bit-widths. FADE consistently improves mean word error rate over strong baselines and markedly reduces run-to-run variance.
- •
  We provide layer-wise analyses showing that the learned coefficients are non-trivial, robust across random seeds, and vary smoothly across encoder and decoder blocks in a manner consistent with their differing sensitivities.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Layer-Wise Post-Training Quantization

Layer-wise post-training quantization (PTQ) methods perform quantization layer by layer \[gptq, awq\]. Concretely, given the weight matrix $`\mathbf{W}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}\times d_{\text{in}}}`$ at the $`l`$-th layer and the input activation $`\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}}\times N}`$ collected from $`N`$ calibration datapoints, the goal is to find a low-precision weight matrix $`\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{l}`$ that approximates the original layer output. Formally, this is cast as the following optimization problem:

``` math
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{l}=\underset{\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{l}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n_{l}\times d_{l}}}{\arg\min}\|\mathbf{W}_{l}\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{l}-\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{l}\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{l}\|_{F}^{2},
```

where $`\mathbb{Q}`$ defines the quantization constraints (e.g., bit-width, grouping). Following the convention of \[gptq\], $`\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{l}`$ is obtained by running the calibration data through all preceding *quantized* layers in full precision (FP16/BF16).

Many existing PTQ approaches adopt asymmetric uniform group-wise quantization. Let $`\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{g}`$ denote a weight group, typically a contiguous subvector of a column of $`\mathbf{W}`$. Given a quantization scale $`s`$ and target bit-width $`b`$, the quantized representation is:

``` math
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}=s\cdot\text{clamp}\!\left(\left\lfloor\frac{\mathbf{w}}{s}\right\rceil,\;0,\;2^{b-1}-1\right),
```
``` math
\mathrm{clamp}(x,u,v)=\min(\max(x,u),v).
```

where $`\lfloor\cdot\rceil`$ denotes round-to-nearest and $`\text{clamp}(\cdot)`$ restricts values to the signed $`b`$-bit integer range.

### 2.2 Hessian-Based Methods

The standard least-squares reconstruction loss in Eq. (2) assigns equal importance to all weight entries. A more principled approach leverages *second-order* (Hessian) information to identify structurally sensitive weights, so that quantization error is concentrated in directions that matter least to the output. This idea traces back to Optimal Brain Damage \[obd\] and has been adopted by recent methods including Optimal Brain Quantization (OBQ) \[obc\] and GPTQ \[gptq\].

#### Second-order approximation.

Let $`\Delta\mathbf{W}_{l}=\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{l}-\mathbf{W}_{l}`$ denote the weight perturbation induced by quantization, and define the reconstruction loss $`\mathcal{L}(\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{l}):=\|\mathbf{W}_{l}\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{l}-\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{l}\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{l}\|_{F}^{2}`$. Expanding $`\mathcal{L}`$ via Taylor series around $`\mathbf{W}_{l}`$:

``` math
\mathcal{L}(\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{l})=\text{Tr}\!\left(\Delta\mathbf{W}_{l}\,\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{l}\,\Delta\mathbf{W}_{l}^{\top}\right)+o\!\left((\Delta\mathbf{W}_{l})^{3}\right),
```

where $`\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{l}=\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{l}\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{l}^{\top}`$ is the (input) Hessian matrix. The zeroth- and first-order terms vanish because the gradient is zero at the full-precision weights. The Hessian $`\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{l}`$ thus fully characterizes to second order how sensitive each weight is to perturbation: a weight corresponding to a large diagonal entry of $`\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{l}`$ should be quantized carefully, whereas one with a small diagonal entry can tolerate larger rounding error.

#### Row-wise decomposition.

Because rows of $`\mathbf{W}_{l}`$ act independently in the linear computation, the loss decomposes into $`d_{\text{out}}`$ independent per-row sub-problems:

``` math
\min_{\widehat{\mathbf{w}}}(\mathbf{w}-\widehat{\mathbf{w}})^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{l}\,(\mathbf{w}-\widehat{\mathbf{w}}),
```

where $`\mathbf{w}`$ is a single row of $`\mathbf{W}_{l}`$. This decomposition enables efficient parallel processing of rows without loss of optimality.

#### OBQ: greedy weight-wise quantization.

Optimal Brain Quantization \[obc\] solves each row sub-problem by a greedy column-by-column procedure. At each step it selects the weight $`w_{q}`$ minimising the ratio of squared quantization error to the corresponding inverse-Hessian diagonal:

``` math
w_{q}=\arg\min_{j}\,\frac{(w_{j}-\hat{w}_{j})^{2}}{[\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{F}^{-1}]_{jj}},
```

where $`\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{F}`$ is the Hessian restricted to the subset of still-unquantized columns $`F`$. After quantizing $`w_{q}`$, the remaining unquantized weights are updated to compensate:

``` math
\bm{\delta}_{F}=\frac{w_{q}-\hat{w}_{q}}{[\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{F}^{-1}]_{qq}}\cdot(\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{F}^{-1})_{\cdot,\,q}.
```

This compensation step is the key insight: rounding error at one weight is *partially cancelled* by adjusting its neighbours, guided by second-order correlations.

#### GPTQ: efficient column-wise quantization.

While OBQ’s greedy selection is optimal, it does not scale to billion-parameter models. GPTQ \[gptq\] makes two approximations that dramatically reduce cost. First, it quantizes columns in a fixed left-to-right order rather than searching for the globally optimal sequence. Second, it avoids explicitly inverting $`\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{l}`$ at each step by pre-computing the Cholesky factorisation $`\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{l}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\top}`$ (with a small damping term $`\lambda\mathbf{I}`$ added for numerical stability), from which inverse-Hessian products can be read off cheaply.

The resulting procedure processes columns $`j=1,\ldots,d_{\text{in}}`$ sequentially:

1.  1.
    Quantize: round $`\mathbf{W}_{:,j}`$ to the nearest grid point $`\hat{\mathbf{W}}_{:,j}=Q(\mathbf{W}_{:,j})`$.
2.  2.
    Compute error: $`\bm{\delta}_{j}=\mathbf{W}_{:,j}-\hat{\mathbf{W}}_{:,j}`$.
3.  3.
    Compensate: update all subsequent columns $`k>j`$:

    ``` math
    \mathbf{W}_{:,k}\leftarrow\mathbf{W}_{:,k}-\frac{[\widehat{\mathbf{H}}^{-1}]_{kj}}{[\widehat{\mathbf{H}}^{-1}]_{jj}}\,\bm{\delta}_{j}.
    ```

This is precisely the OBQ update of Eq. (8) applied to a fixed order, and it still substantially outperforms simple round-to-nearest (RTN) because each column benefits from the accumulated compensation from all previously quantized columns. The full algorithm is summarised in Algorithm 1.

0: Weight $`\mathbf{W}`$, calibration inputs $`\mathbf{X}`$, quantizer $`Q(\cdot)`$, damping $`\lambda`$

0: Quantized weight $`\hat{\mathbf{W}}`$

1: $`\mathbf{H}\leftarrow\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}+\lambda\mathbf{I}`$ {Hessian with damping}

2: Compute Cholesky factorization $`\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\top}`$; derive $`\mathbf{H}^{-1}`$ via back-substitution

3: for $`j=1`$ to $`d_{\text{in}}`$ do

4:  $`\hat{\mathbf{W}}_{:,j}\leftarrow Q(\mathbf{W}_{:,j})`$ {Quantize column $`j`$}

5:  $`\bm{\delta}\leftarrow\mathbf{W}_{:,j}-\hat{\mathbf{W}}_{:,j}`$ {Quantization error}

6:  $`\mathbf{W}_{:,j+1:}\leftarrow\mathbf{W}_{:,j+1:}-\dfrac{\bm{\delta}}{[\mathbf{H}^{-1}]_{jj}}\cdot(\mathbf{H}^{-1})_{j,\,j+1:}`$ {Compensate future columns}

7: end for

8: return $`\hat{\mathbf{W}}`$

### 2.3 Quantization Error Accumulation and Error Correction

Consider a model $`f`$ composed of $`m`$ layers,

``` math
f:=f_{m}\circ f_{m-1}\circ\cdots\circ f_{1}(\mathbf{X}),
```

and its quantized counterpart $`\widehat{f}`$. The total quantization error is

``` math
\delta=\|f(\mathbf{X})-\widehat{f}(\mathbf{X})\|_{F}^{2}.
```

Because each layer is quantized *independently*, the per-layer error $`\|f_{i}(\mathbf{X})-\widehat{f}_{i}(\mathbf{X})\|_{F}^{2}`$ propagates forward without correction, leading to exponential error accumulation across depth \[arai2025quantization\]. This phenomenon occurs in both decoder-only Transformers (LLMs) and encoder-decoder Transformers (e.g., ASR models).

Quantization Error Propagation (QEP) \[arai2025quantization\] mitigates this by modifying the per-layer optimization target to account for the drift $`\bm{\delta}_{l}:=\mathbf{X}_{l}-\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{l}`$ accumulated in the activations up to layer $`l`$:

$`\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{l}^{*}(\alpha_{l})`$$`\displaystyle=\mathbf{W}_{l}+\alpha_{l}\mathbf{W}_{l}\bm{\delta}_{l}\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{l}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{l}^{-1},`$$`\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{l}`$$`\displaystyle=\underset{\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{l}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n_{l}\times d_{l}}}{\arg\min}\|\mathbf{W}_{l}^{*}(\alpha_{l})\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{l}-\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{l}\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{l}\|_{F}^{2}.`$

Setting $`\alpha_{l}=0`$ recovers the original objective (2), while $`\alpha_{l}=1`$ yields the fully-corrected target that directly compensates for activation drift.

Despite the simplicity of this formulation, selecting $`\{\alpha_{l}\}`$ optimally is non-trivial. The ideal value depends on the weight distribution of the layer, its depth in the network, the data modality it processes, and its functional role (encoder vs. decoder). In the original QEP paper, $`\alpha_{l}=1/2`$ is used as a uniform default, with manual adjustments in special cases. A principled, data-driven method for determining $`\alpha_{l}`$ per layer remains an open problem.

### 2.4 Quantization for Automatic Speech Recognition

Post-training quantization (PTQ) has been widely studied for automatic speech recognition (ASR), but existing methods exhibit several limitations in terms of architectural assumptions and evaluation settings.

Early work such as Q-ASR \[kim2022integeronlyzeroshotquantizationefficient\] adopts a data-free quantization strategy by leveraging BatchNorm statistics to construct synthetic calibration data. While effective for CNN-based ASR models, this approach inherently relies on the presence of BatchNorm layers. However, modern ASR systems such as Whisper and Qwen-ASR are based on Transformer architectures that employ LayerNorm instead of BatchNorm, making such methods inapplicable in contemporary settings.

More recent approaches attempt to improve PTQ for speech models through architecture-aware designs. For instance, StableQuant \[hong2025stablequantlayeradaptiveposttraining\] introduces layer-wise stabilization techniques tailored to the heterogeneous structure of speech foundation models. While these methods improve robustness, they rely on heuristics specific to speech architectures, which may limit their generality across different model families.

GenPTQ \[kang-kim-2025-genptq\] further proposes a mixed-precision quantization scheme based on gradient-based sensitivity analysis to allocate bit-width across layers. Although it is not tied to a specific architecture, it operates in a layer-wise manner and treats each layer independently. As a result, it does not explicitly model the accumulation of quantization error across layers, which is particularly critical in deep encoder–decoder ASR models.

In addition to architectural assumptions, a common limitation of prior work is the lack of evaluation on modern state-of-the-art ASR systems. Many existing studies focus on outdated architectures or relatively small-scale models, which are not representative of current deployment scenarios. Recent advances in ASR are driven by large-scale pretrained models such as Whisper and Qwen-ASR, which exhibit different activation characteristics and error propagation behavior. Therefore, it remains unclear whether conclusions drawn from earlier models generalize to these settings.

In contrast, our method is designed to be model-agnostic and focuses on explicitly modeling cross-layer error propagation. By addressing the accumulation of quantization error throughout the network, our approach provides a more principled and scalable solution for quantizing modern ASR systems.

## 3 Methodology

![Refer to caption](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.02455v2/x2.png)

As discussed in Section 2.3, quantization errors in encoder–decoder ASR models accumulate across successive layers and blocks. Existing cross-layer compensation methods mitigate this effect with a single global coefficient $`\alpha`$, but Fig. 5 shows that no fixed global $`\alpha`$ performs uniformly well across models and quantization settings. This issue is particularly pronounced in encoder–decoder ASR models, where layers process different representations and exhibit distinct sensitivities to quantization noise.

To address this issue, we propose FADE (Fine-grained Alpha for Dynamic Quantization Error Propagation), a diagnostic-driven layer-wise compensation framework that assigns each layer an adaptive correction coefficient $`\alpha_{l}`$. Specifically, FADE combines two complementary signals: an intrinsic weight-vulnerability signal that measures sensitivity to low-bit approximation, and a calibration-reliability signal that measures the trustworthiness of the estimated correction direction. Their fusion yields a layer-adaptive coefficient that controls the strength of cross-layer error compensation.

### 3.1 From Global to Layer-Wise Compensation

Consider an $`L`$-layer encoder–decoder ASR model with weight matrices $`\{\mathbf{W}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}`$, where $`\mathbf{W}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{l}\times d_{l}}`$. Let $`\mathbf{X}_{l}`$ denote the clean input activation to layer $`l`$, namely the activation produced when all preceding layers operate in full precision, and let $`\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{l}`$ denote the perturbed activation produced when preceding layers have already been quantized. The upstream activation drift at layer $`l`$ is

``` math
\bm{\delta}_{l}\triangleq\mathbf{X}_{l}-\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{l}.
```

A unified family of cross-layer compensation objectives can then be written as

``` math
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{l}(\alpha_{l})=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{l}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n_{l}\times d_{l}}}\left\|\left(\mathbf{W}_{l}+\alpha_{l}\mathbf{W}_{l}\bm{\delta}_{l}\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{l}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{l}^{-1}\right)\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{l}-\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{l}\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{l}\right\|_{F}^{2},
```

where $`\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{l}=\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{l}\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{l}^{\top}`$ is the input Hessian and $`\alpha_{l}\in[0,1]`$ is the compensation coefficient for layer $`l`$.

This formulation recovers several existing settings as special cases. Setting $`\alpha_{l}=0`$ for all $`l`$ reduces Eq. (14) to the standard layer-wise PTQ objective in Eq. (1). Setting $`\alpha_{l}=1`$ gives the fully corrected target. Setting $`\alpha_{l}\equiv\alpha`$ to a tuned constant recovers the global interpolation strategy used in prior cross-layer compensation methods. Our goal is to replace this single global coefficient with a layer-dependent coefficient that adapts to the needs of each layer.

### 3.2 FADE: Diagnostic-Driven Layer-Wise Compensation

The core task of FADE is to determine $`\alpha_{l}`$ for each layer. Rather than treating all layers uniformly, FADE asks two questions for every layer $`l`$:

- •
  Is this layer intrinsically hard to quantize?
- •
  Is the calibration-based correction direction trustworthy for this layer?

The first question captures whether low-bit quantization would introduce substantial local distortion even before considering calibration data. The second captures whether the data-driven correction obtained from a Hessian-based solver is likely to be useful rather than noisy or overfit. Figure 2 illustrates this process: FADE first extracts these two signals, then fuses them into a layer-wise gate that determines the strength of cross-layer correction.

### 3.3 Signal I: Intrinsic Weight Vulnerability

We first quantify the quantization difficulty inherent to the weight distribution itself, independently of any calibration data. Let $`\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{l}^{\mathrm{rtn}}`$ denote the Round-to-Nearest (RTN) \[zeroquant\] solution for layer $`l`$, which minimizes quantization error strictly in the weight space. We define the normalized RTN reconstruction error as

``` math
e_{r}(l)=\frac{\|\mathbf{W}_{l}-\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{l}^{\mathrm{rtn}}\|_{F}}{\|\mathbf{W}_{l}\|_{F}+\varepsilon}.
```

This quantity serves as a proxy for weight outliers and heavy-tailed distributions, both of which are common in modern ASR models and tend to make low-bit quantization more difficult. A large $`e_{r}(l)`$ indicates that the layer is structurally vulnerable to low-bit approximation. We therefore define the *Intrinsic Weight Vulnerability* signal as

``` math
\phi_{\mathrm{int}}(l)=\log\!\big(1+e_{r}(l)\big).
```

This signal is calibration-independent. It captures the structural difficulty of quantizing the layer itself, and it should increase the incentive to apply stronger cross-layer compensation.

### 3.4 Signal II: Calibration Reliability

The second signal assesses whether the calibration-based correction direction for layer $`l`$ is trustworthy. Let $`\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{l}^{\mathrm{calib}}`$ denote the quantized weights returned by a Hessian-based PTQ solver; in this work, we instantiate this solver with GPTQ \[gptq\]. We define

$`\displaystyle e_{\mathrm{calib}}(l)`$$`\displaystyle=\frac{\|\mathbf{W}_{l}-\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{l}^{\mathrm{calib}}\|_{F}}{\|\mathbf{W}_{l}\|_{F}+\varepsilon},`$$`\displaystyle\Delta(l)`$$`\displaystyle=\frac{e_{r}(l)-e_{\mathrm{calib}}(l)}{e_{r}(l)+\varepsilon},`$$`\displaystyle e_{\mathrm{stab}}(l)`$$`\displaystyle=\frac{\|\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{l}^{\mathrm{rtn}}-\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{l}^{\mathrm{calib}}\|_{F}}{\|\mathbf{W}_{l}\|_{F}+\varepsilon}.`$

Here, $`\Delta(l)`$ measures the *calibration gain*: it is positive when the calibration-based solution improves upon the data-free RTN baseline, indicating that the calibration set provides useful corrective information for this layer. In contrast, $`e_{\mathrm{stab}}(l)`$ measures the *solution displacement* between the data-free and data-driven solutions. A large displacement indicates that the calibration solver has moved far away from a structurally safe baseline. When this displacement is not accompanied by a meaningful gain, it is a sign that the calibration-based correction may be unstable or overfit.

We combine these quantities into the *Calibration Reliability* signal

``` math
\phi_{\mathrm{calib}}(l)=\max\!\big(\Delta(l),0\big)-\log\!\big(1+e_{\mathrm{stab}}(l)\big).
```

This construction rewards layers for which calibration produces a clear benefit, while penalizing layers whose calibration solution deviates substantially from RTN without sufficient gain. In this sense, $`\phi_{\mathrm{calib}}`$ captures whether the correction direction is reliable enough to be trusted.

#### Why the two signals are both needed.

These two signals play different roles. $`\phi_{\mathrm{int}}`$ tells us whether the layer is hard to quantize in principle, while $`\phi_{\mathrm{calib}}`$ tells us whether the calibration-based correction is reliable in practice. Either one alone is insufficient. A layer may be intrinsically vulnerable but have an unreliable correction direction, in which case aggressive compensation would amplify calibration noise. Conversely, a layer may have a stable calibration solution but be easy to quantize, in which case strong compensation is unnecessary. FADE therefore uses both, and we empirically verify in Section 4.4 that removing either signal degrades both mean WER and run-to-run stability.

### 3.5 Signal Fusion and Adaptive Gating

Given the two diagnostic signals, FADE computes a fused score

$`\displaystyle s_{l}`$$`\displaystyle=\phi_{\mathrm{int}}(l)+\phi_{\mathrm{calib}}(l)`$$`\displaystyle=\log\!\big(1+e_{r}(l)\big)+\max\!\big(\Delta(l),0\big)-\log\!\big(1+e_{\mathrm{stab}}(l)\big),`$

and maps it to a valid compensation interval through a bounded fusion operator $`\mathcal{F}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to[\alpha_{\min},\alpha_{\max}]`$ defined as

``` math
\alpha_{l}\;=\;\mathcal{F}\!\big(\phi_{\mathrm{int}}(l),\,\phi_{\mathrm{calib}}(l)\big)\;\triangleq\;\alpha_{\min}+(\alpha_{\max}-\alpha_{\min})\,\sigma(s_{l}),
```

where $`\sigma(\cdot)`$ is the logistic function and $`[\alpha_{\min},\alpha_{\max}]\subseteq[0,1]`$ is a fixed untuned interval. In all experiments, we use $`\alpha_{\min}=0.1`$ and $`\alpha_{\max}=0.8`$.

The gate in Eq. (22) plays the central role in FADE. It controls the interpolation between local quantization fidelity and cross-layer correction. When a layer is both highly vulnerable and supported by a reliable calibration signal, $`s_{l}`$ becomes large and positive, and $`\alpha_{l}`$ approaches $`\alpha_{\max}`$, yielding strong correction. When a layer is difficult to quantize but the calibration signal is unreliable, the penalty term suppresses $`s_{l}`$, driving $`\alpha_{l}`$ toward $`\alpha_{\min}`$ to avoid amplifying calibration noise. For benign layers, all diagnostic terms remain small and $`\alpha_{l}`$ stays near the middle of the interval, resulting in a balanced correction strength. The gate $`\alpha_{l}`$ thus jointly encodes the layer’s quantization difficulty and the trustworthiness of its calibration-based correction, providing a principled per-layer trade-off between local fidelity and cross-layer compensation.

Algorithm 2 FADE: Diagnostic-Driven Layer-Wise Compensation

FADE is not a standalone quantizer. Rather, it acts as a lightweight per-layer coefficient generator that can be inserted into any Hessian-based PTQ pipeline that supports a cross-layer compensation term. At each layer $`l`$, it computes a data-free RTN baseline $`\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{l}^{\mathrm{rtn}}`$ and a data-driven calibrated solution $`\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{l}^{\mathrm{calib}}`$, extracts the scalar diagnostics $`e_{r}(l)`$, $`\Delta(l)`$, $`e_{\mathrm{stab}}(l)\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}`$, and fuses them through the operator $`\mathcal{F}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to[\alpha_{\min},\alpha_{\max}]`$ defined in Eq. (22) into a layer-wise coefficient $`\alpha_{l}`$. The compensated reconstruction objective in Eq. (14) is then solved with this coefficient. The full procedure is given in Algorithm 2.

#### Offline complexity.

Consider a layer with weight matrix $`\mathbf{W}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{l}\times d_{l}}`$ and calibration activations $`\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}\times N}`$. A standard GPTQ-style solver decomposes into three dominant operations—Hessian construction $`\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{l}=\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{l}\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{l}^{\top}`$, Cholesky-based factorization of $`\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{l}`$, and column-wise reconstruction with error compensation—whose aggregate cost is

``` math
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{GPTQ}}(l)\;=\;\underbrace{\mathcal{O}(d_{l}^{2}N)}_{\text{Hessian}}\;+\;\underbrace{\mathcal{O}(d_{l}^{3})}_{\text{factorization}}\;+\;\underbrace{\mathcal{O}(n_{l}d_{l}^{2})}_{\text{reconstruction}}.
```

Relative to this baseline, FADE introduces three additional operations at layer $`l`$: an RTN pass, the evaluation of the diagnostic statistics, and one supplementary compensated reconstruction pass under the perturbed objective. Both the RTN pass and the diagnostic evaluation are element-wise operations on the weight matrix and reduce to a constant number of Frobenius-norm computations, yielding

``` math
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{RTN}}(l)+\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{diag}}(l)\;=\;\mathcal{O}(n_{l}d_{l}),
```

which is asymptotically dominated by the $`\mathcal{O}(n_{l}d_{l}^{2})`$ cost of a single reconstruction pass. When the Hessian and its Cholesky factor are cached and reused across the two reconstruction passes—a standard implementation choice—the additional cost incurred by FADE relative to the GPTQ baseline is therefore

``` math
\Delta\mathcal{C}_{\textbf{FADE}{}}(l)\;=\;\underbrace{\mathcal{O}(n_{l}d_{l}^{2})}_{\text{extra reconstruction}}\;+\;\underbrace{\mathcal{O}(n_{l}d_{l})}_{\text{RTN + diagnostics}}\;=\;\mathcal{O}(n_{l}d_{l}^{2}),
```

i.e., FADE is asymptotically of the same order as a single GPTQ reconstruction pass and incurs at most a constant-factor overhead over a standard GPTQ pipeline. Empirical wall-clock timings are reported in Table 1.

#### Inference-time complexity.

FADE operates entirely in the offline quantization stage and does not modify the deployed model in any way. Formally, the quantized weights $`\{\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}`$ produced by FADE reside in the same low-bit space $`\mathbb{Q}^{n_{l}\times d_{l}}`$ as those produced by a standard GPTQ pipeline, and the model’s forward pass uses identical execution paths and runtime kernels. Consequently, the inference-time FLOPs, latency, and runtime memory footprint are unchanged. Since all extra computation introduced by FADE is confined to the offline stage and amounts to a single supplementary reconstruction pass per layer, the framework preserves the central practical advantages of post-training quantization—no retraining, no additional calibration data, and no runtime overhead after deployment—while extending PTQ with a principled, diagnostic-driven mechanism for per-layer compensation.

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

#### Audio Model Selection.

We select representative state-of-the-art model families from the Hugging Face ASR leaderboard \[srivastav2025openasrleaderboardreproducible\]: Whisper \[whisper\] (Tiny, Base, Small), Moonshine \[moonshine\] (Tiny, Base), and Qwen3-ASR \[Qwen3-ASR\] (0.6B, 1.7B). These families span an order of magnitude in parameter count and represent the current state of the art across encoder–decoder ASR architectures.

#### Datasets and Metrics.

We evaluate on the test splits of four benchmark datasets, including LibriSpeech \[panayotov2015librispeech\] (clean and other), SPGISpeech \[oneil2021spgispeech\], and TED-Lium \[hernandez2018ted\], reporting Word Error Rate (WER) as the primary metric. The detailed statistic of each dataset is shown in Table 2 We adopt asymmetric per-group quantization with group sizes of 64 for Whisper, 72 for Moonshine-Base, 52 for Moonshine-Small, and 128 for Qwen3-ASR, focusing on 4-bit (W4A16) and aggressive 3-bit (W3A16) weight quantization. Calibration uses 128 random samples from LibriSpeech-clean. All results are averaged over five runs with different random seeds.

Unless otherwise specified, we constrain the propagation coefficient to $`\alpha_{\ell}\in[\alpha_{\min},\alpha_{\max}]`$ with $`\alpha_{\min}=0.1`$ and $`\alpha_{\max}=0.8`$. These bounds are kept identical across all model families and bit-widths, and are not selected through any dataset- or model-specific hyperparameter search.

#### Word Error Rate(WER)

Word error rate (WER) is the standard evaluation metric for automatic speech recognition systems. It measures the discrepancy between the predicted transcription (hypothesis) and the ground-truth transcription (reference). Specifically, WER is computed based on the minimum edit distance between the two word sequences, which corresponds to the minimum number of substitutions, insertions, and deletions required to transform the hypothesis into the reference. Formally, WER is defined as

``` math
\mathrm{WER}=\frac{S+D+I}{N},
```

where $`S`$, $`D`$, and $`I`$ denote the number of substitutions, deletions, and insertions, respectively, and $`N`$ is the total number of words in the reference transcription. Note that due to insertions, WER can exceed $`100\%`$ \[jm3\].

### 4.2 Main Results

We evaluate FADE against four post-training quantization baselines (RTN, AWQ, GPTQ, and GPTQ+QEP) on three modern ASR families. Tables 3–5 report WER and run-to-run standard deviation under 3-bit and 4-bit weight quantization.

#### In-distribution evaluation on LibriSpeech (Table 3).

The most pronounced gains appear on the smallest models, where the calibration signal is noisiest and existing methods are least stable. On Whisper-Tiny, FADE reduces 3-bit WER from 94.62 (GPTQ+QEP) to 62.31—a $`34\%`$ relative improvement—and lowers the 4-bit standard deviation from 5.05 to 0.94, a roughly $`5\times`$ reduction in run-to-run variance. On Moonshine-Base, FADE attains 9.40 WER under 4-bit with standard deviation 0.06, less than half that of GPTQ+QEP and essentially closing the gap to the FP16 baseline of 9.02. On the larger Whisper-Base and Whisper-Small models, FADE consistently delivers substantially smaller standard deviations than the strongest baseline (1.89 vs. 7.46 on Base; 1.04 vs. 1.21 on Small), reflecting the stability gains expected from diagnostic-driven gating.

#### Cross-dataset generalization (Table 4).

Under 3-bit quantization, FADE achieves the lowest WER on Whisper-Base across LibriSpeech-clean (14.22 vs. 16.41 for GPTQ) and TED-Lium (32.13 vs. 35.59 for GPTQ+QEP), with standard deviations roughly half those of the strongest baseline. On Moonshine-Base, FADE reduces 3-bit WER on LibriSpeech-clean from 4.73 (GPTQ+QEP) to 3.10—a $`34\%`$ relative improvement that brings the 3-bit result within $`0.8`$ points of the FP16 baseline. Under 4-bit, FADE obtains the lowest variance on every column, with standard deviations as low as 0.01. These results indicate that the per-layer gating learned from in-distribution calibration generalizes to out-of-distribution test domains.

#### Modern large-scale ASR backbones (Table 5).

Table 5 reports results on Qwen3-ASR, which currently leads the Hugging Face ASR leaderboard \[srivastav2025openasrleaderboardreproducible\]. Under 4-bit quantization, FADE achieves the lowest mean WER on all eight (model, dataset) combinations across both 0.6B and 1.7B, with standard deviations that are either the lowest or tied for the lowest in every column. The most pronounced improvements appear on Qwen3-ASR-1.7B under 3-bit quantization: on TED-Lium, FADE reduces WER from 15.63 (GPTQ+QEP) to 11.63—a $`26\%`$ improvement—while cutting the standard deviation from 7.16 to 4.16. On LibriSpeech-clean, FADE brings the 3-bit WER to 2.34, within $`0.7`$ points of the FP16 baseline of $`1.61`$. To our knowledge, this is the first demonstration that 3-bit weight quantization of a $`\sim`$1.7B-parameter ASR model can operate in this near-FP16 regime across multiple benchmarks.

### 4.4 Ablation of the Diagnostic Signals

To verify that both diagnostic signals contribute to the effectiveness of FADE, we ablate $`\phi_{\mathrm{int}}`$ and $`\phi_{\mathrm{calib}}`$ on Whisper-Tiny and Qwen3-ASR-0.6B under 3-bit quantization. Disabling a signal corresponds to removing the corresponding term from the fused score $`s_{l}`$ in Eq. (21); disabling both reduces FADE to a constant gate $`\alpha_{l}=(\alpha_{\min}+\alpha_{\max})/2`$. As shown in Table 6, either signal alone already improves over the constant-gate baseline, while combining both yields the best mean WER and the lowest variance on both models. This empirically confirms the claim in Section 3.2 that $`\phi_{\mathrm{int}}`$ and $`\phi_{\mathrm{calib}}`$ capture complementary aspects of per-layer quantization difficulty: neither signal is sufficient on its own, and their fusion is what enables stable, accurate per-layer compensation.

### 4.5 Stability and Saturation Analysis of $`\alpha_{l}`$

To examine whether the learned per-layer coefficients $`\alpha_{l}`$ exploit the full feasible interval and remain stable under random initialization, we analyze their distribution. Since $`\alpha_{l}`$ is constrained to a configuration-dependent interval $`[\alpha_{\min},\alpha_{\max}]`$, we report the normalized quantity

``` math
\alpha_{\mathrm{rel}}(l)\;=\;\frac{\alpha_{l}-\alpha_{\min}}{\alpha_{\max}-\alpha_{\min}}\;\in\;[0,1].
```

Figure 6 shows the distribution of $`\alpha_{\mathrm{rel}}`$ on Whisper-Tiny under 3-bit and 4-bit quantization across multiple random seeds. Three observations stand out. First, the values concentrate in the interior of the interval rather than at the boundaries, indicating that the bounded sigmoid map in Eq. (22) does not suffer from saturation despite the use of a sigmoid transformation. Second, the distributions are highly consistent across random seeds, suggesting that the learned coefficients are stable and not sensitive to initialization. Third, the distribution exhibits mild multi-modality, indicating that different layers settle into different $`\alpha_{l}`$ regimes—an emergent property of the diagnostic-driven gating that supports the per-layer-sensitivity hypothesis underlying FADE. We further observe that 3-bit quantization induces a slightly shifted distribution relative to 4-bit, reflecting the increased sensitivity of the diagnostic signals at lower bit-widths.

### 4.6 Sensitivity to $`\alpha`$ Range

Figure 4 shows the sensitivity of performance to $`(\alpha_{\min},\alpha_{\max})`$ under different bit-widths.

#### Stable optimal region.

We observe that optimal performance does not concentrate at a single point, but instead forms a contiguous region in the parameter space. Configurations with small $`\alpha_{\min}`$ and large $`\alpha_{\max}`$ consistently yield low WER, indicating that the method is robust to moderate variations of the $`\alpha`$ range.

#### Asymmetric sensitivity.

A clear asymmetry is observed between $`\alpha_{\min}`$ and $`\alpha_{\max}`$. Increasing $`\alpha_{\max}`$ generally improves performance, while large $`\alpha_{\min}`$ leads to degradation. This suggests that cross-layer compensation is beneficial, but overly suppressing the local reconstruction objective harms performance. This behavior aligns with the design of FADE, which interpolates between local fidelity and cross-layer compensation.

#### Low-bit sensitivity.

Under 3-bit quantization, the performance landscape becomes significantly sharper, with larger fluctuations across configurations. This confirms that low-bit quantization amplifies sensitivity to hyperparameters and noise.

#### Stability-performance alignment.

We further observe that regions with low WER also exhibit low variance across seeds. This indicates that good configurations are both accurate and stable, rather than relying on favorable randomness.

Overall, these results suggest that the effectiveness of $`\alpha`$ stems from its role as a balancing mechanism between local fidelity and cross-layer compensation, rather than precise tuning of a single value.

### 4.7 Memory Analysis

Table 7 compares both static model memory and peak inference memory before and after quantization. Weight-only quantization consistently reduces model storage by approximately 71.9%, which is close to the theoretical $`4\times`$ compression from FP16 to 4-bit representation. This significantly improves deployability, especially for large models.

However, the reduction in peak inference memory is much smaller and, in some cases, negligible. This is because runtime memory is dominated not only by model weights but also by activations, attention buffers, intermediate tensors, and temporary workspace allocations.

As a result, weight-only quantization primarily addresses the storage bottleneck, determining whether a model can fit into device memory, while its impact on runtime memory is limited by the underlying inference pipeline.

In summary, quantization improves *model feasibility* (parameter memory footprint), but additional system-level optimizations are required to substantially reduce *runtime memory consumption*.

## 5 Conclusion

We studied low-bit post-training quantization for encoder–decoder ASR models and identified layer-wise sensitivity heterogeneity as a central challenge. Individual layers differ markedly in weight geometry and tolerance to quantization noise, making compensation schemes based on a single global coefficient inherently mismatched to the model. To address this issue, we proposed FADE, a diagnostic-driven framework that determines the per-layer compensation coefficient by fusing two complementary signals: an intrinsic weight-vulnerability score derived from weight geometry, and a calibration-reliability score derived from the data-driven solution. The resulting layer-wise adaptive gate requires no retraining, no additional calibration data, and no model-specific hyperparameter search. Experiments on Whisper, Moonshine, and Qwen3-ASR across four benchmarks show that FADE generally improves low-bit quantization performance over strong baselines at both 3- and 4-bit precision, with the largest gains under aggressive 3-bit settings, while also reducing run-to-run variance. These results highlight the importance of fine-grained, layer-adaptive error control for robust quantization of heterogeneous encoder–decoder ASR systems.
