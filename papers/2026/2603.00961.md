# Introduction

<span acronym-label="asr" acronym-form="singular+short">asr</span> systems convert speech into text and typically rely on large, diverse, and carefully transcribed corpora to achieve robust performance. Such resources remain scarce for most languages worldwide. Although recent work has improved the situation for Kazakh (a Turkic language spoken by more than 15 million people worldwide), it still qualifies as low-resource by global standards, while several other Turkic languages remain even more under-resourced .

We hypothesise that songs offer a practical, widely available, and language-agnostic data source that can complement or partially substitute conventional speech corpora for low-resource <span acronym-label="asr" acronym-form="singular+short">asr</span>. Songs are prevalent across many languages and communities; their recordings are often high quality, and accompanying lyrics can serve as approximate transcriptions. At the same time, songs pose challenges for <span acronym-label="asr" acronym-form="singular+short">asr</span>—background music, non-conversational prosody, elongated vowels, and repetitions—which makes their net utility an empirical question. This paper presents a feasibility study that asks: _To what extent can song audio with aligned lyric segments help adapt <span acronym-label="asr" acronym-form="singular+short">asr</span> for Kazakh?_

To investigate this, we curated a small song-based dataset of Kazakh consisting of 3,013 audio-text pairs drawn from 195 songs performed by 36 artists, amounting to approximately 4.5 hours. Using Whisper  as the base recogniser, we compare zero-shot models against fine-tuned models trained on songs, on each small corpus individually (<span acronym-label="cvc" acronym-form="singular+short">cvc</span> , <span acronym-label="fleurs" acronym-form="singular+short">fleurs</span> ), on each pair, and on all three combined, to systematically assess where songs help. For context, we also compare against an “upper bound” scenario that assumes access to over 1,100 hours of transcribed recordings from <span acronym-label="ksc" acronym-form="singular+full">ksc</span> , a scale of data far beyond what most low-resource languages can provide.

Our experiments show that incorporating songs yields substantial improvements over zero-shot baselines on multiple benchmarks (notably on <span acronym-label="cvc" acronym-form="singular+short">cvc</span> and <span acronym-label="fleurs" acronym-form="singular+short">fleurs</span>), and that pairing songs with small speech corpora further strengthens generalisation. However, the gains are uneven across evaluation domains, and song-augmented models do not match the performance of the large-corpus upper bound trained on <span acronym-label="ksc" acronym-form="singular+short">ksc</span>. These findings suggest that songs are not a panacea, but they are a promising, broadly accessible resource that can provide measurable benefits when large speech corpora are unavailable.

# Related Work

Research on Kazakh <span acronym-label="asr" acronym-form="singular+short">asr</span> has accelerated in recent years, supported by several initiatives that created foundational resources. <span acronym-label="ksc" acronym-form="singular+short">ksc</span> , for example, provides 1,128 hours of transcribed audio across more than 520,000 utterances. Other efforts, such as <span acronym-label="kazakhtts" acronym-form="singular+abbrv">kazakhtts</span> , its extension <span acronym-label="kazakhtts2" acronym-form="singular+abbrv">kazakhtts2</span> , and <span acronym-label="kazemotts" acronym-form="singular+abbrv">kazemotts</span> , have produced high-quality text-to-speech datasets that also support <span acronym-label="asr" acronym-form="singular+short">asr</span> research. Despite these contributions, Kazakh resources remain small compared to those available for high-resource languages, highlighting the need for alternative data sources.

Open-source multilingual datasets such as <span acronym-label="cvc" acronym-form="singular+short">cvc</span>  and <span acronym-label="fleurs" acronym-form="singular+short">fleurs</span>  include Kazakh and have proven valuable for training and benchmarking. Yet their scale and diversity are modest relative to corpora in languages like English or Mandarin , limiting their effectiveness for robust <span acronym-label="asr" acronym-form="singular+short">asr</span> development.

To address such scarcity, prior work in low-resource <span acronym-label="asr" acronym-form="singular+short">asr</span> has focused on techniques such as data augmentation and transfer learning. Data augmentation has been shown to improve performance by synthesising additional speech data , while transfer learning approaches leverage transliteration  or cross-lingual speech-to-text translation  to adapt models from high-resource languages. These methods, however, often still rely on a non-trivial amount of initial speech data, making them less applicable in scenarios where only minimal resources are available.

This motivates exploration of alternative resources that are both accessible and linguistically broad. In this paper, we propose the use of songs as such a resource. Songs exist in nearly every language, are often distributed in high-quality recordings, and are accompanied by lyrics that provide approximate transcriptions. While challenges include background music and non-standard prosody, their universality makes songs a promising but underexplored candidate for low-resource <span acronym-label="asr" acronym-form="singular+short">asr</span>. To our knowledge, no prior work has systematically investigated the use of songs for Kazakh or other low-resource languages, making this study a first step in addressing this gap.

# Dataset

## Song Selection and Collection

The dataset was collected over four months by the author. To achieve diversity, 195 Kazakh songs performed by 36 artists (14 female and 22 male) were selected. Songs were downloaded from YouTube using the `youtube_dl` library[^1]. Songs were selected according to the following criteria:

- **Vocal focus:** Only tracks featuring prominent solo vocals were included; songs dominated by choral arrangements, duets/overlapping lead vocals, or instrumental-only sections were excluded.

- **Genre diversity:** Songs representing a range of mainstream Kazakh music were included to capture stylistic variety (pop, pop-estrada, folk-pop, folk-rock, R&B, and hip-hop).

- **Artist representation:** Popular contemporary artists with publicly available songs were included.

- **Source search strategy:** Songs were identified using artist discographies, popular streaming platforms, and online music catalogues. Keywords included artist names, album titles, and widely recognised track titles.

In the interest of space, we do not list all artists and songs; however, each audio-text pair retains metadata with the corresponding artist name and song title to support reproducibility and future reference. In addition, each segment retains start/end timestamps to make the line-level segmentation reproducible.

## Data Preparation and Validation

Preprocessing involved several steps to prepare high-quality audio-text pairs suitable for <span acronym-label="asr" acronym-form="singular+short">asr</span>. First, vocal tracks were separated from instrumental accompaniment using `Spleeter`[^2]. While this process does not perfectly isolate vocals and may leave residual background music, the resulting tracks were deemed sufficiently clear to preserve intelligible speech content. As a quality-control step, we manually audited the separated vocals by listening and discarded segments where the lyrics were not intelligible due to residual accompaniment or separation artefacts.

Lyrics were collected from online repositories and official artist websites. As the accuracy of these sources varied, the lyrics were manually reviewed and corrected to match the actual sung content, including repetitions and colloquial pronunciations. Corrected texts retained original casing and punctuation, reflecting the structure of the lyrics rather than applying <span acronym-label="asr" acronym-form="singular+short">asr</span>-style normalisation.

Alignment was performed manually using `Audacity`[^3], an open-source audio editing tool. Each song was segmented at the line level by listening and synchronising with the corrected lyrics, after which the “Export Labels” feature in `Audacity` was used to generate audio-text pairs.

The resulting dataset comprises 3,013 pairs with a total duration of approximately 4.5 hours. Summary statistics are presented in Table <a href="#tab:summary_stats" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="tab:summary_stats">1</a>. Note that the totals for unique utterances and words are lower than the sum of female and male counts, as some material occurs in songs by both groups; such overlaps were removed in the overall totals. Mean utterance length was nearly identical across male and female artists (5.4 s), an outcome that appears coincidental given the diversity of genres and performers. The dataset, while carefully constructed, also presents several limitations, discussed below.

## Limitations

As with any dataset of this scale and nature, it has several limitations. First, its total duration of 4.5 hours is relatively small compared to conventional <span acronym-label="asr" acronym-form="singular+short">asr</span> training corpora, which may restrict model generalisation. Second, although multiple genres were included, the selection is not exhaustive and may underrepresent less common styles or regional variations. Third, despite vocal separation with `Spleeter`, residual background music remains in some segments, potentially introducing noise. Finally, alignment and lyric correction were performed manually by the author, which, although carefully conducted, may introduce subjective inconsistencies. These limitations highlight that the dataset should be viewed as a proof-of-concept resource rather than a comprehensive corpus.

# Methodology

## Training Setup

Given the computational expense of fine-tuning large <span acronym-label="asr" acronym-form="singular+short">asr</span> models, all experiments were conducted on `Vast.AI`[^4] using an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU (24 GB VRAM). Across all fine-tuning runs, the total compute cost was approximately \$25, underscoring the affordability of this approach relative to large-scale training.

The fine-tuning configuration was kept consistent across experiments. We used an initial learning rate of $`5 \times 10^{-6}`$ with 50 warm-up steps, a batch size of 60, and an early stopping criterion with a patience of two epochs. Training was conducted under seven scenarios using the training splits of the respective datasets: (i) Songs only, (ii) <span acronym-label="cvc" acronym-form="singular+short">cvc</span> only, (iii) <span acronym-label="fleurs" acronym-form="singular+short">fleurs</span> only, (iv) Songs + <span acronym-label="cvc" acronym-form="singular+short">cvc</span>, (v) Songs + <span acronym-label="fleurs" acronym-form="singular+short">fleurs</span>, (vi) <span acronym-label="cvc" acronym-form="singular+short">cvc</span> + <span acronym-label="fleurs" acronym-form="singular+short">fleurs</span>, and (vii) Songs + <span acronym-label="cvc" acronym-form="singular+short">cvc</span> + <span acronym-label="fleurs" acronym-form="singular+short">fleurs</span>. Validation was performed on the <span acronym-label="cvc" acronym-form="singular+short">cvc</span> and <span acronym-label="fleurs" acronym-form="singular+short">fleurs</span> validation sets. This choice reflects the fact that the Songs dataset was intended solely as a training resource, and that the <span acronym-label="ksc" acronym-form="singular+short">ksc</span> corpus differs substantially in format (all lowercase, no punctuation). In contrast, <span acronym-label="cvc" acronym-form="singular+short">cvc</span> and <span acronym-label="fleurs" acronym-form="singular+short">fleurs</span> include casing and punctuation, making them more compatible with the song-based training data and more informative for monitoring model generalisation. These format differences underscore the broader challenge of cross-dataset evaluation in Kazakh <span acronym-label="asr" acronym-form="singular+short">asr</span>, where corpora often vary in orthographic conventions and normalisation standards. Final evaluation was conducted on three independent benchmarks: the <span acronym-label="ksc" acronym-form="singular+short">ksc</span> test set , the <span acronym-label="cvc" acronym-form="singular+short">cvc</span> test set , and the <span acronym-label="fleurs" acronym-form="singular+short">fleurs</span> test set .

The benchmark datasets are briefly summarised below. <span acronym-label="ksc" acronym-form="singular+short">ksc</span> contains 1,128 hours of transcribed audio, crowdsourced and collected for both <span acronym-label="asr" acronym-form="singular+short">asr</span> and <span acronym-label="tts" acronym-form="singular+short">tts</span> development. It spans a wide range of sources, including news broadcasts, radio programs, parliamentary speeches, podcasts, and Kazakh-Russian code-switching utterances. <span acronym-label="cvc" acronym-form="singular+short">cvc</span> is a large-scale, volunteer-based corpus for multilingual <span acronym-label="asr" acronym-form="singular+short">asr</span>, which includes a Kazakh subset. Inspection of the Kazakh test set revealed that it predominantly consists of sayings and proverbs. <span acronym-label="fleurs" acronym-form="singular+short">fleurs</span> is a multilingual read-speech benchmark developed by Google Research. It extends the FLoRes machine translation dataset  by adding speech recordings of its sentences, originally derived from English Wikipedia. The Kazakh subset includes some words of foreign origin spelt in Latin script, in contrast to the Cyrillic-only orthography of the other test sets used here.

Before training and evaluation, additional preprocessing was applied for consistency. Two English sentences were identified in the <span acronym-label="fleurs" acronym-form="singular+short">fleurs</span> training set and removed. Sentences in the <span acronym-label="ksc" acronym-form="singular+short">ksc</span> test set that contained only Russian words were identified and removed. In addition, homoglyphs appearing in both the <span acronym-label="ksc" acronym-form="singular+short">ksc</span> and <span acronym-label="cvc" acronym-form="singular+short">cvc</span> test sets were replaced with their respective Kazakh letters to maintain script consistency.

Another important aspect of this study is that the datasets differ in their transcription conventions. The Songs dataset and <span acronym-label="cvc" acronym-form="singular+short">cvc</span> both use Cyrillic script with casing and punctuation, but contain no digits. In contrast, <span acronym-label="fleurs" acronym-form="singular+short">fleurs</span> includes a mix of Cyrillic and Latin script, digits, casing, and punctuation. The <span acronym-label="ksc" acronym-form="singular+short">ksc</span> corpus is distinct from all of these, being entirely lowercased, Cyrillic-only, and stripped of punctuation. ## Evaluation Metrics

To assess <span acronym-label="asr" acronym-form="singular+short">asr</span> performance, this study employed <span acronym-label="wer" acronym-form="singular+short">wer</span> and <span acronym-label="cer" acronym-form="singular+short">cer</span>, both of which are standard evaluation metrics in speech recognition . <span acronym-label="wer" acronym-form="singular+short">wer</span> measures transcription accuracy at the word level, while <span acronym-label="cer" acronym-form="singular+short">cer</span> provides finer-grained evaluation at the character level, making it particularly useful for languages with rich morphology such as Kazakh.

Two versions of <span acronym-label="wer" acronym-form="singular+short">wer</span> and <span acronym-label="cer" acronym-form="singular+short">cer</span> were computed. The first, orthographic <span acronym-label="wer" acronym-form="singular+short">wer</span> and <span acronym-label="cer" acronym-form="singular+short">cer</span> (<span acronym-label="wer" acronym-form="singular+short">wer</span>$`_{or}`$ and <span acronym-label="cer" acronym-form="singular+short">cer</span>$`_{or}`$), was calculated using the original transcriptions, preserving casing, punctuation, and extra whitespace. The second, normalised <span acronym-label="wer" acronym-form="singular+short">wer</span> and <span acronym-label="cer" acronym-form="singular+short">cer</span> (<span acronym-label="wer" acronym-form="singular+short">wer</span>$`_{no}`$ and <span acronym-label="cer" acronym-form="singular+short">cer</span>$`_{no}`$), was computed after normalising text by lowercasing, removing punctuation, and collapsing extra whitespace. Given the formatting of <span acronym-label="ksc" acronym-form="singular+short">ksc</span> (see Table <a href="#tab:formats" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="tab:formats">2</a>), we report only normalised <span acronym-label="wer" acronym-form="singular+short">wer</span> and <span acronym-label="cer" acronym-form="singular+short">cer</span> for the respective test set.

### Baselines

Zero-shot models struggle on Kazakh overall. On <span acronym-label="cvc" acronym-form="singular+short">cvc</span> (normalised), Whisper Large-V3 achieves 56.5, while the pruned Turbo variant is better at 47.7; however, Turbo is markedly worse on <span acronym-label="ksc" acronym-form="singular+short">ksc</span> (81.2 vs. 58.9). ### Fine-tuning Whisper Large-V3 Turbo

We fine-tuned the Turbo model on Songs, <span acronym-label="cvc" acronym-form="singular+short">cvc</span>, and <span acronym-label="fleurs" acronym-form="singular+short">fleurs</span>—individually and in mixtures.

#### Single-source fine-tuning.

_Songs only_ improves <span acronym-label="cvc" acronym-form="singular+short">cvc</span> and <span acronym-label="ksc" acronym-form="singular+short">ksc</span> over the Turbo baseline (<span acronym-label="cvc" acronym-form="singular+short">cvc</span>: 37.3 vs. 47.7; <span acronym-label="ksc" acronym-form="singular+short">ksc</span>: 45.2 vs. 81.2 normalised <span acronym-label="wer" acronym-form="singular+short">wer</span>), but hurts <span acronym-label="fleurs" acronym-form="singular+short">fleurs</span> (23.7 vs. 21.0). _<span acronym-label="cvc" acronym-form="singular+short">cvc</span> only_ helps in-domain (39.1 on <span acronym-label="cvc" acronym-form="singular+short">cvc</span>) but remains weak out-of-domain (<span acronym-label="fleurs" acronym-form="singular+short">fleurs</span> 37.9; <span acronym-label="ksc" acronym-form="singular+short">ksc</span> 58.1). _<span acronym-label="fleurs" acronym-form="singular+short">fleurs</span> only_ helps <span acronym-label="fleurs" acronym-form="singular+short">fleurs</span> strongly (13.6) and also improves <span acronym-label="ksc" acronym-form="singular+short">ksc</span> vs. baseline (46.6), with modest <span acronym-label="cvc" acronym-form="singular+short">cvc</span> gains (43.6).

## Qualitative Error Analysis

Beyond quantitative metrics, we analyse representative outputs in Table <a href="#tab:asr_samples" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="tab:asr_samples">[tab:asr_samples]</a> to understand how song-based adaptation affects recognition behaviour. Across all test sets, models further fine-tuned with Songs—Whisper Large-V3 Turbo fine-tuned on Songs + <span acronym-label="cvc" acronym-form="singular+short">cvc</span> + <span acronym-label="fleurs" acronym-form="singular+short">fleurs</span> (WLT_SCF) and the community <span acronym-label="ksc" acronym-form="singular+short">ksc</span>-trained model further fine-tuned on Songs + <span acronym-label="cvc" acronym-form="singular+short">cvc</span> + <span acronym-label="fleurs" acronym-form="singular+short">fleurs</span> (CFT_SCF)—exhibit more stable and linguistically coherent transcriptions than the zero-shot baselines.

A key difference is reduced cross-lingual drift. On the <span acronym-label="ksc" acronym-form="singular+short">ksc</span> sample, the zero-shot Whisper Large-V3 output shifts into another language and the turbo variant produces nonsensical tokens, whereas the song-adapted models remain consistently in Kazakh and recover the intended meaning with only minor variation. This suggests that exposure to song data strengthens lexical grounding and decoding stability under acoustically challenging conditions.

Improvements are also evident in lexical and morphological accuracy. On the <span acronym-label="cvc" acronym-form="singular+short">cvc</span> example, zero-shot models produce unintelligible outputs, while song-adapted models recover the syntactic structure and core vocabulary, with only minor phonetic substitutions (e.g., <span lang="ru">қ</span> → <span lang="ru">ұ</span>) that do not affect lexical interpretability. On the <span acronym-label="fleurs" acronym-form="singular+short">fleurs</span> sample, which represents instructional prose, song-adapted models more reliably preserve key lexical items and suffixes, whereas zero-shot variants exhibit vowel distortions and incorrect substitutions.

Finally, qualitative differences appear in punctuation and sentence segmentation. Song-adapted models more consistently restore clause boundaries and punctuation on the <span acronym-label="fleurs" acronym-form="singular+short">fleurs</span> example, suggesting improved modelling of prosodic and syntactic cues. This behaviour is consistent with the nature of song data, where lyrical phrasing and rhythmic pauses provide additional boundary information.

Overall, the qualitative evidence indicates that song-based fine-tuning improves not only acoustic robustness but also language stability, lexical recovery, morphological accuracy, and resistance to cross-lingual hallucinations, complementing the quantitative <span acronym-label="wer" acronym-form="singular+short">wer</span> improvements.

## Discussion

Three conclusions emerge. First, songs alone are not sufficient and may hurt some domains (e.g., <span acronym-label="fleurs" acronym-form="singular+short">fleurs</span> on Turbo; most domains on the <span acronym-label="ksc" acronym-form="singular+short">ksc</span> model). Second, songs are valuable when combined with modest corpora: the triple mixture achieves the best <span acronym-label="cvc" acronym-form="singular+short">cvc</span> (27.6) and ties the best <span acronym-label="fleurs" acronym-form="singular+short">fleurs</span> (11.8) on Turbo, while also halving <span acronym-label="ksc" acronym-form="singular+short">ksc</span> error relative to the Turbo baseline (39.3 vs. 81.2). Third, once a model is trained on approximately 1,100 hours (<span acronym-label="ksc" acronym-form="singular+short">ksc</span> upper bound), additional fine-tuning on songs/small corpora confers marginal gains at best and often induces forgetting.

A further factor likely contributing to the modest gains from song-only fine-tuning is dataset size. With only 4.5 hours of audio and roughly 3,000 lyric lines, the song corpus represents a very small fraction of the data typically required to meaningfully adapt a billion-parameter model such as Whisper. At this scale, the training signal may have been too limited to shift model parameters substantially, even though it proved complementary when combined with other small corpora. Future work should investigate whether larger and more diverse song collections—potentially including synthetic data—could yield stronger and more consistent adaptation effects.

Orthographic metrics reinforce these findings: while casing and punctuation remain challenging, mixtures (especially those including <span acronym-label="fleurs" acronym-form="singular+short">fleurs</span>) deliver substantial orthographic error reductions, indicating that the benefits of songs extend beyond pure lexical recognition to improved written-form fidelity.

#### Ethical and legal considerations.

Beyond these technical outcomes, it is important to acknowledge the elephant in the room: the copyrighted nature of the Songs dataset. The recordings used here are copyrighted works, and no explicit permission was obtained from the artists for use in <span acronym-label="asr" acronym-form="singular+short">asr</span> development. This raises a broader question: _Is the absence of prior research on songs as an <span acronym-label="asr" acronym-form="singular+short">asr</span> resource due primarily to lack of exploration, or to the legal and ethical complexities surrounding their use?_ This study is exploratory and not intended as a deployment-ready approach; rather, it seeks to assess whether songs have technical merit as a training signal. If the answer is yes, the next step would involve dialogue on how such data might be ethically and legally integrated into <span acronym-label="asr" acronym-form="singular+short">asr</span> development pipelines for low-resource languages—for example, through short excerpts, public-domain materials, collaborations with artists, or structured fair-use frameworks.

#### Synthetic alternatives.

A promising direction for addressing copyright concerns lies in synthetic music generation. Modern tools such as `Suno.com`[^9] can generate songs with customisable parameters: lyrics in low-resource languages, stylistic control (e.g., folk, pop, rap), and varied vocal timbres (male/female, solo/chorus). If song-based training proves beneficial, synthetic songs could provide a scalable and legally permissible alternative. They would allow researchers to systematically generate datasets reflecting specific phonetic or prosodic characteristics without relying on copyrighted works. This opens a potential new research avenue: _To what extent can synthetic songs stand in for real-world ones in improving low-resource <span acronym-label="asr" acronym-form="singular+short">asr</span>?_

# Conclusion

This study investigated the feasibility of using songs as a novel training resource for Kazakh <span acronym-label="asr" acronym-form="singular+short">asr</span>. The results show that while songs alone do not consistently improve recognition performance and can even degrade generalisation, they provide a meaningful signal when combined with modest corpora such as <span acronym-label="cvc" acronym-form="singular+short">cvc</span> and <span acronym-label="fleurs" acronym-form="singular+short">fleurs</span>. Mixtures that included songs consistently outperformed single-corpus baselines, with the best results achieved by combining all three datasets (Songs + <span acronym-label="cvc" acronym-form="singular+short">cvc</span> + <span acronym-label="fleurs" acronym-form="singular+short">fleurs</span>). In this setting, normalised <span acronym-label="wer" acronym-form="singular+short">wer</span> dropped to 27.6 on <span acronym-label="cvc" acronym-form="singular+short">cvc</span> and 11.8 on <span acronym-label="fleurs" acronym-form="singular+short">fleurs</span>, marking substantial improvements over the zero-shot Whisper models and narrowing the gap to the community model trained on the 1,100-hour <span acronym-label="ksc" acronym-form="singular+short">ksc</span> corpus. These findings highlight that songs are not a standalone solution but can amplify the value of small existing corpora in low-resource <span acronym-label="asr" acronym-form="singular+short">asr</span>.

The multi-domain evaluation also revealed important limitations. Song-based training does not fully transfer to conversational or broadcast speech, and gains remain modest compared to the large-scale upper bound. Moreover, orthographic errors (casing, punctuation) remain challenging, though the inclusion of songs helped reduce them in some scenarios, suggesting that lyrics-based data may support better modelling of written-form conventions.

From a practical standpoint, the entire set of fine-tuning experiments cost only \$25 in compute, underscoring that meaningful exploratory research in low-resource <span acronym-label="asr" acronym-form="singular+short">asr</span> can be conducted with modest resources.

Beyond technical findings, this work raises crucial legal and ethical questions. The Songs dataset consists of copyrighted recordings without explicit permission, underscoring the barriers to directly incorporating music into <span acronym-label="asr" acronym-form="singular+short">asr</span> pipelines. This study is therefore intended as a proof of concept rather than a deployable approach. The Kazakh Songs dataset—containing short vocal excerpts (≤ 10 s) aligned with lyric transcriptions—is made available on Hugging Face[^10] under a gated, non-commercial research licence. Full recordings are not distributed; access to excerpts is reviewed and granted solely for academic research purposes. Future research must explore ethical pathways, including collaboration with artists, the use of public-domain or fair-use excerpts, and the development of frameworks for responsible data use.

Finally, synthetic alternatives represent a promising direction. Modern tools such as `Suno.com` allow the generation of songs with customisable lyrics, genres, and voices, enabling the creation of corpora in low-resource languages without copyright restrictions. Such synthetic songs could be designed to cover diverse phonetic contexts, prosodic variations, and stylistic registers, offering a scalable complement to natural data. Exploring whether synthetic music can replicate or even surpass the benefits of real songs is a natural next step for advancing low-resource <span acronym-label="asr" acronym-form="singular+short">asr</span>.
