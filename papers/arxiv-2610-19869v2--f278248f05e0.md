# SEA-HELM Audio: Evaluating Multilingual ASR-LLM Pipeline Performance Across SEA-HELM Subsets

Aulia    Yixian    Jonathan    Kunat    Donghang    Anocha

###### Abstract

We introduce SEA-HELM Audio, a Southeast Asian (SEA) multilingual
evaluation framework that extends the text-based SEA-HELM benchmark to
assess performance degradation in cascaded automatic speech recognition
and large language model (ASR–LLM) pipelines. Using synthesized speech,
we compare speech-input and text-input performance across five
languages—Chinese, English, Malay, Indonesian, and Tamil—on
multiple-choice reasoning and instruction-following tasks. We evaluate
open-weight and commercial ASR–LLM systems using identical verifiers and
quantify the speech gap as the difference between text and speech
accuracy. Results show substantial degradation, with accuracy drops of
9.00–18.50 percentage points for multiple-choice reasoning and
20.00–42.86 points for instruction following. Tamil exhibits the largest
degradation, exceeding 40 percentage points. Our findings highlight the
limitations of text-only benchmarks in predicting voice-agent
reliability and demonstrate the value of SEA-HELM Audio for evaluating
multilingual voice-agent systems.

###### keywords

automatic speech recognition, multilingual evaluation, spoken language
understanding, SEA-HELM Audio

## 1 Introduction

As artificial intelligence systems transition from text-based interfaces
to conversational voice-enabled agents, evaluating the end-to-end
accuracy of cascaded speech-language stacks has become a critical
research priority. While text-based Large Language Models (LLMs)
continue to achieve high benchmark scores, deploying these models within
voice pipelines introduces acoustic transcription errors, phonetic
ambiguities, and domain-specific degradation. Quantifying this
degradation, specifically measuring the accuracy penalty a user incurs
by speaking rather than typing, is essential for understanding
real-world agent reliability across diverse tasks.

Existing spoken language model benchmarks, such as VoiceBench \[1\],
evaluate capabilities including speech recognition, reasoning, and
instruction following through spoken inputs. However, their
predominantly English-language evaluation settings provide limited
insight into how voice-agent systems perform on Southeast Asian
languages and tasks. In particular, it remains unclear to what extent
transcription errors alter task-critical information and how these
errors affect downstream task performance, especially in the context of
Southeast Asian languages.

To address the need for localized evaluation, AI Singapore developed
SEA-HELM (Southeast Asian Holistic Evaluation of Language Models) \[2\].
SEA-HELM provides authentic per-language prompts and tailored
programmatic verifiers across regional languages. However, SEA-HELM was
engineered natively as a text-only benchmark, consisting exclusively of
written prompts without paired audio recordings.

This paper addresses the central research question: How can a
text-based, regionally grounded benchmark be adapted into a speech-based
evaluation dataset to systematically measure the impact of speech input
on downstream LLM task performance across Southeast Asian languages? By
bridging SEA-HELM’s text prompts with synthesized audio pipelines, we
establish a framework to quantify the Speech gap, the difference between
$`\mathrm{Score}_{\mathrm{text}}`$ and
$`\mathrm{Score}_{\mathrm{speech}}`$, across multiple Southeast Asian
languages, providing empirical baselines for regional speech agent
deployment.

## 2 Methodology

This section outlines the experimental framework, dataset selection, and
evaluation metrics employed to systematically isolate and quantify the
performance degradation introduced by the speech modality across our
five target languages.

### 2.1 Experimental Design

Prior to finalizing the evaluation pipeline, we conducted an exploratory
phase to establish a robust and regionally relevant testing environment.
A primary distinction between our setup and existing voice evaluation
frameworks lies in audio corpus generation: VoiceBench uses pre-recorded
human speech, whereas SEA-HELM provides only text prompts. Consequently,
our cascade architecture synthesizes its own input speech before
evaluating downstream models.

To ensure audio fidelity across regional languages, we generated
synthetic speech with OmniVoice \[3\] Text-to-Speech (TTS) engines,
selected for acoustic naturalness, prosodic accuracy, and regional
accent representation.

For task design, we selected multiple-choice and instruction-following
formats, which are established in voice evaluation, and instantiated
them with SEA-HELM text prompts. This approach preserves regional
nuances, code-switching patterns, and local cultural contexts across
Chinese, Malay, Tamil, Indonesian, and English. By evaluating each
sample twice, once through the synthesized voice pipeline
($`\mathrm{Score}_{\mathrm{speech}}`$) and once via direct text prompts
($`\mathrm{Score}_{\mathrm{text}}`$), we establish a controlled baseline
to isolate and measure the precise performance penalty imposed by the
speech-to-text layer.

### 2.2 Datasets and Task Selection

To evaluate the voice cascade cost-effectively and deterministically, we
selected tasks from SEA-HELM that rely on rule-based scoring and closely
align with established VoiceBench task types.

The evaluation suite draws from four primary SEA-HELM task subsets,
summarized in Table 1.

| Task               | Task Type                   | SEA-HELM Languages | Evaluated Here                    | Selection Rationale                                                                                                                                    |
| ------------------ | --------------------------- | ------------------ | --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| SEA-HELM IFEval    | Instruction following \[4\] | 10                 | English, Malay, Indonesian, Tamil | Prompts are plain instructions that can be spoken as written. Scored by SEA-HELM’s multilingual verifier, which also verifies the response language.   |
| Global-MMLU-Lite   | Multiple choice \[5\]       | 7                  | Chinese, English                  | Short questions with four options; the answer is a single letter, so scoring is exact.                                                                 |
| SEA-HELM NLU QA    | Question answering          | 5                  | Future work                       | Scored with SEA-HELM’s rule-based token-F1 matching.                                                                                                   |
| SEA-HELM Safeguard | Harmful-request refusal     | 8                  | Future work                       | Harmful prompts used as a refusal probe (analogous to AdvBench \[6\] in VoiceBench), testing whether the LLM still refuses when the request is spoken. |

Table 1: SEA-HELM tasks considered for SEA-HELM Audio and their
evaluation scope.

#### 2.2.1 Benchmark Alignment and Active Subset Scope

For the initial empirical runs detailed in this study, benchmarking
focuses on the SEA-HELM IFEval and Global-MMLU-Lite subsets across our
five focus languages (Chinese, Malay, Tamil, Indonesian, and English).

The selection criteria prioritized two key attributes:

1.  1.  Speakable Prompts: Prompts can be read aloud as natural speech
        without removing formatting or template markup first.
2.  2.  Verifiable Evaluation: All scoring is executed via programmatic,
        rule-based verifiers, so the text path and the speech path are
        scored in exactly the same way.

### 2.3 Evaluation Metrics

To formalize the measurement of the speech modality penalty, the
following metrics were captured across every benchmark run:

- •
  Text accuracy ($`\mathrm{Score}_{\mathrm{text}}`$): The accuracy of
  the target LLM when provided with the original, ground-truth text
  input. This represents the theoretical performance ceiling for the
  task.
- •
  Speech accuracy ($`\mathrm{Score}_{\mathrm{speech}}`$): The end-to-end
  accuracy of the target LLM when the prompt is spoken, synthesized by
  TTS and transcribed by ASR.
- •
  $`\Delta`$ (The Gap):
  $`\Delta=\mathrm{Score}_{\mathrm{text}}-\mathrm{Score}_{\mathrm{speech}}`$.
  This absolute difference represents the modality penalty, quantifying
  how much task performance degrades due to transcription errors,
  phonetic ambiguities, or missing acoustic signals.

## 3 SEA-HELM Audio

SEA-HELM contains only written prompts, so SEA-HELM Audio creates the
spoken input itself: each prompt is read aloud by a speech synthesizer,
transcribed by the ASR system under test, and answered by the LLM.
Comparing this against the same LLM answering the original text isolates
the cost of the speech front-end.

### 3.1 Evaluation Flow

Each SEA-HELM prompt is evaluated in two ways (Figure 1). In the speech
path, the prompt is converted to speech with TTS, transcribed back to
text by ASR, and the transcript is answered by the LLM. In the text
path, the LLM answers the original prompt directly. Both answers are
scored by the same rule-based verifier, giving
$`\mathrm{Score}_{\mathrm{speech}}`$ and
$`\mathrm{Score}_{\mathrm{text}}`$. Because the two paths share the same
LLM and the same verifier, the difference between them ($`\Delta`$ =
$`\mathrm{Score}_{\mathrm{text}}`$ $`-`$
$`\mathrm{Score}_{\mathrm{speech}}`$) is the accuracy lost by speaking
the prompt instead of typing it.

Figure 1: SEA-HELM Audio evaluation: speech path (TTS $`\rightarrow`$
Speech $`\rightarrow`$ LLM) vs. text path (LLM only).

### 3.2 Spoken Prompt Construction

The TTS system plays the role of the user. Its voice is chosen
independently of the system being evaluated, so every ASR–LLM
configuration hears the same spoken prompts and differences in
$`\mathrm{Score}_{\mathrm{speech}}`$ reflect the system rather than the
input.

Only the content a user would actually say is synthesized. For
multiple-choice tasks, this is a short instruction followed by the
question and its four options; for instruction-following tasks, it is
the prompt exactly as written. Written conventions that do not read
naturally aloud are adapted per language: in Chinese, the option labels
A–D and the answer marker are written as Chinese words. The answer key
(the correct option, or the constraints an IFEval response must satisfy)
is never spoken and is used only at scoring time.

### 3.3 Speech Recognition

The ASR system turns the synthesized speech back into text. We evaluate
three systems chosen for their coverage of the target languages
(Table 2): Qwen3-ASR-1.7B \[7\] as an open-weight multilingual baseline,
MERaLiON3-ASR-3B \[8\] as a regionally adapted open-weight model for
Tamil, and ElevenLabs Scribe Realtime as a commercial service.
Open-weight models use greedy decoding so that transcripts are
reproducible.

Transcription quality is also measured directly, as Word Error Rate
(WER) and Character Error Rate (CER) between the transcript and the
original prompt. This separates how much the ASR changed the prompt from
how much those changes affected the task.

| ASR System                 | Type           | Languages          | Decoding         |
| -------------------------- | -------------- | ------------------ | ---------------- |
| Qwen3-ASR-1.7B             | Open-weight    | en, zh, ms, id     | Greedy           |
| MERaLiON3-ASR-3B           | Open-weight    | ta                 | Greedy           |
| ElevenLabs Scribe Realtime | Commercial API | en, zh, ms, id, ta | Provider default |

Table 2: ASR systems evaluated.

### 3.4 Response Generation

The LLM answers the ASR transcript (speech path) and the original prompt
(text path). We evaluate SEA-LION-4.5-27B \[9\], a regional open-weight
model, and Gemini 3.5 Flash, a commercial model. Both use deterministic
decoding (temperature 0), so any difference between the two paths comes
from the input rather than from sampling.

### 3.5 Scoring

All responses are scored with rule-based verifiers taken from SEA-HELM,
which keeps scoring deterministic, cheap to repeat, and identical across
the two paths. For multiple-choice questions, the chosen option letter
(A–D) is extracted from the response and compared with the answer key.
For instruction following, each response is checked against the
constraints stated in its prompt and is counted as correct only if it
also uses the expected language.

## 4 Results

This section presents the empirical findings from our multi-lingual
voice cascade evaluations. Performance is reported across two primary
axes: task type (multiple-choice questions via Global-MMLU-Lite
vs. instruction following via SEA-HELM IFEval) and pipeline
configuration (Regional Open-Weight Baseline vs. Commercial Stack).

### 4.1 Multiple Choice Reasoning (Global-MMLU-Lite)

Evaluations on the SEA-HELM Global-MMLU-Lite subset were conducted for
Chinese and English, assessing general domain knowledge under a
multiple-choice format (400 samples per run). Across both languages, the
commercial stack (ElevenLabs Scribe Realtime paired with Gemini 3.5
Flash) outperformed the regional open-weight baseline in both text
accuracy and speech accuracy.

However, Chinese demonstrated a distinct resilience to the modality
shift compared to English. For Chinese, $`\Delta`$ remained between 9.00
and 10.75 points, whereas English incurred a much steeper penalty of
15.50 to 18.50 points.

| Language | ASR Model                  | LLM              | Speech | Text  | $`\Delta`$ |
| -------- | -------------------------- | ---------------- | ------ | ----- | ---------- |
| Chinese  | Qwen3-ASR-1.7B             | SEA-LION-4.5-27B | 71.50  | 80.50 | 9.00       |
| Chinese  | ElevenLabs Scribe Realtime | Gemini 3.5 Flash | 79.75  | 90.50 | 10.75      |
| English  | Qwen3-ASR-1.7B             | SEA-LION-4.5-27B | 69.00  | 87.50 | 18.50      |
| English  | ElevenLabs Scribe Realtime | Gemini 3.5 Flash | 76.75  | 92.25 | 15.50      |

Table 3: Global-MMLU-Lite results (400 samples per run; TTS: OmniVoice).
Accuracy (Speech, Text) and $`\Delta`$ = Text $`-`$ Speech, in
percentage points.

### 4.2 Instruction Following (SEA-HELM IFEval)

SEA-HELM IFEval provided multiple test of deterministic instruction
adherence across English, Malay, Indonesian, and Tamil (105 samples per
run). The text-only baselines achieved exceptionally high scores,
peaking at 97.14% for English, but introducing the speech modality
resulted in severe performance degradation across all tested languages.

The modality gap ($`\Delta`$) expanded significantly in
instruction-following tasks compared to the MCQ evaluations. Tamil
suffered the steepest performance drops (exceeding 40 points) for both
the specialized MERaLiON3-ASR-3B pipeline and the commercial ElevenLabs
equivalent, indicating severe structural vulnerabilities in Tamil ASR
transcriptions.

While the commercial stack generally outperformed the baseline,
SEA-LION-4.5-27B proved highly competitive in text accuracy for
Southeast Asian languages, notably surpassing Gemini 3.5 Flash in
Indonesian IFEval (93.33% vs 89.52%). Despite this strong text
foundation, the open-weight pipeline struggled to recover from
ASR-induced errors, reflecting $`\Delta`$ of over 25 points in Malay and
Indonesian.

| Language   | ASR Model                  | LLM              | Speech | Text  | $`\Delta`$ |
| ---------- | -------------------------- | ---------------- | ------ | ----- | ---------- |
| English    | Qwen3-ASR-1.7B             | SEA-LION-4.5-27B | 69.52  | 97.14 | 27.62      |
| English    | ElevenLabs Scribe Realtime | Gemini 3.5 Flash | 76.19  | 97.14 | 20.95      |
| Indonesian | Qwen3-ASR-1.7B             | SEA-LION-4.5-27B | 60.95  | 94.29 | 33.33      |
| Indonesian | ElevenLabs Scribe Realtime | Gemini 3.5 Flash | 58.10  | 89.52 | 31.43      |
| Malay      | Qwen3-ASR-1.7B             | SEA-LION-4.5-27B | 52.38  | 84.76 | 32.38      |
| Malay      | ElevenLabs Scribe Realtime | Gemini 3.5 Flash | 60.00  | 80.00 | 20.00      |
| Tamil      | MERaLiON3-ASR-3B           | SEA-LION-4.5-27B | 47.62  | 87.62 | 40.00      |
| Tamil      | ElevenLabs Scribe Realtime | Gemini 3.5 Flash | 41.90  | 81.90 | 40.00      |

## 5 Discussion & Analysis

The empirical results underscore a critical reality in voice-agent
deployment: strong text-based LLM performance does not guarantee
equivalent voice capabilities. The modality gap ($`\Delta`$) observed
across our evaluations highlights significant disparities driven by
language, task structure, and pipeline composition.

### 5.1 Linguistic Resilience vs. Vulnerability

The most striking finding is the sheer variance in the gap across the
five evaluated languages.

- •
  Resilience in Chinese: Chinese exhibited the lowest performance
  degradation across all configurations, maintaining a Delta of
  approximately 9.00 to 10.75 points. This suggests two compounding
  advantages: first, modern ASR systems (both Qwen and ElevenLabs)
  possess highly robust acoustic models for Chinese; second, the
  downstream LLMs demonstrate strong semantic recovery, able to infer
  correct answers even in the presence of minor phonetic transcription
  errors.
- •
  The Tamil Bottleneck: Conversely, Tamil suffered the most severe
  modality penalty, shedding over 40 points in accuracy when
  transitioning from text to voice. Despite SEA-LION-4.5-27B achieving a
  respectable 85.71% baseline accuracy on perfect text, the end-to-end
  performance collapsed to 42.86% utilizing the specialized
  MERaLiON3-ASR-3B model. This indicates a severe bottleneck at the
  acoustic processing layer, highlighting a critical data scarcity issue
  in Tamil ASR training corpora compared to high-resource languages.

### 5.2 Task Format Sensitivity: MCQ vs. Instruction Following

The data reveals that the modality gap is heavily influenced by the
nature of the evaluation task.

On Global-MMLU-Lite (MCQ), LLMs could often recover from noisy
transcripts because multiple-choice reasoning primarily requires
identifying key semantic concepts to select an option (A, B, C, or D).
If an ASR model slightly misheard a noun, the LLM could still deduce the
correct multiple-choice letter from contextual clues.

IFEval, in contrast, scores each response against explicit lexical,
length, and format constraints stated in the prompt. When ASR drops or
substitutes a word that carries one of these constraints, the LLM
answers a different instruction, and the rule-based verifier marks the
response as a failure even if it is otherwise correct. This mechanism
accounts for the larger IFEval gaps of 20 to 43 points.

### 5.3 Commercial vs. Open-Weight

While the commercial stack (ElevenLabs Scribe Realtime + Gemini 3.5
Flash) consistently delivered higher speech accuracy, analyzing text
accuracy reveals a nuanced picture of regional model capabilities.

The SEA-LION-4.5-27B model proved highly competitive, and occasionally
superior, in pure-text regional evaluations. Most notably, it
outperformed Gemini in the Indonesian IFEval text baseline (94.29%
vs. 89.52%). The fact that SEA-LION’s end-to-end voice performance drops
so sharply (a 33.33 point penalty) confirms that the limiting factor in
the open-weight pipeline is not the LLM’s regional reasoning capability,
but rather the fragility of the open-weight ASR models (Qwen3-ASR-1.7B
and MERaLiON3-ASR-3B) handling Southeast Asian accents and
code-switching.

For organizations deploying regional voice agents, these findings
suggest that heavily investing in localized ASR fine-tuning will yield a
much higher return on investment than further scaling the LLM
parameters.

## 6 Conclusion and Future Work

This study shows that a text-native benchmark, such as SEA-HELM, can be
adapted to benchmark end-to-end voice pipelines, provided the evaluation
tasks are highly deterministic. By measuring the modality gap
($`\Delta`$) between pure text accuracy and speech accuracy across five
languages, we have isolated the precise performance penalty introduced
when a user speaks to a conversational agent rather than typing.

Our findings demonstrate that voice pipeline reliability is heavily
dependent on the target language and the structural rigidity of the
task. While Chinese multiple-choice reasoning exhibited a robust
resilience to the modality shift ($`\Delta<11`$ points), instruction
following in Tamil suffered catastrophic degradation ($`\Delta>40`$
points). Furthermore, while the commercial stack (ElevenLabs Scribe
Realtime + Gemini 3.5 Flash) provided the most stable end-to-end
performance, the strong pure-text baseline of SEA-LION-4.5-27B suggests
that the primary bottleneck for regional open-weight deployments lies in
the acoustic transcription layer, not the language model’s reasoning
capabilities.
