# Reading-Level Verification of Kanji Words in Japanese Financial Speech Synthesis

Cheng ZHANG Affiliation: Best AI Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan  
Email: bestaijapan@gmail.com

###### Abstract

Few-shot voice cloning makes it cheap to produce daily Japanese
financial commentary with several synthetic speakers, but kanji words
with more than one reading, such as 市場 (shijō vs. ichiba) or 大勢
(taisei vs. ōze), are still misread. A common quality gate transcribes
each synthesized sentence with automatic speech recognition (ASR),
converts both the input and the transcript to kana, and regenerates on
mismatch. We show that this gate is unreliable in both directions: when
the audio contains a misreading, the ASR language model frequently
writes the expected kanji back, hiding the error, and when the audio is
correct, ordinary recognition errors trigger needless regeneration. We
propose a reading-level check that scores the expected reading and its
confusable alternatives with a connectionist temporal classification
(CTC) hiragana recognizer and flags a word when an alternative explains
the audio better. On 540 utterances from three voice-cloned production
voices covering 30 financial terms, with misreadings injected under
controlled conditions, the transcript-based gate detected 78.9% of
misreadings at a 44.4% false-alarm rate, whereas the proposed check
detected 91.7% at 5.0% (AUC 0.990). On natural kanji input, it caught a
front-end error that the transcript-based gate missed for one voice, and
reduced regeneration requests from 38.9% to 6.1% of sentences.

## I Introduction

Synthetic voices are increasingly used to produce daily market
commentary for video platforms and podcasts, often as a dialogue between
two characters. Few-shot voice cloning systems built on neural codec or
semantic-token language models \[1, 2\] allow a small team to maintain
several distinct voices and to generate a ten-minute episode in a few
minutes. In this setting the main quality problem is no longer
naturalness but _correctness_. Listeners of financial content are
sensitive to terminology: a voice that reads 市場 (market) as _ichiba_
(marketplace) or 大勢 (overall trend) as _ōze_ (a crowd) sounds
amateurish and damages the credibility of the channel.

Japanese is particularly prone to such errors because many kanji words
have several readings whose choice depends on domain and context.
Financial text adds a vocabulary of jargon with idiosyncratic readings
(上値 _uwane_, 後場 _goba_, 手仕舞い _tejimai_) and of numeral
expressions whose euphonic changes are easy to get wrong (十分 _juppun_
“ten minutes” vs. _jūbun_ “enough”). Misreadings come from two sources:
the text front-end may predict the wrong reading, and the acoustic
model, fine-tuned on limited speaker data, may deviate from the phonemes
it was given. In our production system we have observed both, e.g., 千円
rendered as _sennen_ in all of nine candidate generations although the
front-end reading was correct.

A practical and widely used safeguard is an ASR-based gate: synthesize
each sentence, transcribe it with a robust recognizer such as
Whisper \[8\], compare the transcript with the input, and regenerate on
mismatch. Comparing kana readings rather than surface strings avoids
false alarms from orthographic variants (上げ/揚げ). Our own pipeline
uses exactly this design. However, the gate relies on the recognizer’s
_text_ output, and a strong recognizer is also a strong language model.
When the audio says _ichiba_ in a sentence about stocks, the most
probable transcript is still 市場, which the morphological analyzer
converts back to the expected _shijō_; the gate passes and the error
reaches the listener. Conversely, when the audio is correct, recognition
errors on rare jargon change the kana string and cause needless
regeneration.

This paper makes three contributions:

- •
  We quantify, under controlled error injection, how often the
  transcript-based kana gate misses misreadings and how often it raises
  false alarms on correct audio, using three production voices and 30
  financial terms.
- •
  We propose a reading-level check that compares CTC likelihoods of the
  expected reading and its confusable alternatives, independent of the
  recognizer’s choice of kanji.
- •
  We show that the check also detects a real front-end misreading in
  natural generation and sharply reduces unnecessary regeneration.

## II Related Work

Reading prediction. Japanese TTS front-ends predict readings with a
morphological analyzer and dictionary, as in Open JTalk \[4\] and its
Python binding pyopenjtalk \[5\], optionally with user dictionaries.
Neural approaches to heteronym and polyphone disambiguation, e.g., g2pW
for Mandarin \[6\], improve accuracy on general text. All of these act
_before_ synthesis and cannot observe errors introduced by the acoustic
model, nor verify that a domain term was spoken as intended.

Neural TTS and voice cloning. End-to-end models such as VITS \[7\]
synthesize speech directly from phonemes, while recent few-shot systems
treat TTS as language modeling: VALL-E \[1\] predicts neural codec
tokens and SPEAR-TTS \[2\] predicts self-supervised semantic tokens
autoregressively from text, before a second stage generates the
waveform. Such two-stage systems build custom voices from small amounts
of speech, but their autoregressive stage can deviate from the input
phonemes, and for Japanese they still depend on a front-end for
readings.

ASR for TTS evaluation. ASR transcripts are routinely used to measure
the intelligibility of synthesized speech via character or word error
rate, and large models such as Whisper \[8\] and WhisperX \[9\] are
popular for this purpose. Such metrics measure what the recognizer
_writes_, which for logographic scripts is only loosely tied to what was
_pronounced_.

Pronunciation assessment. Scoring the likelihood of an expected phone
sequence against alternatives is the basis of goodness-of-pronunciation
measures in computer-assisted language learning \[10\]. We apply the
same idea to synthesized speech, using a CTC \[11\] recognizer with kana
outputs and a small set of confusable readings per word.

## III System

### III-A Generation Pipeline

Fig. 1 shows the production loop. Scripts are written as dialogue; each
line carries a speaker and a speaking-style tag. Lines of the same
speaker are synthesized together to avoid repeated model loading and are
concatenated in script order with 0.3 s pauses between sentences and
0.6 s at speaker or paragraph changes. The voices are built on an
open-source two-stage voice-cloning framework of the type described in
Section II: pyopenjtalk converts the text to phonemes, an autoregressive
Transformer predicts self-supervised semantic tokens from the phonemes,
and a VITS-based decoder \[7\] generates the waveform. Each voice is
fine-tuned with LoRA \[3\] on approximately 30 minutes of Japanese
speech from a single speaker and conditioned on a 3–10 s reference clip
per style.

Before synthesis, a substitution table rewrites expressions that have
been misread in the past into kana (e.g., 円安 $`\rightarrow`$ えんやす,
千円 $`\rightarrow`$ せんえん). Entries are applied longest-match first,
may be restricted to particular voices, and numeral-initial entries are
skipped when preceded by a digit. The original text is kept for
subtitles and verification. The table is reactive by design: an entry is
added only after an error has been heard, which is why automatic
detection matters.

### III-B Stage 1: Transcript-Based Kana Check

Each synthesized sentence is transcribed by WhisperX (large-v3) \[9\].
Both the input text and the transcript are converted to katakana
readings with pyopenjtalk, all non-kana symbols are removed, and the
sentence passes only if the two strings are identical. Orthographic
variants therefore count as matches. This stage reliably catches
skipped, repeated or hallucinated phrases, which are typical failure
modes of autoregressive TTS, and misreadings that lead the recognizer to
a different word.

### III-C Stage 2: Reading-Level Check

Stage 2 targets words with known confusable readings. For each such word
$`w`$ in a sentence, let $`r^{*}`$ be the expected reading, taken from a
domain lexicon, and $`\mathcal{A}(w)`$ a small set of alternative
readings that a front-end or acoustic model could plausibly produce (in
this study, one alternative per word; Table I). The sentence is split as
$`x=p\,w\,q`$, and the hiragana readings $`\rho(p)`$ and $`\rho(q)`$ of
the surrounding text are taken from the front-end. For a candidate
reading $`r`$, the sentence-level label sequence is
$`y(r)=\rho(p)\,r\,\rho(q)`$, and its score is the CTC log-likelihood

```math
s(r)=\log P_{\mathrm{CTC}}\bigl(y(r)\mid\mathbf{a}\bigr), \tag{1}
```

where $`\mathbf{a}`$ is the audio and $`P_{\mathrm{CTC}}`$ is computed
by a wav2vec 2.0 XLSR-53 model \[12\] fine-tuned on Common Voice
Japanese \[13\] with hiragana targets \[14\]. The word is flagged if

```math
m(w)=\max_{r\in\mathcal{A}(w)}s(r)-s(r^{*})>\theta, \tag{2}
```

and the sentence fails if any word is flagged. Because $`y(r)`$ and
$`y(r^{*})`$ differ only in the target span, context errors of the
recognizer affect both scores similarly and largely cancel in the margin
$`m(w)`$. The decision depends on acoustic evidence for each reading
rather than on which kanji a language model would write. The cost is one
forward pass of the CTC model per sentence plus one CTC loss evaluation
per candidate.

### III-D Building the Reading Lexicon

Stage 2 needs, for each word to be verified, its expected reading and a
few confusable alternatives. We build this lexicon from three sources.
First, every entry added to the substitution table records an observed
misreading, which becomes an alternative for that word. Second, domain
glossaries of market terms provide the expected financial readings,
which often differ from the general-domain default (e.g., 上値 _uwane_,
後場 _goba_). Third, alternatives can be enumerated from dictionary
readings of the constituent kanji and from the front-end’s own output
when it disagrees with the glossary; this third step is not used in the
present study, where one alternative per word was chosen manually.
Because the check only scores readings and does not need audio examples,
a new term can be covered by adding one line to the lexicon.

The expected reading must not be taken from the same front-end that
drives the synthesizer: if the front-end is wrong, synthesizer and
verifier agree on the wrong reading. Section V-F shows a real instance
of this failure.

### III-E Regeneration Policy

A sentence failing either stage is regenerated with a new random seed up
to $`K`$ times ($`K\!=\!9`$ in production: three rounds of three
candidates), and the first candidate passing both stages is kept. If
none passes, the best-scoring candidate is used and the flagged word is
reported, so that the operator can add an entry to the substitution
table.

## IV Experimental Setup

### IV-A Voices and Test Set

We used three production voices: two female (F1, F2) and one male (M1),
fine-tuned from the framework’s pretrained models with LoRA ranks 64, 32
and 32, respectively. Each voice was conditioned on its default
reference clip.

We selected 30 terms frequent in Japanese market commentary, each with
one financial reading and one plausible misreading (Table I). The
misreadings fall into three types: (a) _reading choice_, where a
different on- or kun-reading of the same characters is used (21 terms,
e.g., 市場 _ichiba_, 前場 _maeba_, 相場 _aiba_); (b) _sound change_,
where the correct morphemes are used but voicing or gemination is wrong
(7 terms, e.g., 大手 _ōde_, 寄り付き _yoridzuki_, 手仕舞い _teshimai_,
日経平均 _nichikei_); and (c) _numeral euphony_ (2 terms: 十分 _jūbun_,
千円 _sennen_). Type (a) changes several morae, whereas type (b) often
changes a single phonetic feature. For each term we wrote two natural
sentences in which context determines the financial reading, giving 60
sentences with a mean duration of 4.1 s when synthesized.

### IV-B Conditions and Ground Truth

Each sentence was synthesized by each voice under three input conditions
with a fixed seed: K, the target word replaced by its expected reading
in hiragana; W, the target word replaced by the injected misreading in
hiragana; and N, the original kanji text. This yields
$`60\times 3\times 3=540`$ utterances. Because the front-end converts
hiragana to phonemes deterministically, K and W utterances are requested
to contain the correct and the wrong reading, respectively, which
provides ground truth without manual labeling. The 360 K and W
utterances form the _controlled set_; the 180 N utterances measure
behavior on natural input. In all conditions the verifier is given the
original kanji sentence, as in production.

### IV-C Compared Methods

We compare (i) the deployed Stage 1 gate; (ii) a direct phonetic
transcription baseline, which decodes the hiragana CTC model greedily
and flags the word if the expected reading does not occur in the output;
and (iii) the proposed Stage 2 check with $`\theta=0`$. We report recall
on W utterances, false-alarm rate on K utterances, precision, and the
area under the ROC curve (AUC) of the margin $`m(w)`$, with Wilson 95%
confidence intervals and exact McNemar tests for paired comparisons.

injected misreadings and, more strikingly, rejected 44.4% of utterances

Stage 1 false alarms on correct audio were caused by recognition errors

### V-C Failure Cases of the Proposed Check

The proposed check missed all six misreadings of 寄り付き (_yoritsuki_
vs. _yoridzuki_). The two readings differ only in the voicing of one
mora, and the CTC model’s outputs for this region were unstable (e.g.,
_yarisuki_, _yoritsuki_) in both conditions. Excluding this term, recall
was 94.8% (165/174) at the same false-alarm count. The remaining misses
(円安, 空売り, 大勢, 市場, 大手, 人気; one or two utterances each) had
small negative margins in utterances where the recognizer’s output was
degraded overall. The nine false alarms concentrated on 大引け and
手仕舞い, terms whose correct reading contains a voiced mora that the
recognizer frequently heard as unvoiced. These cases suggest that
minimal voicing contrasts call for a stronger acoustic model or a
word-specific threshold.

Table VII confirms this pattern by misreading type. For reading-choice
errors, which change several morae, the proposed check detected 93.7%
with a single false alarm in 126 correct utterances, while Stage 1
produced 61 false alarms. Sound-change errors were harder for both
methods: the proposed check detected 83.3% (35/42), six of the seven
misses being 寄り付き, and 7 of its 9 false alarms fell into this type.
Numeral-euphony errors were detected in all 12 utterances.

### V-D Combining the Two Stages

In production the two stages are applied in sequence, so a sentence is
rejected if either fails. With the deployed exact-match Stage 1, the
combination detects 177/180 misreadings but inherits Stage 1’s false
alarms (83/180 on K). Stage 1 is still needed for omissions, insertions
and garbled speech, which Stage 2 does not target, but it need not
require an exact kana match. Relaxing Stage 1 to reject only sentences
whose kana similarity to the input (difflib ratio) falls below 0.8
leaves just 2 Stage 1 rejections among the 180 correct K utterances;
combined with Stage 2 this yields 165/180 recall at 11/180 false alarms,
and 12/180 regeneration requests on natural input. We therefore
recommend a lenient Stage 1 for gross errors and Stage 2 for readings.
Our test set contains no omissions, so the appropriate Stage 1 threshold
for catching them remains to be validated.

### V-E Computational Cost

Stage 2 requires one forward pass of the 300M-parameter CTC model per
sentence and one CTC loss per candidate reading. On one NVIDIA H100 NVL
GPU, scoring took 17.6 ms per sentence on average (median 14.9 ms) for
utterances averaging 4.1 s, a real-time factor of 0.004, which is
negligible compared with synthesis and Whisper transcription.

### V-F Natural Input

With the original kanji input, the front-end produced the expected
reading for 29 of 30 terms; it read 大勢 as _ōze_ (a crowd) in both of
its sentences, so all six N utterances of 大勢 contained a real
misreading originating in the front-end. Stage 1 flagged 4 of the 6: for
M1 both utterances passed because Whisper transcribed the audio as 大勢,
which pyopenjtalk again read as _ōze_, matching the (equally wrong)
expected reading derived from the input. The proposed check, whose
expected reading comes from the domain lexicon rather than the
front-end, flagged 5 of 6. This illustrates a second blind spot: a
verifier that derives its expectation from the same front-end as the
synthesizer cannot detect front-end errors.

On the remaining 174 natural utterances, Stage 1 requested regeneration
for 66 (37.9%) and the proposed check for 6 (3.4%); over all 180 natural
utterances the rates were 38.9% and 6.1%. Stage 1’s rate varied strongly
with the voice (F1 50.0%, F2 41.7%, M1 25.0%), reflecting how well
Whisper recognized each voice rather than how many misreadings it
produced; the proposed check’s rate was 10.0%, 5.0% and 3.3%,
respectively. The six natural utterances flagged only by the proposed
check (大引け $`\times 2`$, 手仕舞い $`\times 3`$, 一時 $`\times 1`$)
were verified by a native listener, and all six contained the correct
reading. They are therefore false alarms, and they involve the same
voiced morae (_bi_ in _ōbike_, _ji_ in _tejimai_) that caused false
alarms in the controlled set. In total, 5 of the proposed check’s 11
alarms on natural input were genuine front-end misreadings and 6 were
false alarms, a false-alarm rate of 3.4% (6/174) on utterances without
front-end errors, consistent with the 5.0% measured on the controlled
set. Stage 1 raised 66 alarms on the same 174 utterances; we did not
verify all of them by listening, but the front-end readings of these
utterances were correct, 4 of them are among the six utterances verified
as correct above, and the remaining 62 had negative margins, so most of
them are likely needless regenerations.

## VI Discussion and Limitations

Practical guidelines. From these results we derive three recommendations
for production pipelines. (1) Do not use exact kana match of an ASR
transcript as the only correctness gate for Japanese: it both hides
misreadings of common terms and rejects many correct sentences. Use it
leniently, for gross errors only. (2) Verify readings of known ambiguous
terms with likelihood comparison of explicit candidates, with the
expected reading taken from a domain lexicon rather than from the
synthesizer’s front-end. (3) Choose $`\theta`$ according to the cost of
errors: for published content where a misreading is costly, a negative
threshold (e.g., $`\theta=-5`$, 93.9% recall at 8.3% false alarms) is
affordable because a false alarm only costs one regeneration of a short
sentence. Fig. 4 also shows that natural utterances score like the K
condition, so the controlled false-alarm rates are representative of
production.

Limitations. The controlled set relies on injected misreadings, which
are spoken fluently by the TTS model and may be easier to detect than
natural acoustic deviations that are partially articulated. The ground
truth assumes that the acoustic model followed the requested phonemes;
the K-condition false alarms show that this is mostly but not always
true, so a small part of the reported errors may be label noise. The
proposed check requires a lexicon of terms with their expected and
confusable readings. In our use case this lexicon grows naturally from
the substitution table and from domain glossaries, but it does not cover
unseen words; Stage 1 remains necessary for omissions and insertions,
and the two stages are complementary. Finally, the study uses one TTS
architecture, one CTC model trained on read speech, and 60 sentences;
pitch-accent errors, which are also noticeable to native listeners, are
not addressed.

## VII Conclusion

We showed that ASR-based verification of synthesized Japanese, even at
the kana level, is unreliable for kanji reading errors: the recognizer’s
language model hides misreadings of common financial terms, recognition
errors on jargon cause frequent false alarms, and expectations derived
from the synthesizer’s own front-end cannot reveal front-end errors. A
reading-level check that compares CTC likelihoods of the expected and
confusable readings detected 91.7% of injected misreadings at a 5.0%
false-alarm rate across three voices, caught a real front-end
misreading, and reduced regeneration requests on natural input from
38.9% to 6.1%. Future work includes automatic generation of confusable
readings, word-specific thresholds for voicing contrasts, pitch-accent
verification, and extension to Mandarin polyphonic characters.
