# ASR-Style Transcript Normalization Removes Useful Pragmatic Signal for Dialogue Act Classification

###### Abstract

Automatic speech recognition (ASR) pipelines often normalize transcripts
by removing filled pauses and punctuation and expanding colloquial
forms, potentially discarding cues useful for dialogue act
classification. We evaluate nine transcript conditions across six
classifiers (TF-IDF logistic regression, RoBERTa, BERT, ALBERT, GPT-2,
and BiLSTM) and three conversational corpora (Switchboard, ICSI MRDA,
and SLUE-HVB), supplemented by multi-seed experiments, normalization
controls, and real-ASR evaluations. Across five random seeds, heavy
normalization reduces Macro-F1 in all 15 neural model–corpus
combinations under the original preprocessing protocols. Removal using
the original filler and backchannel lexicon consistently degrades
performance on MRDA and SLUE-HVB but produces mixed effects on
Switchboard. Punctuation-aware TF-IDF experiments show that punctuation
removal reduces Macro-F1 by 0.036 on Switchboard and 0.078 on MRDA,
demonstrating that the original linear baseline’s apparent insensitivity
resulted from tokenization. Restricting filler removal to um, uh, and
erm preserves degradation on MRDA but largely eliminates the SLUE-HVB
effect, where the original lexicon removes class-defining backchannel
expressions. These findings demonstrate that normalization effects
depend on preprocessing definitions, feature extraction, and
conversational context, supporting task-dependent rather than
indiscriminate transcript normalization.

###### Index Terms: 

automatic speech recognition, transcript normalization, dialogue act
classification, disfluency, filled pauses

## I Introduction

ASR transcripts are commonly normalized by dropping filled pauses,
removing punctuation, and standardizing colloquial forms. These changes
improve readability, but they can also erase signals that help manage
turns and organize discourse \[1, 2, 3\]. Although disfluencies and
recognition errors have been studied, how normalization itself affects
dialogue act classification across models and interaction types is still
not well characterized.

We investigate nine transcript conditions across six classifiers (TF-IDF
logistic regression, RoBERTa, BERT, ALBERT, GPT-2, and BiLSTM) and three
corpora (Switchboard, ICSI MRDA, and SLUE-HVB), yielding 18 model–corpus
combinations. The main experiments transform reference transcripts,
while separate analyses examine recognition errors in real ASR output.
We also assess tokenization, lexicon sensitivity, and random-seed
variability.

Our main contributions are:

1.  1.  A multi-model, cross-corpus evaluation showing consistent Macro-F1
        degradation under heavy normalization across five random seeds, with
        more variable effects from filler removal.
2.  2.  A punctuation-aware TF-IDF analysis demonstrating that the apparent
        insensitivity of linear classifiers to punctuation removal results
        from tokenization rather than model architecture alone.
3.  3.  A restricted filler-lexicon analysis showing that degradation
        persists on MRDA but largely disappears on SLUE-HVB when
        class-defining backchannel tokens are preserved.
4.  4.  Cluster-level statistical analyses, real-ASR evaluations, and
        selective normalization experiments assessing the robustness and
        practical implications of transcript preprocessing.

Our findings show that normalization effects depend on the linguistic
cues removed, feature extraction, and conversational setting, supporting
task-dependent rather than indiscriminate transcript preprocessing.

## II Related Work

Disfluencies, filled pauses, and backchannels convey information about
hesitation, turn management, and discourse structure \[1, 2, 4, 5, 3\].
Removing these expressions during transcript normalization may therefore
eliminate cues useful for downstream pragmatic inference.

Prior studies have investigated ASR recognition errors and configurable
transcription-noise evaluation \[6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11\]. However,
recognition errors differ from deliberate transcript normalization,
which can alter linguistic cues even when transcription is accurate.

Dialogue act recognition research has examined lexical and
conversational cues \[12, 13, 14\]. Zelasko et al. \[15\] investigated
the role of punctuation in transformer-based dialogue act recognition
and segmentation. Prior research has compared lightweight and
transformer-based architectures for speech emotion classification
through systematic ablation studies \[16\].

Disfluency studies establish the communicative role of hesitations and
backchannels, whereas ASR-robustness work examines recognition errors
and Zelasko et al. focus on punctuation in dialogue-act recognition.
These studies do not jointly isolate deliberate filler/backchannel
removal, punctuation stripping, and colloquial expansion for
dialogue-act classification across model families and conversational
registers. Our controlled text ablations address this gap with nine
conditions, six classifiers, and three corpora, supplemented by
tokenizer and lexicon controls, multi-seed evaluation, and real-ASR
experiments.

## III Experimental Setup

Datasets. We evaluate three conversational corpora: Switchboard \[17\],
ICSI MRDA \[18, 19\], and SLUE-HVB \[20\], representing telephone
conversations, multi-party meetings, and scripted call-center dialogues,
respectively. SLUE-HVB is multi-label; approximately 40% of utterances
have multiple tags, and we use the release’s first tag as the primary
class. Table I summarizes the datasets after removing labels with fewer
than 50 instances in combined train–test counts. Switchboard uses a
conversation-level 80/20 split, while MRDA and HVB use their
release-defined splits. The MRDA release describes its canonical
partition as meeting-disjoint, although the reformatted records do not
expose meeting identifiers.

Models. We evaluate TF-IDF with logistic regression (TF-IDF+LR),
RoBERTa-base \[21\], BERT-base-cased \[22\], ALBERT-base-v2 \[23\],
GPT-2 \[24\], and BiLSTM \[25\]. TF-IDF+LR uses unigram/bigram features
($`\mathrm{min\_df}=3`$, $`\mathrm{max\_df}=0.95`$) and class-balanced
logistic regression. Transformers are fine-tuned for three epochs
(learning rate $`2\times 10^{-5}`$, batch size 16, maximum length 96).
BiLSTM uses 200-dimensional embeddings, 128 hidden units, and 15
training epochs. Neural experiments use five seeds while TF-IDF+LR is
deterministic. We additionally evaluate a punctuation-aware,
case-preserving TF-IDF tokenizer to examine punctuation discarded by the
default tokenizer.

Normalization Conditions. We evaluate nine conditions: gold_text
(original transcripts), norm_colloq_only (colloquial expansion),
rm_punct_only (punctuation removal), rm_discourse_only (removal of
like/well), gold_alpha_text (corpus-specific alphanumeric form),
rm_fillers_only (filler/backchannel lexicon removal), clean_light
(lowercasing and removal of non-alphanumeric characters except
apostrophes), clean_heavy (light cleaning, filler removal, and
colloquial expansion), and asr_noise (synthetic recognition errors).

Synthetic ASR noise applies token deletion (8%), substitution (8%), and
insertion (4%). Each main-grid condition is applied to both training and
test transcripts.

The original filler lexicon contains um, uh, erm, mm, hmm, mhm, uhhuh,
uh-huh, and huh. The restricted lexicon retains only um, uh, and erm.

An additional pragmatic_norm control applies lowercasing and colloquial
expansion while preserving fillers, backchannels, and punctuation. This
transformation leaves MRDA unchanged; its reported neural comparisons
are single-seed supplementary controls.

Normalization implementations have corpus-specific differences.
Switchboard’s original clean_heavy additionally removes like and
youknow, while its gold_alpha_text uses ConvoKit’s provided field, which
also removes clitics. MRDA and HVB instead use regex-based alphanumeric
filtering. Colloquial normalization changes no Switchboard or MRDA
utterances, while punctuation removal and light cleaning leave HVB
unchanged.

As a separate single-seed sensitivity test, we evaluate a harmonized
strict heavy-normalization condition applying lowercasing, punctuation
removal, colloquial expansion, and deletion of only um, uh, and erm
across all three corpora.

Real-ASR Evaluation. We evaluate Whisper-base.en \[26\] under two
protocols. On SLUE-HVB, models are trained and tested on ASR transcripts
(17.7%/18.3% train/test WER) and compared with gold-trained/gold-tested
models. On MRDA, gold-trained models are compared on gold and ASR test
transcripts from a separately reconstructed 60/15 meeting-disjoint split
(41.0% WER), combining recognition errors with train–test transcription
mismatch. Switchboard uses synthetic ASR noise because its audio
requires an LDC license.

Evaluation. Macro-F1 is the primary evaluation metric, assigning equal
weight to each dialogue-act class. Primary neural results are summarized
using mean and standard deviation across five seeds. At the primary
seed, paired Macro-F1 differences are assessed using 2,000
cluster-bootstrap resamples, with conversations as clusters for
Switchboard and calls for HVB. McNemar’s test assesses paired
utterance-level correctness rather than Macro-F1, with Bonferroni
correction across 36 comparisons; 21 remain significant at
$`\alpha=0.05/36`$. Because utterances within conversations are
dependent, McNemar results are supplementary to cluster-level
uncertainty estimates. Individual $`p`$-values and complete
condition-level outputs are included in the public repository.

Implementation scripts and supplementary materials are provided in a
public project repository.¹¹ 1
[https://github.com/luckymaduabuchi/asr-normalization-dialogue-acts](https://github.com/luckymaduabuchi/asr-normalization-dialogue-acts)

### IV-A Multi-Seed Normalization Effects

Table II reports selected primary conditions across five seeds for the
neural models and a deterministic TF-IDF+LR baseline. Under the original
preprocessing protocols, heavy normalization reduces Macro-F1 in all 75
paired seed–model–corpus comparisons. Filler removal also reduces
Macro-F1 in all 50 neural comparisons on MRDA and SLUE-HVB but produces
mixed effects on Switchboard. For example, MRDA RoBERTa decreases from
0.593 to 0.550 after filler removal and to 0.485 under heavy
normalization. In contrast, Switchboard BERT improves from 0.440 to
0.449 after filler removal but falls to 0.387 under heavy normalization.
Thus, heavy normalization produces more consistent degradation than
filler removal alone.

The complete primary-seed nine-condition grid is available in the
repository. Across its 18 model–corpus combinations, synthetic ASR noise
degrades all 18 ($`\Delta=0.033`$–$`0.225`$). Punctuation removal and
light cleaning each degrade 11 combinations and leave seven unchanged
($`\Delta=0`$–$`0.084`$ for each operation). Colloquial expansion and
discourse-marker removal have mixed, smaller effects
($`|\Delta|\leq 0.011`$); alphanumeric filtering is model-sensitive
($`\Delta=-0.005`$–$`0.209`$). The punctuation-aware control below
explains the default TF-IDF result.

### IV-B Punctuation and Filler-Lexicon Controls

The original TF-IDF+LR baseline appeared insensitive to punctuation
removal because its default tokenizer discarded punctuation before
feature extraction. Using a punctuation-aware, case-preserving
tokenizer, punctuation removal reduces Macro-F1 by 0.036 on Switchboard
and 0.078 on MRDA (Table III). Therefore, the original near-neutral
effect was a tokenization artifact rather than an intrinsic property of
linear classifiers. SLUE-HVB remains unaffected because its transcripts
lack punctuation.

Table IV reports results under an identical strict heavy-normalization
function. All six models exhibit Macro-F1 degradation on Switchboard and
MRDA, whereas five of six SLUE-HVB models change by at most 0.015. The
exception is GPT-2, which improves by 0.049 from an unusually low gold
baseline. All strict-control results use the primary seed.

Separately, omitting Switchboard’s extra like/youknow removal while
retaining the nine-word lexicon changes the normalized text for 17.7% of
utterances. TF-IDF+LR Macro-F1 changes from 0.2933 to 0.2954;
corresponding neural reruns are unavailable.

At the primary seed, pragmatic_norm Macro-F1 remains within
$`\pm 0.013`$ of gold across 12 Switchboard and SLUE-HVB model
combinations. It outperforms heavy normalization in 11 of 12
comparisons, suggesting that mild standardization may preserve
performance when discourse cues remain.

### IV-C Statistical and Class-Level Analysis

Five-seed standard deviations summarize training variability. Paired
cluster-bootstrap confidence intervals quantify test-cluster sampling
uncertainty at primary seed, not uncertainty in five-seed means.
Throughout, positive $`\Delta`$ denotes the Macro-F1 decrease relative
to gold.

At the primary seed, cluster-bootstrap 95% confidence intervals for
heavy-normalization Macro-F1 differences exclude zero for all five
neural models on Switchboard and four of five on SLUE-HVB.
Filler-removal intervals exclude zero for only ALBERT on Switchboard and
four neural models on SLUE-HVB. For TF-IDF+LR on Switchboard,
filler-removal and heavy-normalization differences are 0.011
$`[0.003,0.020]`$ and 0.017 $`[0.009,0.027]`$, respectively. These are
nominal 95% intervals without multiplicity adjustment; an F1 decrease
alone does not establish statistical significance. The reformatted
primary MRDA records lack meeting identifiers. On a separate exploratory
60/15 meeting-disjoint reconstruction, heavy- normalization intervals
exclude zero for four of five neural models, while filler-only effects
are split-sensitive.

Utterance-level McNemar tests provide an additional paired-correctness
comparison but are not tests of Macro-F1 and do not adjust for
conversation clustering; cluster-bootstrap intervals therefore remain
the primary uncertainty evidence.

Table V shows different class-level effects across corpora. On
Switchboard, backchannel precision decreases from 0.558 to 0.436, with
an F1 reduction of 0.068. On MRDA, recall decreases for floor holders
(fh) and holds before answers or agreements (h), indicating difficulty
recognizing turn-management dialogue acts. In contrast, SLUE-HVB
backchannel recall remains at 0.750, while precision decreases from
0.353 to 0.092, reducing F1 from 0.480 to 0.163. Because Macro-F1
weights this low-support class equally with the other 15 classes, its
large F1 change warrants cautious interpretation. The restricted-lexicon
analysis indicates that much of this degradation results from deleting
class-defining backchannel expressions.

### IV-D Real-ASR Evaluation

Whisper-base.en is evaluated under two protocols. On SLUE-HVB, models
are trained and tested on Whisper transcripts (17.7%/18.3% train/test
WER). Five models exhibit lower Macro-F1, whereas GPT-2 improves from
0.6674 to 0.6765 at primary seed. This isolated result does not
establish an ASR benefit, especially given its unusually low baseline.

On MRDA, models trained on gold transcripts are evaluated on Whisper
transcripts from 15 held-out meetings (WER: 41.0%). All six models
degrade, with Macro-F1 decreases ranging from 0.1366 to 0.2822. Unlike
SLUE-HVB, this experiment combines recognition errors with train–test
transcription mismatch; the effect sizes are therefore not directly
comparable.

## V Discussion and Conclusion

Our findings show that transcript normalization affects dialogue act
classification differently across models, corpora, and preprocessing
operations. Under the original protocols, heavy normalization reduces
Macro-F1 across all five seeds for every neural model–corpus
combination, whereas filler removal produces mixed effects on
Switchboard but consistent degradation on MRDA and SLUE-HVB.

Interpretation of normalization effects. The punctuation-aware TF-IDF
control shows that punctuation is predictive when the feature extractor
preserves it. The default TF-IDF baseline appeared insensitive because
its tokenizer discarded punctuation. Filler-lexicon controls reveal a
different mechanism: on MRDA, degradation persists when removal is
restricted to um, uh, and erm, suggesting that these expressions provide
useful classification cues. On SLUE-HVB, most degradation disappears
under the restricted lexicon because the original inventory removes
class-defining backchannel expressions. Thus, not all performance losses
reflect the same type of information removal.

Robustness and practical implications. The five-seed experiments show
that heavy-normalization degradation is more consistent than
filler-removal effects. Under harmonized strict normalization,
degradation persists across all six classifiers on Switchboard and MRDA
but is largely absent on SLUE-HVB, demonstrating sensitivity to corpus
characteristics and lexicon definitions. Single-seed
pragmatic-normalization controls further suggest that modest text
standardization can retain dialogue-act performance when backchannels
and punctuation are preserved.

Limitations. The study is limited to three English-language corpora.
SLUE-HVB findings concern a first-tag, single-label reduction and do not
evaluate its full multi-label dialogue-act task. The original
Switchboard heavy-normalization implementation additionally removes like
and youknow, and harmonized lexicon experiments use a single seed. On
Switchboard, spaced backchannel expressions such as Uh - huh are
partially affected by the strict lexicon, which removes uh but retains
huh. Thus, identical normalization rules do not necessarily remove
equivalent linguistic cues across corpora. According to the
release-defined canonical split, the primary MRDA partition is
meeting-disjoint but not speaker-disjoint; because meeting identifiers
are absent from the reformatted records, meeting-level confidence
intervals are unavailable for that primary split. The alternative MRDA
neural comparisons use different training scripts, hyperparameters, and
text formatting, preventing controlled attribution of their differences
to normalization. Real-ASR evaluations use different protocols: matched
ASR training/testing on SLUE-HVB and gold-trained/ASR-tested models on
MRDA. The pragmatics-preserving control uses reference transcripts and
does not recover cues already missing from real ASR output. All reported
BiLSTM results use the corrected empty-input pooling implementation.

Overall, aggressive transcript normalization can degrade dialogue act
classification, but the magnitude and mechanism depend on feature
extraction, lexicon design, and conversational setting. These findings
support selective, task-dependent normalization rather than
indiscriminate transcript cleaning. Future work will examine multi-seed
harmonized normalization, matched ASR training on MRDA, and additional
conversational domains.
