# Introduction

Research in the <span acronym-label="SE" acronym-form="singular+short">SE</span> domain has experienced considerable progress in recent years, largely attributed to <span acronym-label="DNN" acronym-form="singular+short">DNN</span>-based approaches that promise significant improvements in speech quality and intelligibility even under challenging acoustic conditions. Although discriminative <span acronym-label="DNN" acronym-form="singular+short">DNN</span>-based methods remain widely used and have already been deployed in numerous practical applications on consumer devices, researchers in the <span acronym-label="SE" acronym-form="singular+short">SE</span> domain are increasingly investigating generative methods for this task . This shift is largely motivated by the need to mitigate speech distortions introduced in many discriminative methods and to achieve superior performance in very low-SNR scenarios .

Generative methods, such as <span acronym-label="GAN" acronym-form="singular+short">GAN</span>- and diffusion-type approaches have been shown to achieve <span acronym-label="SOTA" acronym-form="singular+short">SOTA</span> performance in various <span acronym-label="SE" acronym-form="singular+short">SE</span> tasks . Although showing promising performance, the majority of generative methods in the literature rely on very large <span acronym-label="DNN" acronym-form="singular+short">DNN</span> models, which impose significant computational constraints for practical deployment. Moreover, most <span acronym-label="CFM" acronym-form="singular+short">CFM</span>- and diffusion-based approaches rely on an iterative generation process, further increasing the overall computational complexity. As a result, it becomes challenging to systematically evaluate the perceptual performance gains in relation to the associated computational cost when comparing discriminative and generative methods. Such a comparison requires training and evaluating a large set of models while carefully evaluating the trade-offs between performance and computational complexity. As another critical aspect, many existing approaches are trained and evaluated on small and homogeneous datasets (e.g., VCTK dataset , EARS dataset with WHAM noises ) that lack diversity in noise types and <span acronym-label="SNR" acronym-form="singular+short">SNR</span> conditions; factors that are common in real-world applications . We argue that properly assessing the true benefits of these different approaches requires a comprehensive comparative study that considers multiple evaluation aspects.

In , the performance of different <span acronym-label="SOTA" acronym-form="singular+short">SOTA</span> discriminative methods has been evaluated in low <span acronym-label="SNR" acronym-form="singular+short">SNR</span> scenarios. Several studies also investigate the impact of different training objectives for <span acronym-label="SE" acronym-form="singular+short">SE</span> . However, to the best of our knowledge, no study in the literature comprehensively considers a wide range of discriminative and generative methods, diverse model architectures, and varied training and evaluation datasets. Furthermore, existing studies do not jointly evaluate performance using both classical and <span acronym-label="DNN" acronym-form="singular+short">DNN</span>-based metrics alongside the complexity–performance trade-off. In this work, we present a comprehensive evaluation of these methods for noise reduction tasks. Our specific contributions are as follows:

- We trained a large set of discriminative and generative models on diverse datasets to evaluate their performance under various noise conditions, including low-<span acronym-label="SNR" acronym-form="singular+short">SNR</span> and mismatched training scenarios.

- We analyze the convergence characteristics of different methods and investigate the impact of training data volume on performance, as well as the hallucination tendencies of these methods.

- We examine the complexity–performance trade-off between different approaches and provide insights into when each method is most suitable for practical use.

# Methods

Let $`p(\mathbf{x}_0 \mid \mathbf{y})`$ denote the conditional distribution of clean speech $`\mathbf{x}_0 \in \mathbb{R}^L`$ given a noisy signal $`\mathbf{y}\in \mathbb{R}^L`$, where $`L`$ is the length in samples in the time domain. In conditional generative <span acronym-label="SE" acronym-form="singular+short">SE</span>, the objective is to learn a transformation of samples from a source distribution such that the transformed samples follow the target clean speech distribution using information from $`\mathbf{y}`$. For brevity, we assume that all transformations preserve the signal dimensions and, hence, do not mention their dimensions in the remainder of this paper.

## Diffusion-Type Models

**Score Matching:** Diffusion models  typically define a stochastic transform from clean data into Gaussian noise via a forward <span acronym-label="SDE" acronym-form="singular+short">SDE</span>. In the <span acronym-label="SE" acronym-form="singular+short">SE</span> context (e.g., SGMSE+ ), the diffusion kernel $`q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{y})`$ is used to perturb the clean speech signal toward the noisy signal $`\mathbf{y}`$ for different time steps $`t \in [0, T]`$. Here, $`\mathbf{x}_t`$ denotes the noisy signal at timestep $`t`$ according to an additive Gaussian noise schedule along the path to $`\mathbf{y}`$ defined by the forward <span acronym-label="SDE" acronym-form="singular+short">SDE</span> . A score network $`\mathbf{S}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{y}, t)`$, with parameters $`\theta`$, is trained to approximate the conditional score function $`\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t} \log p_t(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{y})`$ by minimizing the denoising score-matching objective

```math
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diff}} =
\mathbb{E}_{t, \mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{y}, \boldsymbol{\epsilon}}
\left[
\left\| \boldsymbol{\epsilon} - \mathbf{S}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{y}, t) \right\|^2_2
\right],
\quad \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I}).
\label{eq:loss_diffusion}
\end{equation}
```

Here, $`\boldsymbol{\epsilon}`$ denotes the diffusion noise at diffusion time step $`t`$, where $`t`$ is sampled from a uniform distribution $`t \sim \mathcal{U}(0, T)`$. While the network $`\mathbf{S}_\theta`$ estimates diffusion noise $`\boldsymbol{\epsilon}`$, the loss <a href="#eq:loss_diffusion" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:loss_diffusion">[eq:loss_diffusion]</a> is mathematically equivalent to score function estimation up to a time-dependent scaling factor. By solving the reverse-time <span acronym-label="SDE" acronym-form="singular+short">SDE</span>, the model iteratively refines the noisy observation into a clean speech estimate. In this work, we consider SGMSE+  and BBED  as diffusion models. In addition, the score-matching objective can be reformulated by directly predicting the initial clean speech signal $`\mathbf{x}_0`$ at each diffusion step $`t`$ . For this formulation, we consider GALDSE .

**Conditional Flow Matching (CFM):** <span acronym-label="CFM" acronym-form="singular+short">CFM</span> frameworks, such as FlowSE , model <span acronym-label="SE" acronym-form="singular+short">SE</span> via sample transport by a (deterministic) velocity field $`\mathbf{v}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{y}, t)`$. These approaches define a probability path $`\{p_t(\mathbf{x})\}_{t \in [0,1]}`$ connecting the noisy signal $`\mathbf{y}`$ (at $`t=1`$) to the clean target $`\mathbf{x}_0`$ (at $`t=0`$). The model of the velocity field is trained to match a conditional target vector field $`\mathbf{u}_t(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{y})`$

```math
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CFM}} =
\mathbb{E}_{t, \mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{y}}\left[\left\|\mathbf{v}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{y}, t)
-\mathbf{u}_t(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{y})\right\|^2_2\right].
\end{equation}
```

For optimal transport (OT) paths, $`\mathbf{u}_t`$ reduces to the constant velocity $`\mathbf{x}_0 - \mathbf{y}`$, enabling efficient deterministic transport along a straight line via an <span acronym-label="ODE" acronym-form="singular+short">ODE</span>. In this work, we consider FlowSE  for this formulation.

**Consistency Models:** Consistency models  enforce a trajectory-invariance property to eliminate the need for iterative refinement in <span acronym-label="SDE" acronym-form="singular+short">SDE</span> or <span acronym-label="ODE" acronym-form="singular+short">ODE</span> solvers. A consistency function $`f_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{y}, t)`$ is trained to map any point $`\mathbf{x}_t`$ on a trajectory directly to its origin $`\mathbf{x}_0`$. The objective enforces self-consistency across all time steps $`t \in [0, T]`$

```math
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cons}} =
\mathbb{E}_{n, \mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{y}}
\left[\left\|f_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t_n}, \mathbf{y}, t_{n})-f_{\theta^-}(\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}}, \mathbf{y}, t_{n-1})
\right\|^2_2\right].
\end{equation}
```

where $`n`$ is sampled uniformly from $`\{1, \dots, N\}`$, with $`N`$ denoting the total number of discrete time steps, $`\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}}`$ and $`\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}}`$ denote adjacent points on the trajectory and $`\theta^-`$ is obtained by an exponential moving average (EMA) of the parameters $`\theta`$. This approach enables single-step <span acronym-label="SE" acronym-form="singular+short">SE</span>. As a representative method, we consider SEBridge .

## Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)

Conditional <span acronym-label="GAN" acronym-form="singular+short">GAN</span>s, such as DisCoGAN and NoCoGAN (where only the noisy signal $`\mathbf{y}`$ is used for conditioning) , learn a mapping $`G_\theta(\mathbf{y})`$ that transforms a noisy signal $`\mathbf{y}`$ directly into an estimate of its clean version. In this framework, the generator $`G_\theta`$ is trained through a min-max optimization with a discriminator $`D_\phi`$. The training objective is defined as

```math
\begin{equation}
\min_{\theta} \max_{\phi} \; \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{y}}
\big[ \mathcal{L}_D(\phi, \mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{y}) \big]+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}}\big[ \mathcal{L}_G(\phi, \theta, \mathbf{y}) \big].
\end{equation}
```

Here, $`\mathcal{L}_G`$ and $`\mathcal{L}_D`$ denote the generator and discriminator loss functions, respectively. This approach enables direct single-step <span acronym-label="SE" acronym-form="singular+short">SE</span> without relying on any iterative generation process. In this work, we trained NoCoGAN and DisCoGAN , as well as CMGAN . We also train the backbone network NCSN++ , which is used in previously discussed diffusion-type methods, with a <span acronym-label="GAN" acronym-form="singular+short">GAN</span> objective as described in ; we refer to this variant as NCSN++ (GAN).

## Discriminative Models

In contrast to generative paradigms, discriminative models approach <span acronym-label="SE" acronym-form="singular+short">SE</span> as a regression problem. These models focus on learning a deterministic mapping $`\mathbf{x}_0 \approx \mathcal{F}_\theta(\mathbf{y})`$ by minimizing a signal- or mask-level loss function $`\ell`$ (e.g., SI-SNR or mean-squared error)

```math
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{disc}} =
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{y}}
\left[
\ell\!\left(\mathbf{x}_0, \mathcal{F}_\theta(\mathbf{y})\right)
\right].
\end{equation}
```

In this work, we consider DCCRN  and GCRN . We also train NoCoGAN and NCSN++ discriminatively using the reconstruction loss described in ; we refer to these variants as NoCoGAN (D) and NCSN++ (D).

# Experiments

## Implementation details

**Training and Evaluation Datasets:** We created training data using the Interspeech 2020 DNS Challenge dataset  by mixing clean speech with noise at random <span acronym-label="SNR" acronym-form="singular+short">SNR</span>s within $`[-25, 0]\, \mathrm{dB}`$ for a low-<span acronym-label="SNR" acronym-form="singular+short">SNR</span> dataset and within $`[-5, 30]\, \mathrm{dB}`$ for a high-<span acronym-label="SNR" acronym-form="singular+short">SNR</span> dataset, each comprising approximately $`1000`$ hours of data, following .

For evaluation, we use the DNS Challenge non-reverberant test set  for high-<span acronym-label="SNR" acronym-form="singular+short">SNR</span> scenarios, which contains noises from $`12`$ VoIP-relevant categories at <span acronym-label="SNR" acronym-form="singular+short">SNR</span>s in $`[0, 25]\, \mathrm{dB}`$. For low-<span acronym-label="SNR" acronym-form="singular+short">SNR</span> scenarios, we use the dataset from , which comprises $`1200`$ samples, each lasting $`10`$ s. The dataset is divided into four <span acronym-label="SNR" acronym-form="singular+short">SNR</span> groups, $`[-15, -12]`$, $`[-11, -8]`$, $`[-7, -4]`$, and $`[-3, 0]\, \mathrm{dB}`$, and contains $`20`$ different stationary and non-stationary noise types.

**Evaluation Metrics and Criteria:** We evaluate the different methods using both intrusive and non-intrusive metrics. Specifically, we use PESQ , frequency-weighted segmental SNR (FwSegSNR) , SI-SDR , DNSMOS , and SCOREQ (reference-based) . To evaluate hallucination effects, we report <span acronym-label="WER" acronym-form="singular+short">WER</span> and <span acronym-label="CER" acronym-form="singular+short">CER</span> using the Whisper (base) ASR system  with the JiWER toolkit , along with the Levenshtein phoneme similarity (LPS) . For computational complexity, we report giga multiply–accumulate operations per second (GMACs) and the number of model parameters. The best candidate models among different epoch-wise checkpoints for each method are selected based on PESQ and SI-SDR performance on the validation set.

## Results and Discussion

**High- and Low-SNR scenarios:** In Tab. <a href="#tab:DNSnoreverb" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="tab:DNSnoreverb">[tab:DNSnoreverb]</a>, we present the results for matched high-<span acronym-label="SNR" acronym-form="singular+short">SNR</span> scenarios on the DNS Challenge non-reverberant test set. We observe that <span acronym-label="GAN" acronym-form="singular+short">GAN</span>-based methods outperform both diffusion and discriminative methods in terms of PESQ, SI-SDR and SCOREQ improvement. The discriminative methods achieve PESQ scores comparable to <span acronym-label="GAN" acronym-form="singular+short">GAN</span>-based approaches; however, they lag behind in other objective metrics. In terms of the non-intrusive DNN-based metric DNSMOS, diffusion-based methods slightly outperform other approaches, potentially indicating stronger generative capabilities compared to <span acronym-label="GAN" acronym-form="plural+short">GANs</span> and discriminative methods .

Based on the results from high-<span acronym-label="SNR" acronym-form="singular+short">SNR</span> matched scenarios, we trained the best-performing (and distinct) models with the low-<span acronym-label="SNR" acronym-form="singular+short">SNR</span> dataset, as described above. However, in low-<span acronym-label="SNR" acronym-form="singular+short">SNR</span> scenarios, <span acronym-label="GAN" acronym-form="singular+short">GAN</span>-based methods clearly outperform discriminative and diffusion-based methods under matched and mismatched conditions, as shown in Tabs. <a href="#tab:lowsnrmatched" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="tab:lowsnrmatched">[tab:lowsnrmatched]</a> and <a href="#tab:lowsnrmismatched" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="tab:lowsnrmismatched">[tab:lowsnrmismatched]</a>. In matched scenarios, diffusion-based methods achieve comparable or better SI-SDR performance to GAN-based methods (e.g., FlowSE achieves SI-SDR improvements of $`16.80`$, $`15.59`$, and $`13.63`$ for the <span acronym-label="SNR" acronym-form="singular+short">SNR</span> groups $`[-11, -8]`$, $`[-7, -4]`$, and $`[-3, 0]`$, respectively). However, they perform poorly in terms of PESQ and FwSegSNR, which may indicate that iterative refinement methods are less suitable for extremely low-<span acronym-label="SNR" acronym-form="singular+short">SNR</span> conditions, potentially due to over-denoising or oscillations while converging toward producing clean speech outputs. It is important to note that NCSN++ (GAN) (i.e., the model architecture used in diffusion-based methods but trained with a GAN objective) performs on par with or better than DisCoGAN in both matched and mismatched scenarios. In low-<span acronym-label="SNR" acronym-form="singular+short">SNR</span> scenarios, NCSN++ (GAN) outperforms other methods by a clear margin in terms of FwSegSNR ($`9.90`$, $`10.53`$, and $`11.12`$) and SI-SDR ($`16.03`$, $`15.13`$, and $`13.41`$ dB) improvement across <span acronym-label="SNR" acronym-form="singular+short">SNR</span> groups $`[-11, -8]`$, $`[-7, -4]`$, and $`[-3, 0]`$, in both matched and mismatched scenarios, respectively.

**Training Convergence and Dataset:** In Fig. <a href="#fig:datasteps" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="fig:datasteps">1</a>, we analyze the training convergence characteristics of different methods using the same NCSN++ backbone network in terms of PESQ and SI-SDR improvement. We observe that the discriminative model achieves peak performance at around $`200`$k steps and shows minimal improvement up to $`600`$k steps. The <span acronym-label="GAN" acronym-form="singular+short">GAN</span>-based NCSN++ (GAN) model reaches peak performance at approximately $`250`$k steps; however, its performance exhibits noticeable oscillations before stabilizing around $`400`$k steps. This instability in GAN training can be attributed to the competing optimization of the generator and discriminator. In contrast, the diffusion-based BBED model demonstrates a more stable training behavior, with gradual performance improvements. It required around $`300`$k steps to achieve SI-SDR performance comparable to the discriminative NCSN++ (D) model and approximately $`400`$k steps to approach the performance of the <span acronym-label="GAN" acronym-form="singular+short">GAN</span>-based method. However, it did not achieve PESQ improvements comparable to discriminative or <span acronym-label="GAN" acronym-form="singular+short">GAN</span>-based approaches.

To assess the impact of volume of training data, we created four subsets of the high-<span acronym-label="SNR" acronym-form="singular+short">SNR</span> dataset with durations of $`50`$, $`100`$, $`200`$, and $`500`$ hours by randomly sampling the training data. As shown in Fig. <a href="#fig:datahours" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="fig:datahours">2</a>, NCSN++ (GAN) achieves peak performance in terms of SI-SDR with only $`50`$ hours of training data. In contrast, the diffusion-based BBED method is significantly more sensitive to data scale, requiring at least $`200`$ hours of data to achieve comparable results to its peak performance. These results suggest that <span acronym-label="GAN" acronym-form="singular+short">GAN</span>-based approaches are more data-efficient and converge faster for <span acronym-label="SE" acronym-form="singular+short">SE</span> tasks.

**Hallucination Effects:** In Tab. <a href="#tab:wer_lps_lsd_with_noisy" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="tab:wer_lps_lsd_with_noisy">[tab:wer_lps_lsd_with_noisy]</a>, we report <span acronym-label="WER" acronym-form="singular+short">WER</span>, <span acronym-label="CER" acronym-form="singular+short">CER</span>, and LPS to assess the hallucination tendencies of generative methods. All methods consistently improve these metrics, indicating that they appear to introduce only limited hallucination effects within the evaluated SNR ranges. GAN-based methods appear to outperform diffusion-based methods in these metrics. In particular, NCSN++ (GAN) and DisCoGAN achieve higher phoneme similarity scores of $`85\%`$ and $`92\%`$ in the SNR groups $`[-7, -4]`$ and $`[-3, 0]`$, respectively. However, our inspections also revealed that in very low <span acronym-label="SNR" acronym-form="singular+short">SNR</span> scenarios (e.g., below $`-7`$ dB), these metrics degrade significantly. In this <span acronym-label="SNR" acronym-form="singular+short">SNR</span> range, we also observed spurious spectral content in the enhanced spectrograms of the generative methods which was not present in the original clean speech signal. These results suggest that conditional generative models may rely heavily on the noisy input signal $`\mathbf{y}`$, leading to limited hallucination under moderate to high <span acronym-label="SNR" acronym-form="singular+short">SNR</span> matched conditions; however, in very low <span acronym-label="SNR" acronym-form="singular+short">SNR</span> scenarios, where the noisy signal $`\mathbf{y}`$ may be almost entirely masked by noise, the generative methods tend to hallucinate more .

**Complexity-Performance Tradeoff:** In Fig. <a href="#fig:lis2" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="fig:lis2">3</a>, we show the complexity of different <span acronym-label="SE" acronym-form="singular+short">SE</span> methods in terms of GMACs and the number of parameters. We observe that most of the evaluated diffusion-based methods are significantly more complex, both in terms of GMACs and parameter count. SGMSE+ (30 steps) and SToRM (50 steps) are at least $`60-100\times`$ more computationally expensive in terms of GMACs than NCSN++ (GAN) and NoCoGAN, mainly due to the large number of network evaluations during the reverse diffusion process. FlowSE and SEBridge reduce the number of network evaluations to a single or a few steps (e.g., five); however, they still remain considerably complex due to the use of the computationally expensive NCSN++ backbone. In contrast, discriminative and GAN-based methods relying on single-step inference, show less computational complexity than diffusion-based methods. NCSN++, which is computationally more intensive than NoCoGAN and DisCoGAN, achieves better results in several objective metrics in different scenarios for both GAN-based and discriminative training methods, which indicates that higher model complexity, also scales to better objective performance for discriminative and GAN-based methods.

**Discussion:** Our experimental results suggest that, at least for <span acronym-label="SE" acronym-form="singular+short">SE</span> tasks, which benefit from the strong conditioning provided by the noisy signal $`\mathbf{y}`$, very large models or multiple network evaluations, as required in diffusion-type methods, are not always necessary. In high-SNR scenarios, the discriminative model remains competitive to generative approaches while showing significant benefits in computational efficiency . We hypothesize that generative methods, particularly diffusion-type approaches, may be more beneficial than discriminative methods for tasks that are inherently generative in nature, such as speech reconstruction/synthesis and bandwidth extension . Under the evaluated conditions, diffusion-based methods do not demonstrate clear advantages in terms of the complexity-performance trade-off.

# Conclusions

In this study, we show that, while generative methods improve <span acronym-label="SE" acronym-form="singular+short">SE</span> performance, particularly in low-SNR scenarios and mismatched conditions, the complexity–performance trade-off does not always justify the performance gains, especially for diffusion-based methods. Our analysis also shows that, for <span acronym-label="SE" acronym-form="singular+short">SE</span> tasks, discriminative and GAN-based methods result in faster training times and can achieve better efficiency in terms of training data. Future studies should focus on other <span acronym-label="SE" acronym-form="singular+short">SE</span>-related tasks and on the impact of different neural network architectures across training paradigms.
