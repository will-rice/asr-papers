###### Abstract.

Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe ranks first among open-weight models on the
Hugging Face Open ASR leaderboards. Serving it efficiently takes more
than fast operators: continuous batching changes the amount and mix of
work at every iteration, and the best fusion granularity changes with
it. We present a serving engine that, for every pass, chooses between
persistent megakernels and chains of fused kernels at crossovers
measured offline. On an H100 SXM GPU, throughput of our
accuracy-preserving serving peaks at 4,345$`\times`$ real time,
1.39$`\times`$ the peak of tuned vLLM, and is up to 4.39$`\times`$
faster at low concurrency; on B200 it peaks at 6,367$`\times`$,
1.64$`\times`$ vLLM at the highest concurrency we measured. Word error
rate (WER) is 1.91 to 1.93% against 1.88 to 1.93% for vLLM. Within a 400
ms budget for p95 end-to-end completion time, it serves 8$`\times`$ as
many concurrent requests. Choosing per pass beats either level alone:
23% higher throughput than always using megakernels at 128 concurrent
requests, and 9% higher than always using fused kernels at 8. Opt-in
switches, including greedy MoE layer skipping, add 13 to 16% throughput
on B200 for offline jobs that can accept higher WER.

## 1. Introduction

Speech transcription is a core operation in speech-to-speech (S2S)
systems and modern user-facing applications. Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe is
the leading open speech transcription model at the time of its release
in October 2026. It combines speech and speaker encoders with a hybrid
Mamba Transformer mixture-of-experts (MoE) based language model,
supports transcription in 40 languages and processes recordings up to
one hour long.

Although inference serving frameworks like vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023)
provide optimized kernels, continuous batching and speculative decoding,
vLLM’s existing Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe integration leaves significant
opportunities for optimization across operator boundaries.

Separate kernels for normalization, quantization, projections, expert
routing and output processing introduce intermediate memory traffic and
synchronization. Fusion can reduce these costs but constrain registers,
occupancy and compute efficiency. The challenge is to choose fusion
granularity as serving workload size changes.

We address this problem with a regime-aware serving design which reduces
p95 end-to-end completion time by 1.50 to 4.10$`\times`$ on H100 SXM,
1.45 to 2.19$`\times`$ on H100 NVL and 1.93 to 3.75$`\times`$ on B200
across 32 to 1,280 concurrent requests (Figure 1). Our approach combines
persistent megakernels, which execute a model region within one launch,
with specialized kernel chains that fuse selected operations while
retaining independent launch configurations. We select between these
implementations using measured behavior across token lengths. The
resulting native serving engine also processes prompt and verification
work together, adapts expert computation to routed populations and keeps
speculative drafting and token selection on the GPU.

Our major contributions include:

- •
  A design methodology for prefill-dominated, megakernel-receding
  architectures using regime-aware megakernels and kernel fusion.
- •
  An engine inside a production inference server that, for every pass,
  chooses between megakernels and fused kernels at measured crossovers.
- •
  Regime-aware kernels designed for Nemotron 3.5 Lightning apply to
  other models built on it.
- •
  Optimizations for applications that can tolerate a higher word error
  rate (WER), including greedy selection of MoE layers to skip within a
  WER budget.

## 2. Industrial context

As of 6 November 2026, it ranks first among open-weight models on the
English, multilingual and long-form Hugging Face Open ASR leaderboards
(Srivastav et al., 2025; Motamedi et al., 2026) (5.33%, 4.62% and 10.98%
WER) and first among 14 systems on VoiceArena’s speaker-attributed
transcription benchmark (Voice Arena, 2026).

### 2.1. Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe model

Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe (NVIDIA, 2026c) is NVIDIA’s language model for
transcription. One model, steered by a natural-language instruction,
produces verbatim or written-form, contextual, speaker-attributed and
rich transcripts, translates speech and identifies the spoken language
in 40 languages.

Its audio path is a Parallel Expert Encoder: a 32-layer Transformer ASR
encoder (d=1,280, 0.6B parameters) and a frozen Sortformer (Park et al., 2025) diarization model (0.1B) run side by side on 128-bin log-mel
features and the per-frame activities of up to eight speakers are added
to the ASR states through a fixed sinusoidal kernel. A linear adapter
projects the result to the LLM width (1,280 $`\rightarrow`$ 2,688). The
LLM backbone, Nemotron 3.5 Lightning 30B-A3B, has 52 layers: 23 Mamba-2
(Dao and Gu, 2024), 23 MoE layers with 128 routed experts (top-6) and a
shared expert and only 6 grouped-query attention (GQA) layers so 3.5B of
its 31.6B parameters are active per token. A multi-token prediction
(MTP) module (1.3B parameters) serves as the draft model for speculative
decoding and a CTC (Graves et al., 2006) adapter (50M parameters) on the
ASR encoder aligns words to audio. The complete model contains
approximately 34B parameters, with approximately 4.3B active parameters
including the audio-processing components (Figure 2).

Audio flows through parallel Sortformer and ASR encoders, speaker
fusion, the modality adapter, and the hybrid Mamba, MoE, and GQA
language model. MTP drafts tokens, and a CTC adapter aligns words to
audio.

### 2.2. Target workload and serving requirements

We target transcription at high concurrency. Our benchmark is NVIDIA’s
English Bias corpus (test split): 4,820 single-speaker recordings
totaling 15.36 hours (3.5 to 58.8 s, 11.5 s on average, 25 words per
transcript on average). The recordings are split evenly across four
domains (telecom and finance, technical, alphanumeric codes and numbers,
and general) and six US and UK accents, with an even gender split. Our
primary evaluation uses the full Bias corpus; we additionally evaluate
the complete LibriSpeech (Panayotov et al., 2015) test-clean and
test-other splits. Expert and layer rankings for the opt-in mode
(Section 7.1) come from a separate calibration set of 512 LibriSpeech
dev utterances.

We report end-to-end RTFx, the seconds of audio transcribed per second
over a whole pass including ramp-up and drain; latency per request
(p95); and WER and character error rate (CER). We measure on single
GPUs: H100 SXM (700 W), H100 NVL (400 W) and B200 (144 SMs, 1,000 W).
Every comparison is a matched run on the same GPU.

Online serving maximizes concurrent requests while keeping p95
end-to-end completion time below 400 ms, within a 1-second S2S budget
allocating 100 ms to TTS and 500 ms to the LLM. Offline serving
maximizes RTFx across batch sizes.

### 2.3. Deployment and use cases

Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe was released as open weights on Hugging Face on
28 October 2026 under the OpenMDW-1.1 license, with BF16, FP8 and NVFP4
checkpoints. vLLM, SGLang, Hugging Face Transformers and NeMo-Speech.cpp
support it, and inference providers including Baseten, DeepInfra and
Simplismart have hosted it since release day. NVIDIA’s reference service
is a Rust inference server with OpenAI-compatible routes in front of a
vLLM engine. It is packaged per GPU family, from Jetson Thor and DGX
Spark to H100/H200 and GB200/GB300. Our engine runs in process inside
that server through a C ABI with no Python in the serving path. It keeps
the same API, word timestamps and speaker labels, and has parity tests
against the vLLM reference. Our optimized serving engine will be
available in a future release of NVIDIA Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe NIM
(NVIDIA, 2026d).

One checkpoint serves two workloads. Offline, it transcribes
call-center, meeting and media archives of up to an hour per recording,
where transcription throughput (RTFx) decides deployment. Online, it is
the recognizer in voice agents and speech-to-speech systems, where a
short utterance must be transcribed within about 400 ms.

## 3. Regime-aware megakernel architecture

The vLLM baseline executes Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe at operator
granularity: each operator, or a small group fused by torch.compile, is
a separate kernel, launched by vLLM’s model runner or replayed from a
CUDA graph, and consecutive kernels pass intermediate results through
global memory. Our optimized engine replaces every component with one of
two fusion levels:

- •
  A megakernel runs the whole region in one persistent launch.
- •
  Fused kernels run the region as a short chain of custom kernels, each
  fusing several operators, as CUTLASS (NVIDIA, 2026b) GEMM epilogues
  and chained with programmatic dependent launch (PDL) (NVIDIA, 2026a).

### 3.1. Why is one megakernel not enough?

Prior work on megakernel composition (Chatterjee et al., 2026) found
that a single megakernel is rarely the fastest design. That work
describes each fused region by how its gain from a megakernel changes
with batch size. A megakernel-stable region gains at every size. A
megakernel-receding region gains up to a crossover and loses beyond it.

With in-flight batching, the batch changes at every iteration; we size
each region by the work of one pass: tokens per encoder pass or rows per
decoder step.

For a region which recedes beyond a crossover point, the engine keeps
both a megakernel and a conventional implementation and switches between
them. Conventional means one kernel launch per operation inside our
engine, with CUTLASS or cuBLASLt GEMMs and our custom CUDA kernels.

Table 1 lists the result. Two megakernels are stable and always run.
Five are receding: three switch at runtime and two are selected by the
serving configuration.

### 3.2. Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe is a prefill-dominated megakernel-receding architecture

The five megakernels, classified as megakernel-receding, dominate the
end-to-end computation time, which makes the Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe
model a megakernel-receding architecture.

The thresholds were measured offline and are applied from the pass size
alone. At B128 most passes are large, so the encoder and MoE take the
conventional path, while the GQA and draft megakernels run on every
pass. At low load, and while the batch fills or drains, the megakernels
take over.

Neither level alone matches this choice (Table 2). Megakernels slow down
large passes (7.66 against 6.05 ms per encoder pass at 1,024 tokens),
and fused kernels slow down small ones (549 launches instead of four per
encoder pass). A megakernel-only design would also need separate prompt
steps, because the prefill megakernel accepts only prompt-only batches,
so each iteration would stream the 29.8 GB of FP8 expert weights twice.

The workload is prefill-dominated at serving loads. Prefill rows consume
24.1% to 46.2% of end-to-end time across batch sizes 1 to 512. Decode
rows dominate prefill rows only at batch size 1 where decode consumes
35.3% while prefill is at 24.1%.

Small encoder passes use concurrent ASR and Sortformer megakernels with
a fixed SM split. Large passes use fused kernels on concurrent streams,
with the CTC timestamp head overlapping the decoder.

## 4. Speech encoder across load regimes

In the speech encoder, each iteration encodes the audio of all requests
it admits in one pass. One second of audio becomes 12.5 encoder tokens,
so the size of a pass is the amount of audio admitted in that iteration.

### 4.1. One encoder pass

Figure 3 shows a pass in B128 serving. A front-end kernel computes
log-mel features for all requests. The two branches of the Parallel
Expert Encoder (NVIDIA, 2026c) run concurrently: the ASR branch (32
layers, d = 1,280, FP8 projections) on the main stream and the
Sortformer diarization branch (31 layers, d=512, BF16) on a side stream.
A tail kernel adds the speaker activities to ASR states and projects the
result to the LLM width (modality adapter). The CTC timestamp head runs
last, on the lowest-priority stream, overlapping the decoder step; its
output feeds the draft hints.

The pass is queued behind the previous iteration’s MTP draft, so the
host builds the next batch while the GPU encodes. In steady-state B128
serving, the 2,048-row prompt budget of a decoder step limits a pass to
about 1,800 tokens: passes carry 1,218 to 1,798 tokens (median 1,690 or
135 s of audio) and take 8.8 ms of the 55 ms iteration on H100 NVL.
Under light load, a pass holds one or a few requests; a 5- to 10-second
utterance is 63 to 125 tokens.

### 4.2. Megakernels for small passes

We have designed two separate megakernels for ASR and Sortformer. Below
384 tokens, the ASR and Sortformer megakernels launch on separate
streams with persistent grids of 94 and 38 CTAs, respectively.

In every CTA, a producer warp loads tiles of weights and activations
with TMA into a six-stage shared-memory ring and two consumer warpgroups
multiply them with wgmma instructions (NVIDIA, 2026e), in FP8 for ASR
and BF16 for Sortformer.

Each layer is a sequence of phases separated by software grid barriers.
Figure 4 illustrates the phases for ASR. The Sortformer megakernel
follows the same plan and ends with the speaker head.

Small passes have too few output tiles to fill the GPU. At 64 tokens,
the projections expose 10 to 40 output tiles. Under a
one-working-CTA-per-SM mapping, this provides useful tile work for
approximately 8 to 30% of 132 SMs. Split-K tile scheduling increases the
available parallel work to 50 to 80 tasks, corresponding to
approximately 53 to 85% of the 94 CTA ASR worker pool.

The megakernel separates phases with grid barriers. The fused path
implements QKV projection, normalization and RoPE, attention, output
projection, and feed-forward phases as eight kernels.

Figure 4. ASR layer phases in the megakernel and fused-kernel paths.The
megakernel separates phases with grid barriers. The fused path
implements QKV projection, normalization and RoPE, attention, output
projection, and feed-forward phases as eight kernels.

Each phase boundary costs a grid barrier instead of a kernel launch, and
a whole pass takes four launches instead of 549. We synchronize at phase
boundaries instead of scheduling tiles across operators. In tile-level
dataflow, as in recent LLM megakernels (Cheng et al., 2026; Spector et
al., 2025), each tile of the next operator waits on counters for only
the tiles it reads, so consecutive phases can overlap. Our encoder
passes leave little to overlap. Below 384 tokens a pass has at most
three 128-row blocks, so each phase fits in one or two waves of tiles on
94 SMs, and every phase reads whole rows: LayerNorm needs all ten column
tiles of a row and their split-K partial sums, GQA needs every key and
value of a request, and the down-projection needs all 40 tiles of the
5,120-wide FFN row. The tiles that one next-phase tile reads are spread
over most CTAs and finish at about the same time, so per-tile counters
would start the next phase little earlier than a barrier, while adding
release and acquire cost to every tile. Based on experiments, a
megakernel with tile-level scheduling is 4 to 10% slower at every B128
shape than the megakernel with grid barriers: its dependency fences
added latency to every task.

### 4.3. Kernel fusion for large passes

Beyond 384 tokens, each ASR layer runs as eight kernels (Figure 4). The
four projections for the H100 GPU are CUTLASS SM90 FP8 GEMMs (TMA,
warp-specialized, 128$`\times`$128$`\times`$128 tiles) whose epilogue
visitor trees apply the per-channel weight scales: the output and FFN
down-projections read the residual stream and add bias and residual in
place, and the FFN up-projection applies bias, GELU and FP8 quantization
for the next GEMM. Four custom kernels complete the layer: QK LayerNorm
with RoPE, a variable-length FlashAttention-3 (Shah et al., 2024) that
writes FP8 for the output projection and two LayerNorm-plus-FP8 kernels.
The ASR kernels launch with programmatic dependent launch (PDL): each
kernel is launched while its producer still runs and waits for the
producer’s results on the GPU so the chain of 258 kernels per pass has
significantly fewer launch gaps.

The Sortformer branch runs as 289 kernels: cuBLASLt BF16 GEMMs, row
kernels for RoPE, attention, bias with residual and LayerNorm, and GELU,
and the speaker head. Instead of a fixed SM split, the two branches
share all SMs: they are queued layer by layer on two streams of equal
priority, so each branch fills the SMs the other leaves idle.

The front end and the speaker fusion are one fused kernel each, in both
regimes.

## 5. Nemotron 3.5 Lightning backbone

Prefill and decode phases share the same Nemotron 3.5 Lightning backbone
which results in 45% to 61% of the end-to-end computation time being
consumed by this backbone.

### 5.1. Prefill and decode share one backbone

The backbone does two jobs:

- •
  Prefill processes a new request’s prompt once (the instruction, 85
  audio embeddings and a short text suffix) to fill the request’s Mamba
  states and KV cache and produce its first token: about 99 rows for a
  typical 6.8 s request after the shared prefix is reused.
- •
  Decode extends the transcript. With speculative decoding, each decode
  pass verifies the last accepted token plus up to 15 drafted tokens per
  request, about 9 rows on average, and a request needs three to four
  passes: about 30 decode rows for the same request.

The engine runs both jobs in the same step: each step reads every weight
once, for the prefill rows of the requests admitted in that iteration
and the decode rows of all requests in flight.

Each second of input audio gives the backbone about 21 prompt tokens to
prefill (12.5 audio embeddings and, on average, 8.6 instruction and
template tokens) but yields only about 3.6 transcript tokens, which
speculative decoding verifies in about 4.4 decode rows. Even after
prefix reuse, prompt rows are 76% of the backbone’s rows, and although a
transcript token costs about four times as much backbone time as a
prompt token (60 against 16 $`\mu`$s at B128), prefill takes 1.6 times
as long as decode: 36% against 22% of serving time in tuned vLLM.

### 5.2. Prefill megakernel design

The prefill megakernel is one cooperative launch of 132 persistent CTAs,
one per SM in the H100 GPU, that runs a packed batch of up to 64 prompts
and 2,048 tokens through all 52 layers. The producer streams
128$`\times`$128 FP8 tiles of weights and activations from HBM into a
six-stage shared-memory ring (6 $`\times`$ 32 KB) with
cp.async.bulk.tensor, and per-stage mbarriers carry the transaction byte
counts. Its distinguishing choice is one pipeline for three operator
families (Mamba-2, GQA and MoE) inside one launch (Figure 5).

A single cooperative launch processes Mamba, attention, and MoE layers
using TMA producers, a shared-memory ring, and consumer warpgroups. Grid
barriers separate the phases.

Figure 5. Prefill megakernel phases and shared execution pipeline.A
single cooperative launch processes Mamba, attention, and MoE layers
using TMA producers, a shared-memory ring, and consumer warpgroups. Grid
barriers separate the phases.

Phases end at software grid barriers. For MoE, expert tiles are swap-AB:
the weight takes wgmma’s M side and the expert’s 8 to 128 tokens its N
side, chosen per expert, so small expert populations no longer pad to
128 rows. Per-expert readiness counters replace the barrier between up
and down projections.

For a fixed cohort, all new incoming requests are computed using the
prefill megakernel. In in-flight batching, prefill rarely runs alone as
a monolithic prefill megakernel: at B128, 90% of engine steps mix the
prompt rows of newly admitted requests with the decode rows of running
ones, 9% carry only decode rows (the drain at the end of a pass) and 1%
carry only prompts. New prompts join the mixed step following the
encoder pass and take the same kernels as the decode rows; that is the
decoder’s regime-aware design.

### 5.3. Decoder regime-aware design

The decoder kernel is a combination of three megakernels and
corresponding custom fused CUTLASS/cuBLAS kernels (Figure 6). During
runtime, the GQA megakernel is executed for each iteration while the
Mamba and MoE megakernels are replaced by corresponding custom fused
kernels depending on the tensor dimensions.

Each decoder layer uses fused projections and row kernels. Mamba state
replay and chunked SSD operate on separate streams; GQA uses a
megakernel, and MoE selects a megakernel or grouped GEMMs according to
pass size.

Figure 6. Regime-aware kernel composition for the decoder step.Each
decoder layer uses fused projections and row kernels. Mamba state replay
and chunked SSD operate on separate streams; GQA uses a megakernel, and
MoE selects a megakernel or grouped GEMMs according to pass size.

The decode step launches its kernels layer by layer on a non-blocking
stream at the highest priority. Our kernels and the CUTLASS GEMMs use
programmatic dependent launch (PDL). The CUTLASS GEMMs are compiled with
grid-dependency control. Prompt and decode rows share every weight read,
so a B128 step streams its 29.8 GB of FP8 expert weights once for both
kinds of rows. The dense projections are CUTLASS SM90 FP8 GEMMs (TMA,
warp-specialized ping-pong mainloops, FP8 GEMMs with FP32 accumulator
types, scale epilogues) and each layer starts with one row kernel for
the residual add, RMSNorm and FP8 cast, which the MoE finalize absorbs
after MoE layers. In a Mamba layer, the prompt rows run a fused
convolution and chunk-parallel SSD on the main stream while a side
stream, forked and joined with events, replays the decode rows: each
request’s 1 MB of BF16 state per layer is read into registers, up to 16
rows are verified from 16-entry x, dt and B rings on CUDA cores, and the
state is written back only when the ring fills. The Mamba megakernel
fuses the same replay into one launch but handles at most four rows per
request.

The GQA megakernel quantizes queries, writes keys and values into the
paged FP8 cache and computes attention with mma.sync m16n8k16 BF16 tiles
whose 16-row M dimension holds the 16 query heads that share a KV head:
each warp handles one query row for all 16 heads, and one CTA per GQA
block and KV head stages the keys and values in shared memory once. In
an MoE layer, a cuBLAS BF16 router and a top-6 kernel feed the MoE
megakernel below 448 rows: one persistent launch that writes each
token’s FP8 copy straight to its six expert positions and the shared
expert, runs both as swap-AB wgmma tiles of 8 to 64 tokens from nine 24
KB ring slots, and issues weight loads before a tile’s inputs are ready.
From 448 rows, CUTLASS ptr-array grouped FP8 GEMMs take over with the
shared expert as a 129th group and ReLU² and FP8 fused into the FC1
epilogue; above 1,200 rows they use 128 $`\times`$ 128 tiles whose
2$`\times`$1 TMA multicast halves the token-tile reads, and they stream
expert weights at 2.5 to 2.8 TB/s.

One finalize kernel combines the experts and applies the next layer’s
residual add, RMSNorm and FP8 cast, and the LM head is a cuBLAS BF16
GEMM with a greedy argmax kernel.

## 6. Optimizing the draft model

The draft model for MTP is megakernel-stable.

### 6.1. Draft model

The model’s multi-token prediction (MTP) module has one attention block
and one MoE block with shared weights, predicts the next token from the
backbone’s last hidden state and can be applied repeatedly. For each
iteration, it proposes K tokens per request and the next decoder step
verifies them.

Mean acceptance length
$`\mathrm{MAL}(K)=1+\sum_{i=1}^{K}\prod_{j=1}^{i}p_{j}`$ counts emitted
tokens per verification for $`K`$ drafts, including one bonus or
correction token. Here, $`p_{j}`$ is acceptance at position $`j`$
conditional on preceding drafts’ acceptance. Measured probabilities of
0.971, 0.977, 0.939, 0.931 and 0.931 yield $`\mathrm{MAL}(5)=5.41`$
tokens.

We use one megakernel for the draft model which works in composition
with other custom fused kernels (Figure 7).

### 6.2. Draft megakernel design

The draft model runs as one megakernel. Each pass is one cooperative
launch of 264 persistent CTAs, two per SM. Cooperative-groups grid
barriers, up to 16 per pass, separate the phases of the MTP block.

The routed experts swap A and B: an expert’s weights fill the 64-row M
side and up to 32 of its tokens the N side, on an eight-stage ring with
64 $`\times`$ 32 $`\times`$ 16 wgmma; above 4 rows, the shared expert
joins this GEMM as one more expert, which removes two phases and their
barriers. Attention puts the 16 query heads of a KV head on the M=16
side of mma.sync m16n8k16: FP8 queries are converted straight into
A-fragment registers, 32-key FP8 tiles of the cache are double-buffered
into shared memory with cp.async and the CTA’s four warps split the
context and merge their softmax maxima and sums in shared memory; long
contexts are split into KV segments that one more phase merges.
Activations stay under 3 MB per buffer at 128 rows, so a pass’s HBM
traffic is its weights plus 512 B of cache per context token and
request.

All drafts of an iteration, K$`\times`$128 token IDs, reach the host in
one 6 KB copy at the end. The gaps between passes total 0.13 ms per
iteration, so we kept one launch per pass.

### 6.3. Custom fused kernels

Six custom kernels feed and finish the megakernel, and the CTC head
drafts from the encoder side (Table 3). Three carry the main ideas: the
prefix KV kernel computes only what the prompt rows need, the head GEMM
reduces its logits to an argmax inside its epilogue, and the embedding
kernel reads the head’s token IDs in place, which keeps all K passes on
the GPU. The head also never dequantizes: each input row is scaled so
its absolute maximum maps to 448 (FP8 E4M3 maximum) and the scale is
discarded, because a common positive scale across the vocabulary logits
does not change their argmax.

## 7. What optimizations did not land?

### 7.1. Optimizations impacting accuracy

As some optimizations trade WER for throughput, the engine exposes them
only as opt-in features.

Expert masking removes the 32 least-routed experts per layer from
routing, so their weights are never streamed (+3.0% throughput at +4.2%
relative WER). Fewer experts per token means fewer routing rounds per
row (top-5: +1.5% at +1.7%; top-4: +3.5% at +6.2%). Diarization bypass
skips the Sortformer branch for single-speaker audio (+2.1% with no WER
change on LibriSpeech dev). Layer skipping removes whole MoE layers,
chosen by greedy forward selection on 512 LibriSpeech dev utterances:
each round adds the layer with the smallest WER increase and stops when
calibration WER would exceed 2.55%. It selects 7 of 23 MoE layers (6, 8,
13, 20, 27, 38 and 43) for 11% more throughput; the Bias corpus is never
used for selection.

Together the switches add 13 to 16% throughput on B200 but raise WER

### 7.2. Optimizations that lowered performance

An expert-major MoE kernel, a persistent warp-specialized FP8 GEMM that
kept token tiles resident, was 1 to 3% slower than the grouped CUTLASS
GEMMs, because the SM clock fell to 0.82 to 1.18 GHz while weights
streamed under the 400 W cap in H100 NVL.

INT4 experts halved the expert bytes, but their mixed-input grouped
GEMMs took 400 to 630 $`\mu`$s per FC1 call against 230 to 245 $`\mu`$s
in FP8.

Green-context SM partitions between the draft and the encoder never beat
sharing all 132 SMs (the best split, 1,818 RTFx, was within noise of
1,809).

Decode megakernels inside vLLM sped decode-only steps by 7.6 to 16.4%
but moved throughput by $`\times`$0.9965 because those steps carried
0.3% of the tokens.

A faster Sortformer could not shorten encoder passes in which the ASR
branch was longer, and larger encoder GEMM tiles left SMs idle with only
90 tiles at 1,152 tokens. Two experts per token reached 5.4% WER and ran
slower than three. Each failure traces to one of three principles, the
lessons we would carry to another hybrid model:

1.  (1)
    Power wall: the board always draws 400 W, so time follows energy,
    $`t\approx(e_{b}B+e_{f}\Phi)/P`$; fewer bytes or FLOPs shorten work
    and better scheduling mostly lowers the clock (expert-major kernel,
    INT4 experts, green contexts).

    The classic roofline (Williams et al., 2009) says a kernel needs at
    least the longer of its memory time and its compute time:

    ```math
    t\geq\max\!\left(\frac{B}{\mathrm{BW}_{\mathrm{peak}}},\frac{\Phi}{F_{\mathrm{peak}}}\right).
    ```

    This assumes the GPU can run memory and arithmetic at their peaks
    simultaneously, so the shorter one hides under the longer one. The
    energy-roofline model of Choi et al. (Choi et al., 2013; Choi et
    al., 2014) adds a third limit for power-capped processors: the total
    energy divided by the power available. When that limit binds, memory
    and compute compete for the same watts, so their times add instead
    of overlapping:

    ```math
    t\approx\frac{e_{b}B+e_{f}\Phi}{P}.
    ```

    Here, $`e_{b}`$ is the energy needed to move a unit of data, B is
    the number of bytes moved, $`e_{f}`$ is the energy per unit of
    computation, $`\Phi`$ is the total computation and P is the power
    available for use:

    ```math
    P=P_{\mathrm{cap}}-P_{\mathrm{static}}.
    ```

    Here, $`P_{\mathrm{cap}}`$ is the power cap of the GPU and
    $`P_{\mathrm{static}}`$ is the minimum power consumed while the GPU
    is on. Over a full pass at 1,280 requests on H100 NVL, board power
    stays within 2% of the 400 W cap in 96% of 10 Hz samples, and the SM
    clock averages 1.32 GHz against a 1.785 GHz maximum.

2.  (2)
    Critical path: parallel branches finish with the longest, so
    shortening the longer branch provides the most direct reduction in
    completion time.
3.  (3)
    Filling the GPU: tiles must match the work; too few tiles leave SMs
    idle, which is why megakernels win below 384 tokens and 448 rows and
    larger encoder tiles lost above.

## 8. Evaluation

We evaluate end-to-end transcription on the English Bias corpus,
comprising 4,820 recordings and 15.36 hours of audio, using single H100
SXM and H100 NVL GPUs with 700 W and 400 W power limits, respectively.
Table 4 reports mean RTFx across three measured passes following a
full-corpus warmup.

The vLLM baseline is NVIDIA’s production configuration of vLLM 0.29.0
(vllm/vllm-openai:v0.29.0, build 98dff2a8) with the NeMo speech plugin
and CUDA 13.0.2. It serves the ModelOpt FP8 checkpoint (FP8 linear
layers, experts and KV cache) with 3-token MTP speculation. It uses
FlashInfer attention (Ye et al., 2025), CUTLASS dense GEMMs,
FlashInfer-CUTLASS MoE and a BF16 Mamba state with 16-step ReplaySSM
decoding. It also uses torch.compile with Inductor graph partitioning
(decode sizes 512 to 768 precompiled), CUDA graphs up to 768 sequences,
and the product’s encoder graphs, packed Sortformer and head graphs.
Limits are 768 concurrent sequences, 19,456 batched tokens per step, a
20 GiB KV-cache budget, 0.88 GPU memory utilization and four API
servers. B200 uses the same limits with its SM100 FP8 profile. Both
systems serve the same FP8 checkpoint on one GPU behind the same HTTP
client. The client prepares all 4,820 requests before starting the
clock, sends them in descending duration order with a 4 s ramp, and
computes RTFx over the whole pass, including ramp and drain.

Our accuracy-preserving serving achieves 1.38 to 4.39$`\times`$ higher
throughput on SXM and 1.40 to 2.39$`\times`$ on NVL, reaching
4,345$`\times`$ and 3,381$`\times`$ RTFx, or 1.39$`\times`$ and
1.40$`\times`$ vLLM’s peak. At 128 concurrent requests, p95 end-to-end
completion time decreases from 1.501 to 0.545 seconds on SXM and from
1.308 to 0.712 seconds on NVL. WER is 1.91 to 1.92%, compared with 1.88
to 1.93% for vLLM using the same checkpoint, workload, and text
normalization; neither system is bit-exact, because batch composition
changes FP8 GEMM tiling and reduction order.

B200 runs a separate SM100 build of the same engine, not a recompile.
Blackwell has no wgmma, so every tensor-core path in the megakernels,
the draft head and the fused GEMMs is reimplemented with UMMA
(tcgen05.mma), which accumulates in tensor memory. The SM100 code
allocates TMEM, waits on MMA-completion barriers and drains accumulators
before each epilogue; register-level mma.sync paths such as attention
carry over. Persistent grids are sized from the SM count queried at
start-up (144, not H100’s 132), so the concurrent ASR and diarization
megakernels split the GPU 72/72 instead of 94/38. The scheduler, switch
points (384 tokens, 448 rows), admission limits (255 resident requests;
6,144 prompt tokens and 8,192 rows per step), MTP depth and numerical
recipes are unchanged. Re-measured on B200, the crossovers stay within
32 tokens and 32 rows of the H100 values, so we keep the same
thresholds. One B200-specific change mattered at high load. The draft’s

slowdown once a draft pass exceeded 128 requests and raised throughput

3.1$`\times`$ faster than vLLM at 32 concurrent requests and

climbing toward 3,894$`\times`$. The p95 end-to-end completion time is
1.9 to 3.8$`\times`$ lower at every concurrency (0.21 s against 0.63 s
at 32), and peak GPU memory is 56.0 GiB against 73.3 GiB. Our optimized
serving holds WER at 1.92 to 1.93% against vLLM’s 1.89 to 1.91%, within
0.04 percentage points. Enabling all opt-in optimizations adds 13 to 16%
throughput, reaching 7,414$`\times`$ at 1,280 requests, but raises WER
to 3.44 to 3.48%.

With a 400 ms limit on p95 end-to-end completion time, our engine serves
64 concurrent requests on H100 SXM (0.365 s; 0.545 s at 128) and 128 on
B200 (0.368 s; 0.595 s at 256), while vLLM meets the limit only up to 8
on H100 SXM and 16 on B200: 8$`\times`$ as many requests within the
budget. Our optimized serving on H100 SXM is more performant than vLLM
serving on B200, bridging the hardware improvement over a GPU generation
with software serving optimizations only.

We additionally evaluate the complete LibriSpeech (Panayotov et al., 2015) test-clean and test-other splits: 5,559 recordings totaling 10.75
hours, with a mean duration of 6.96 seconds compared with 11.47 seconds
for the Bias corpus. Requests follow a frozen shuffled order, and
results are averaged over two measured passes after a full-corpus
warmup, retaining the same end-to-end timing boundaries. Across 32 to
1,280 concurrent requests, our regime-aware serving achieves
1.60$`\times`$ to 3.52$`\times`$ vLLM’s throughput on H100 SXM 700 W and
1.82$`\times`$ to 3.90$`\times`$ on B200, reaching 3,739$`\times`$ and
5,219$`\times`$ real time, respectively. At 128 concurrent requests, p95
end-to-end completion time decreases from 1.587 to 0.454 seconds on H100
SXM and from 1.356 to 0.328 seconds on B200. Combined WER is 2.437 to
2.457%, compared with 2.449 to 2.485% for vLLM. These results extend the
throughput and latency benefits to a public corpus with shorter
recordings and shuffled arrivals, supporting the applicability of the
serving design beyond the original industrial workload.

## 9. Related work

Persistent kernels keep a computation resident across steps (Gupta et
al., 2012; Diamos et al., 2016), and recent LLM megakernels schedule
tiles across operators in a single launch (Cheng et al., 2026; Spector
et al., 2025), whereas path tracing found a monolithic megakernel
harmful (Laine et al., 2013). Following our earlier analysis (Chatterjee
et al., 2026), we run a megakernel only where a pass is small enough for
it to win, and fused kernels above a measured crossover. Speculative
decoding (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024)
drafts with a smaller model, extra decoding heads (Cai et al., 2024) or
multi-token prediction modules (Gloeckle et al., 2024; DeepSeek-AI,
2024); in hybrid models, verification must also restore the state-space
states (Wang et al., 2024).

Mixing prompt and decode rows in one step follows chunked prefill in
Sarathi-Serve (Agrawal et al., 2024), which vLLM also uses; we apply it
to a hybrid model whose verification rows also need Mamba state replay.
Megakernel serving engines (Tong et al., 2026; Spector et al., 2025)
mostly target decode; we target a prefill-dominated workload and switch
per pass. RaMP (Sharma and Datta, 2026) dispatches MoE kernels from the
live routing histogram, whereas we dispatch from pass size alone.

State-space and hybrid Mamba-Transformer models (Gu and Dao, 2024; Dao
and Gu, 2024; NVIDIA, 2025) and speech LLMs such as Nemotron 3.5
Transcribe (NVIDIA, 2026c) are usually served with stacks tuned for
chat, while ASR serving has centered on batched inference for
encoder-decoder models such as Whisper (Radford et al., 2023). We know
of no published study of serving a prefill-dominated hybrid speech LLM
with mixture-of-experts layers.

## 10. Open problems and conclusion

Our regime-aware engine serves Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe at 2.74 times the
audio per second of tuned vLLM at 128 concurrent requests on H100 SXM
(1.38 to 4.39$`\times`$ across 32 to 1,280), cuts p95 end-to-end
completion time from 1.50 s to 0.55 s, and cuts GPU time per audio hour
by 63% at 128 requests and 28% at peak; opt-in switches add 13 to 16% on
B200. We expect the lesson to carry to other hybrid models on
power-capped GPUs: when the board draws its limit, speed comes from
fewer bytes and fewer operations, not from better overlap.

This work raises three open problems:

1.  (1)
    The crossover points are determined offline. Different routing and
    admission limits can move the crossover points at runtime. What is
    the optimal online approach to determine crossover points?
2.  (2)
    Our approach of regime-aware megakernels and kernel fusion results
    in dynamic granularity at the operator level. Is this the optimal
    performance model for all megakernel-receding architectures?
3.  (3)
    Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe is a prefill-dominated megakernel-receding
    architecture. How do the optimization strategy and operator
    granularity change depending on different components becoming
    dominant?
