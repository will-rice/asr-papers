# How Far Can the Gemma 4 Speech Encoder Go Without Changing Its Weights? Saudi and Gulf Arabic Speech Recognition with a Frozen and an Adapter-Augmented Encoder

Amr Abdelkhalik Affiliation: Independent Researcher, Saudi Arabia
Email: [amr.khalil.abdelkhalik@gmail.com](mailto:)

###### Abstract

We study the 305M-parameter audio encoder shared by Gemma 4 and
EmbeddingGemma 2 (identical except for its final projection) for Saudi
and Gulf Arabic speech recognition, without changing any of its weights.
A frozen system trains only a 22.6M-parameter CTC reader over a mix of
encoder layers; an adapted system adds 3.1M low-rank adapter parameters,
a 41.5M-parameter hybrid CTC/attention reader and joint decoding with a
sub-word language model. Against Cohere Transcribe Arabic (2.07B
parameters), re-run on identical audio, the frozen system trails in word
error rate (WER) but is statistically indistinguishable in character
error rate (CER) on five of six human-labelled conditions, while running
3.4 times faster on CPU with greedy decoding. The adapted system is
statistically indistinguishable in WER on a public Saudi
customer-service call (19.20% against 19.27%) and on clean Emirati
speech, is significantly better on telephone-coded Emirati speech, has a
significantly lower CER on all six conditions of these sets, and beats a
0.6B recogniser fine-tuned on SADA in WER on all six; it still trails
Cohere in WER on long-form Saudi speech. On a blind, human-corrected
public call-centre sample it ranks 4 of 15 systems, behind three
commercial APIs (significantly only one) and ahead of the other
self-hostable systems by point estimate. An optimised joint decoder runs
it at 139 times real time on one GPU. We also show that references made
by correcting machine drafts favour the drafting system. Code and
public-set results are released.

## 1 Introduction

Arabic speech recognition is hardest where it matters most commercially:
dialectal speech over the telephone. Saudi and Gulf call centres mix
regional dialects, code-switching, and spoken digit strings such as
identity and account numbers. All of this arrives on 8 kHz narrow-band
audio. Public benchmarks confirm the difficulty. On the Saudi SADA test
set, the best open systems in the Open Universal Arabic ASR Leaderboard
still reach word error rates (WER) above 40% \[1\]. A dedicated
benchmark of Arabic call-domain speech reports WERs near 50%, and this
holds even for large commercial recognisers \[2\].

The usual remedies are to fine-tune a large pretrained recogniser \[3,
4, 5, 6\] or to train a dedicated Arabic model on many hours of labelled
speech \[7, 8\]. Both demand substantial compute for every new domain. A
cheaper option keeps a strong speech encoder frozen and trains only a
small task head on top of it. Probing benchmarks such as SUPERB and
ML-SUPERB \[9, 10\] show that frozen self-supervised encoders retain
enough phonetic information for recognition. Layer-wise analyses go
further and reveal that this information peaks in intermediate layers
rather than at the output \[11, 12\].

Open multimodal language models now ship with their own speech encoders.
Gemma 4 \[13\] attaches a 305M-parameter Conformer audio encoder,
derived from the Universal Speech Model design \[14\], to a text
decoder, and the same encoder has since been reused elsewhere: Google
states that EmbeddingGemma 2, a multimodal embedding model, “shares its
text tokenizer and audio encoder” with Gemma 4 \[15, 16\]. We confirmed
this directly. Of the 752 audio-encoder tensors in the released
EmbeddingGemma 2 checkpoint, 751 are bit-identical to those of Gemma 4
E2B \[17\]; the only difference is the final projection into each
model’s text embedding space, which a recogniser does not use. The
encoder is therefore a component that developers may already be running,
inside an assistant model or an embedding service, and it is released
under the Apache-2.0 licence. Whether it can be reused for Arabic
transcription without fine-tuning has, to our knowledge, not been
studied.

This paper asks a narrow question. How far can this speech encoder go on
Saudi and Gulf Arabic, including telephone speech, when its weights stay
unchanged or are adapted only through a small number of added
parameters? We make five contributions.

1.  1.  A _frozen_ recipe: the unchanged Gemma 4 audio encoder (as
        distributed with EmbeddingGemma 2), a learned mix over its hidden
        layers, a 22.6M-parameter transformer reader trained with
        connectionist temporal classification (CTC) \[18\], and a word
        4-gram language model in beam search \[19, 20\].
2.  2.  An _adapted_ recipe that keeps the encoder’s weights frozen but adds
        3.1M low-rank adapter parameters \[21\] to six middle layers, with a
        41.5M-parameter hybrid CTC/attention reader \[22\], self-conditioned
        CTC \[23\] and joint CTC/attention decoding \[24\] with a sub-word
        language model.
3.  3.  An evaluation on SADA, three further human-labelled Saudi and Gulf
        test sets, a blind human-corrected public call-centre sample with
        fifteen recognisers including twelve commercial services, and real
        call-centre recordings, against open recognisers re-run on identical
        audio: Cohere Transcribe Arabic (2.07B parameters) \[25\] and
        Nemotron 3.5 ASR (0.6B) \[26\], zero-shot and fine-tuned.
4.  4.  We present a faster implementation of joint CTC/attention decoding
        with a language model. On one GPU, it raises throughput from 1.7 to
        139 times real time. Accuracy is essentially unchanged. We also
        measure speed and memory on GPU and CPU under a single protocol.
        Paired bootstrap intervals \[27\] and N-best oracle analyses then
        separate model errors from ranking errors.
5.  5.  Call-centre references produced by correcting machine drafts are
        mostly the unedited drafts. Such references favour the system that
        produced them, so we report results split by reference origin.

The results depend on the measure. The frozen system stays behind the
2B-parameter recogniser in WER on every test set. Its character error
rate, however, is statistically indistinguishable from that recogniser’s
on five of the six human-labelled conditions, and it runs several times
faster on CPU. The adapted system is statistically indistinguishable
from the 2B recogniser in WER on the public customer-service call and on
clean Emirati speech, and better on telephone-coded Emirati speech. On
the further human-labelled sets it is significantly lower in CER across
all six conditions. In WER, however, it remains behind on long-form
SaudiTalk speech. Section 2 reviews related work, Section 3 describes
the systems, Section 4 the data and protocol, Section 5 the results,
Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Dialectal and Telephone Arabic ASR

Arabic recognition research has moved from Modern Standard Arabic
broadcast speech towards dialects. SADA \[28\] provides several hundred
hours of Saudi television speech with human transcripts, and it has
become the standard Saudi test bed. Fine-tuning results on its clean
test subset are reported in \[29\]. Casablanca \[30\] covers eight
Arabic varieties, including Emirati and Yemeni, with human transcripts
and code-switching annotations. The Open Universal Arabic ASR
Leaderboard \[1\] evaluates open models on several of these sets under a
common text normalisation. Large-scale weakly supervised training on
Arabic is studied in \[7\], while ArTST \[8\] pre-trains a joint Arabic
speech–text model. For the call domain, \[2\] released a benchmark of
Arabic call-centre style speech, yet its audio is 16 kHz. No public
Saudi corpus of real 8 kHz call-centre speech of useful size exists,
only small free samples, so we combine real call recordings, which we
cannot release, with public human-labelled sets and with telephone-coded
versions of them.

### 2.2 Frozen Encoders and Layer Analysis

Self-supervised encoders such as wav2vec 2.0 \[5\] and w2v-BERT \[31,
32\] are commonly fine-tuned end to end. SUPERB \[9\] and ML-SUPERB
\[10\] instead evaluate them frozen, feeding a learned weighted sum over
layers into a light downstream model. This protocol is the basis of our
reader. Frozen encoders of supervised recognisers are also strong
general features. Whisper-AT \[33\], for example, tags audio events from
the frozen Whisper encoder. Pasad et al. \[11, 12\] showed that phonetic
and word information is strongest in middle layers and weaker at the top
of pretrained speech models.

### 2.3 Speech Encoders in Multimodal and Embedding Models

Recent multimodal language models attach a speech encoder to a text
decoder. SLAM-ASR \[34\] showed that a frozen encoder, a small projector
and a language model can give strong English recognition. The Gemma 4
report \[13\] describes an audio encoder of about 305M parameters. That
encoder uses two down-sampling convolutions and 12 Conformer layers
\[35\], producing one frame every 40 ms. A separate line of work trains
speech encoders for cross-modal or semantic alignment, including
SAMU-XLSR \[36\], SONAR \[37\], SpeechCLIP \[38\] and CLAP \[39\]. Those
models are optimised for utterance-level representations rather than
transcription. The encoder studied here belongs to the first group,
because it is the encoder of an assistant model reused unchanged in an
embedding model. We are not aware of a published Arabic recognition
study of it.

### 2.4 Reader Architectures and Decoding

CTC \[18\] gives a $`n`$on-autoregressive output layer. Hybrid
CTC/attention models \[22\], intermediate CTC losses \[40\] and
self-conditioned CTC \[23\] relax the conditional independence of CTC.
Joint CTC/attention decoding \[24\] scores attention hypotheses with CTC
prefix probabilities. Shallow fusion adds an external language model to
sequence-to-sequence search \[41\]. External n-gram models remain a
cheap way to add word-level context to CTC output \[19\]. Our frozen
system keeps the reader deliberately simple, so that the measurement
isolates the encoder. The adapted system then combines these established
components.

## 3 Method

Figure 1 gives an overview of both systems.

Figure 1: The two systems. The Gemma 4 audio encoder is never changed
(grey). Both systems train a layer mix, a reader and a CTC head (blue);
the adapted system adds low-rank adapters inside the encoder, an
attention decoder with self-conditioned CTC and joint CTC/attention
search (orange).

### 3.1 Frozen Encoder

The model’s own feature extractor converts audio at 16 kHz into log-mel
features. The audio encoder, loaded from the EmbeddingGemma 2
checkpoint, runs in bfloat16 with all weights frozen. It has 12
Conformer-style layers of width 1024, followed by an output projection
to 1536 dimensions, and it emits 25 frames per second. Its released
configuration uses chunked local self-attention with no right context,
so the encoder itself is causal. The reader described next is, however,
not causal. We take the hidden states of layers 6, 8, 10, 11 and 12,
together with the projected output, which gives six representations in
total. A preliminary probe guided this subset, and the other layers were
not tried in the mix. Telephone audio is up-sampled to 16 kHz before
feature extraction.

### 3.2 Reader

Each selected representation $`h_{\ell}`$ is normalised and projected to
512 dimensions. The projections are then combined with
softmax-normalised weights:

```math
x_{t}=\sum_{\ell}\frac{\exp(a_{\ell})}{\sum_{k}\exp(a_{k})}\,W_{\ell}\,\mathrm{LN}(h_{\ell,t}), \tag{1}
```

where the $`a_{\ell}`$ are learned scalars initialised at zero. The
mixed sequence passes through a residual depth-wise convolution (kernel
5), six pre-norm bidirectional transformer layers (512 dimensions, 8
heads, feed-forward size 2048, dropout 0.1) and a linear CTC output
layer over 68 symbols: the Arabic letters left after normalisation,
digits, the word boundary and the CTC blank. The reader has 22.6M
parameters. During training, two random time spans per utterance, each
up to 5% of its length, are zeroed in the mixed features, in the spirit
of SpecAugment \[42\].

### 3.3 Training

We train the reader with the CTC loss. We use AdamW with a learning rate
of $`3\times 10^{-4}`$ and weight decay 0.01, under a one-cycle
schedule. Batches hold up to 6,000 encoder frames. Encoder features are
computed on the fly, so the telephone simulation below can be applied
afresh in each epoch. We select the model by validation character error
rate on held-out calls.

### 3.4 Telephone Simulation

To expose the reader to narrow-band speech, wideband training audio is
degraded on the fly. The audio is first down-sampled to 8 kHz, then
encoded and decoded with G.711 $`\mu`$-law \[43\], and finally
up-sampled back to 16 kHz. The telephone versions of the test sets are
produced by this same deterministic procedure.

### 3.5 Decoding

We report greedy CTC decoding and beam search with a word 4-gram
language model \[19\] through pyctcdecode \[20\] (beam 64). The language
model is trained on the training-call transcripts and the SADA training
transcripts, after the same normalisation as the scoring. Its weight and
word-insertion bonus are tuned on the validation calls only, and the
selected values are 0.5 and 2.0. Test data are never used for this
tuning.

### 3.6 Adapted System

The adapted system keeps every encoder weight frozen but inserts
low-rank adapters \[21\] (rank 16, scaling 32/16). They are placed first
in the query, key, value and output projections of encoder layers 6 to
10 and later also in the feed-forward and convolution modules of layers
6 to 11, adding 3.1M trainable parameters in total. Its output units are
1,024 SentencePiece sub-words \[44\] instead of characters. The reader
keeps the layer mix, convolution and six transformer layers of the
frozen system and adds a CTC head and a four-layer autoregressive
transformer decoder (512 dimensions, 8 heads) with gated sinusoidal
positions; the reader has 41.5M parameters. Training proceeds in five
warm-started stages: (1) sub-word CTC with the attention adapters; (2)
the hybrid reader, initialised from stage 1, trained with CTC and
attention losses for 6 epochs; (3) the decoder alone on the frozen
reader, with label smoothing of 0.05 on content tokens, none on the
end-of-sequence token and a weight of 1.5 on it, so that the decoder
learns to stop; (4) all reader and adapter parameters jointly for 3
epochs with loss weights 0.3 (CTC), 0.5 (attention) and 0.2
(self-conditioned CTC \[23\], a shared head after reader layers 2 and 4
whose posteriors are fed back through a zero-initialised projection);
and (5) the same joint training for 4 further epochs after adding
zero-initialised adapters to the feed-forward and convolution modules,
with 80 h of SADA per epoch. Every warm-started run first re-scores the
validation set and must reproduce the source model’s CTC result within
one point. Each machine-labelled call window is weighted by the
agreement of its two labels: windows whose labels differ by at most 25%
CER get weight 1, by at most 35% weight 0.5, by at most 45% weight 0.25,
and the rest are dropped; SADA utterances marked as unclear are removed.

The adapted system is decoded in two ways. The first, CTC beam search,
uses a word 4-gram (weight 0.30, word bonus 1.50). The second, joint
CTC/attention beam search \[24\] (CTC weight 0.4), adds a 6-gram over
the model’s own sub-words by shallow fusion \[41\] (weight 0.15, length
bonus 1.00). Both language models are trained on the training-call
transcripts, SADA training transcripts and MASC training transcripts
\[45\], with all validation and test calls excluded. All decoding
weights, and the choice between two language-model variants, were
selected on the validation calls only. That selection followed stage 4
with a beam of 10, where the chosen variant scored WER 29.72% on
validation against 30.05% for the alternative. The choices were then
kept unchanged for stage 5. Test results use a beam of 16 and its top
hypothesis.

## 4 Experimental Setup

### 4.1 Training Data

#### Call-centre speech

The in-domain data are real Saudi call-centre recordings, taken as
single-party legs of 8 kHz telephone calls and stored at 16 kHz. We cut
them into windows of up to 30 s. Training labels are machine-generated:
each window carries one transcript from Cohere Transcribe Arabic and one
from a commercial cloud recogniser, and in each epoch one of the two is
sampled per window. Calls reserved for validation and testing are kept
out of both training and the language-model text. After filtering, 6,050
training windows with 12,089 labels remain. The validation set holds 295
windows drawn from 147 calls, and because these validation labels are
also machine transcripts, validation scores serve only to select models.

#### Voicebot calls

The adapted system additionally uses 1.63 h of real Saudi voicebot
telephone calls (67 calls, 2,011 voice-activity segments), repeated four
times per epoch. Their labels are machine transcripts aligned to Cohere
segment hypotheses. Segments whose machine transcript and Cohere
hypothesis differ by more than 60% WER are dropped.

#### SADA

The SADA training set \[28\] supplies human-labelled Saudi speech:
77,514 utterances (121.6 h), of which 60 h are sampled in each epoch. In
the _telephone_ variant, each SADA utterance is degraded as described
above, and in the _clean_ variant it is used as is.

### 4.2 Systems

We train three readers. The _calls-only_ reader is trained on the
call-centre windows for 20 epochs, and the best validation epoch falls
at 9. Both the _telephone-SADA_ reader and the _clean-SADA_ reader begin
from the calls-only reader and continue for 6 further epochs. Their
training data consist of the calls plus SADA, and SADA is degraded to
telephone quality in one case but kept clean in the other. For both, the
best validation epoch is 3. We trained each configuration once. The
adapted system of Section 3.6 starts from the same data, with the
additions described above.

Two open recognisers serve as baselines. Both were run on exactly the
same audio. They were scored with the same normalisation.

- •
  Cohere Transcribe Arabic (07-2026, 2.07B parameters, Apache-2.0) \[25,
  46\], a dedicated Arabic encoder–decoder recogniser. We use greedy
  decoding on voice-activity segments of at most 25 s, with a repetition
  penalty of 1.2 and no repeated 4-grams to prevent the looping that the
  model otherwise shows on silence.
- •
  Nemotron 3.5 ASR streaming 0.6B \[26\], a cache-aware streaming
  FastConformer transducer. We use it zero-shot and after full
  fine-tuning on SADA only, in two stages of 4,000 steps each (learning
  rate $`10^{-5}`$, cosine schedule): first on clean SADA, then on about
  50 h of SADA coded with GSM to simulate telephony. It never saw the
  call-centre data. Decoding uses beam search of width 8, with the
  attention-context setting of 56 frames of left and 13 of right
  context.

Cohere Transcribe Arabic supplied one of the two machine labels for the
call-centre training data, so the frozen-encoder system is partly
distilled from it. The fine-tuned Nemotron model was trained on SADA
alone.

### 4.3 Test Sets

- •
  SADA test set: 4,971 human-labelled utterances of Saudi television
  speech \[28\], in clean and telephone-coded form.
- •
  The Saudi customer-service sample is the free portion of a commercial
  corpus \[47\]. It consists of one 13-minute call leg, cut at its human
  time stamps into 48 turns. It is the closest public match to our
  domain. Still, it comes from a single call and a single speaker.
- •
  Casablanca, Emirati: the 813 human-labelled utterances of the Emirati
  test split \[30\].
- •
  SaudiTalk comprises 133 human-verified recordings in Hijazi, Ha’il and
  Southern dialects \[48\], amounting to about 6 h in total. Transcripts
  cover whole clips and carry no time stamps, so all systems decode
  voice-activity segments of at most 25 s, and the segment outputs are
  then joined per clip.
- •
  The blind call-centre sample contains 92 clips, totalling 8.3 minutes
  and 854 reference words. The clips come from two one-sided mobile
  recordings in a public Saudi call-centre dialogue sample \[49\]. Clips
  were cut at voice-activity boundaries. Each clip received a draft
  transcript from one of three systems, assigned at random in equal
  shares and hidden from the annotator. The author, a native speaker,
  then corrected each draft into a verbatim reference. Each system is
  scored only on clips its own draft did not produce. Pairs of systems
  are compared only on clips drafted by neither of them.
- •
  The call-centre set contains 72 windows drawn from 63 held-out calls
  in the in-house recordings. The windows were deliberately selected to
  over-represent spoken numbers (29 windows) and windows on which the
  two machine transcripts disagreed most. Consequently, they are harder
  than typical call audio.

Every test set except the in-house calls is also scored after telephone
coding.

#### How the call-centre references were made

An annotator listened to each window in a correction tool prefilled with
the longer of the two machine transcripts, and was asked to make it
verbatim. In 63 of the 72 windows the draft was accepted unchanged: 30
Cohere drafts and 33 drafts from the commercial cloud recogniser. Only 9
were edited. The references are therefore mostly machine text checked by
a single listener, and they favour the drafting systems. Cohere’s own
draft scores 30.39% WER against them, whereas the same model re-run on
identical audio scores 37.62%. We treat this set as secondary and report
it split by reference origin.

### 4.4 Metrics and Statistics

Text is normalised identically for every system: Unicode NFKC, removal
of diacritics and tatweel, unification of alef, alef maqsura, taa
marbuta and hamza carriers, collapse of letters repeated three or more
times, removal of Arabic punctuation (comma, semicolon, question mark
and similar marks), and removal of all characters other than Arabic
letters, digits and spaces. Digits and spelled-out numbers are not
converted into each other, so a number written in words by one system
and in digits by the reference counts as an error. Our main WER and
character error rate (CER, with spaces removed) are computed per
utterance and averaged over utterances, which weights every utterance
equally; we also report corpus-level WER (total errors over total
reference words). CER ignores word boundaries, while WER counts a
wrongly split or merged word as an error. Differences between systems
are tested with a paired bootstrap over utterances (10,000 resamples)
\[27\]; on the call-centre set we also resample whole calls, since
several windows can come from one call. We report the bootstrap mean
difference and its 95% percentile interval; these means can differ from
the difference of the two printed scores in the last digit. References
written mainly in Latin script (Arabic letters fewer than twice the
Latin letters), which are translations rather than transcripts, are
excluded; this removes one SADA utterance and none elsewhere. Validation
figures quoted for model development were computed during development
with an earlier normaliser that kept Arabic punctuation; every test
figure in this paper uses the final normaliser, applied to saved
hypotheses of every system.

On SADA test the adapted system has the lowest WER of all systems:

slightly lower CER than the adapted system on this set (21.21% against

customer-service sample. Corpus-level WER gives the same ordering. At
the character level the picture differs. On Casablanca the CER gap is
0.91 points (interval -0.52 to 2.30), and on SaudiTalk it is 0.09
(interval -1.58 to 1.56). Both intervals include zero, and so does the
customer-service call’s (1.85 points, interval -0.44 to 4.00): in CER
the frozen system is statistically indistinguishable from Cohere on
every condition in Table 4 except telephone-coded SaudiTalk, where it is
2.96 points worse. The frozen encoder therefore recovers most of the
letters but fewer whole words. Two factors likely contribute: CER
ignores word boundaries, which the 4-gram model only partly restores,
and Cohere’s decoder carries a much stronger implicit language model
than our 4-gram.

The language model lowers WER on every set but raises CER on every one
of them. Without it, our reader’s CER on Casablanca is already below
Cohere’s, at 19.93% against 20.35% on clean audio and 23.36% against
24.38% on telephone audio. On SaudiTalk it is 28.61% against Cohere’s
29.51% on clean audio. The 4-gram model thus repairs word boundaries but
sometimes replaces a correctly spelled dialect word with a more frequent
one. A user who needs letters rather than words is better served by
greedy decoding.

The customer-service sample is the only public set in our domain, and it
shows the largest WER gap. It contains a single speaker, however, so its
intervals reflect only within-call variation. It should not be read as
evidence about call centres in general.

### 5.6 Which Layers Does the Reader Use?

Figure 5: Learned layer-mix weights of the frozen telephone-SADA reader.

Figure 5 plots the weights. The learned weights of Eq. (1) for the
telephone-SADA reader are 25.6% for layer 6, 7.8% for layer 8, 21.8% for
layer 10, 25.9% for layer 11, 9.5% for layer 12, and 9.4% for the
projected output. Layers 6, 10 and 11 together receive more than two
thirds of the weight. These weights should not be read as layer
importance. Each layer has its own normalisation and projection, and
these can absorb a scale factor. Only the six listed representations
were offered to the mix, so a single-layer probe of all twelve layers is
needed before any conclusion is drawn about where the encoder holds
transcription-relevant detail.

### 5.7 Search or Model?

Could better re-ranking close the call-centre gap? We computed the
oracle WER of a wider beam (128 hypotheses, same language model), that
is, the lowest WER among the top $`N`$ hypotheses of each window. The
top hypothesis of this wider search scores 47.63%, slightly different
from the 47.20% of the beam-64 system. The oracle falls to 46.03% for
the top 8 and 45.10% for the top 128, and pooling the beams with and
without the language model gives 44.92%. Even a perfect re-ranker would
stay above Cohere’s 37.62%. The same holds on the 33 windows with
cloud-recogniser references, which Cohere did not draft: there the
top-128 oracle is 34.97%, against 30.88% for Cohere. The gap therefore
lies in the acoustic model and its candidates, not in search.

### 5.8 Speed and Compute Cost

Table 14 reports throughput measured on identical audio, one system at a
time on an otherwise idle GPU, with batches sorted by length so that no
system is penalised by padding; on the GPU both systems use batches of
16, and Cohere is also shown with 32. All systems run in PyTorch without
further inference optimisation. The encoder on its own processes audio
at 348 times real time on the GPU; the complete pipeline with greedy
decoding, including feature extraction and the reader, runs at 257 times
real time and needs 1.49 GiB of GPU memory. At the same batch size,
Cohere Transcribe Arabic runs at 157 times real time with 4.76 GiB, so
the frozen-encoder system is 1.6 times faster and uses 3.2 times less
memory. The speed advantage on the GPU is modest, however: with a larger
batch Cohere reaches 222 times real time, because its autoregressive
decoder produces few tokens for short utterances and parallelises well.

On CPU, often the deployment target in call centres, the difference is
larger. With 16 threads, the frozen-encoder system runs at 31.10 times
real time with greedy decoding, which is 3.4 times faster than Cohere
(9.20) and 1.4 times faster than the 0.6B Nemotron model with greedy
decoding (22.60). The accuracy figures above rely on 4-gram beam search,
which adds work on the CPU: one process decodes at 84 times real time,
or about 12 core-hours per 1,000 hours of audio, though utterances can
be decoded in parallel. Nemotron’s batched GPU inference failed with a
memory-access error on the audio of Table 14, so only its CPU figures
appear there; it was timed on the GPU on different audio in Section 5.9.

### 5.9 Fast Joint Decoding

Joint CTC/attention search is what gives the adapted system its
accuracy, but our first implementation ran at only 1.7 times real time,
because every search step issued many small GPU operations and scored
CTC prefixes frame by frame in Python. We rewrote it in six steps,
checking after each step how many transcripts changed: CTC prefix scores
are computed by a compiled routine; the language model is queried only
for the candidates that survive the attention pre-beam; the attention
decoder keeps a key/value cache and projects the encoder memory once per
utterance; one decoder call serves all utterances of a batch at every
step; the decoder runs in 16-bit floating point, which the GPU executes
natively, instead of bfloat16, which it emulates; and the encoder
processes length-sorted batches rather than one segment at a time.
Batching the search across utterances left all 60 checked transcripts
unchanged, and half precision changed two of 198.

## 6 Discussion

#### What the frozen encoder can do

The speech encoder that Gemma 4 and EmbeddingGemma 2 share stays frozen
and is never adapted to Arabic transcription. A small reader on top of
it reaches a CER statistically indistinguishable from that of a 2B
dedicated recogniser on five of the six human-labelled conditions we
tested (Table 4). On real call-centre audio, its WER is not
significantly different from that of a 0.6B recogniser fine-tuned on
SADA, although its CER is clearly worse. Only 22.6M parameters are
trained, so adapting to a new domain is cheap. A deployment that already
runs Gemma 4 or EmbeddingGemma 2 can add transcription without loading a
second large encoder. The system is also cheaper to run. It is about 3.4
times faster than the 2B recogniser on CPU, with 3.2 times less GPU
memory.

#### What adaptation adds

Adding 3.1M adapter parameters, sub-word units, an attention decoder and
joint decoding makes the system statistically indistinguishable from the
2B recogniser in WER on the customer-service call and on clean Emirati
speech. On telephone-coded Emirati speech it is ahead of that
recogniser. It also yields a significantly lower CER on all six
conditions of the further human-labelled sets. Even the frozen system’s
128-best oracle stayed above Cohere’s WER on the call-centre set. The
adapted system’s 16-best lists, by contrast, contain many such
transcripts, so better rescoring is a natural next step.

#### What it cannot yet do

On long-form SaudiTalk speech, the adapted system still trails the
dedicated recogniser in WER, although its CER there is lower. Our
readers are bidirectional. Unlike Nemotron, neither system streams, even
though the encoder is causal.

#### Limitations

Every configuration was trained once, so we have no estimate of seed
variance. The adapted system’s training and language-model data also
differ from the frozen system’s, which means its gains cannot be
attributed to the encoder adapters alone. Its decoding weights were
tuned at an earlier stage on validation calls whose machine labels
favour CTC over joint search, the opposite of every human-labelled set;
a validation set with human labels would be a better guide. We did not
compare against other frozen encoders, such as the Whisper or w2v-BERT
encoders, using the same reader, nor against the full Gemma 4 model used
as a recogniser. Such controls would show how much of the result is
specific to this encoder. The call-centre training labels are machine
transcripts from two systems, one of which is the strongest baseline, so
the reader is partly distilled from it. The call-centre references are
mostly unedited machine drafts, as Section 4 explains, and the windows
were selected to be hard. The blind call-centre sample was corrected by
one annotator, the author, and is small. The only public
customer-service set with original human references is a single call, so
the adapted system’s results there rest on one speaker. The adapted
system also trains on voicebot calls with machine labels. Telephone
conditions are simulated with G.711 $`\mu`$-law only, whereas real
networks add other codecs, packet loss and echo. The SADA and Casablanca
licences forbid commercial use, so the SADA-trained readers and language
model are research artefacts only. Speed was measured on one GPU type
and one CPU type, and only our own system received inference
optimisation; Cohere and Nemotron were run with their standard
implementations.

## 7 Conclusion

To our knowledge, this is the first Arabic speech-recognition study of
the audio encoder shared by Gemma 4 and EmbeddingGemma 2, which we
verified to be identical in both releases except for the final
projection into the text model. Kept frozen and read by a
22.6M-parameter CTC model, the encoder supports Saudi and Gulf
recognition whose CER is statistically indistinguishable from that of a
2B dedicated recogniser on five of six human-labelled conditions. It
does so at several times the CPU speed and about a third of the GPU
memory. With 3.1M adapter parameters, a hybrid reader and joint
decoding, and still without changing a single encoder weight, the system
is statistically indistinguishable from the 2B recogniser in WER on a
public Saudi customer-service call and on clean Emirati speech. It is
significantly better on telephone-coded Emirati speech and has a
significantly lower CER on all six conditions of the further
human-labelled sets. In WER, it still trails on long-form Saudi speech.
It is also significantly better than a 0.6B recogniser fine-tuned on
SADA in WER on all six of those conditions. An optimised joint decoder
runs it at about 139 times real time on one GPU. On a blind,
human-corrected public call-centre sample it has the lowest WER of the
self-hostable systems among the 15 tested, while three commercial APIs
score better, significantly so only GPT-Transcribe. We further show that
references built by correcting machine drafts can inflate the apparent
quality of the drafting system. We recommend that such references be
reported by origin.
