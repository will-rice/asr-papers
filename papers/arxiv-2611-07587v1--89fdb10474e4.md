# Fine-Tuning a Transducer for Impaired Speech: Where the Gains Appear and What They Mean for Evaluation

Kien Nguyen    Tuan Dinh    Bao Phan Affiliation: Eloky
Email: [kien@eloky.ai](mailto:)    tuanad121@gmail.com    bao@eloky.ai

###### Abstract

Speech recognition still works poorly for many people whose speech is
affected by conditions such as ALS, Parkinson’s disease, cerebral palsy,
Down syndrome or stroke. We took part in the Speech Accessibility
Project Challenge 2 (SAPC2) with a public speech recogniser that we
fine-tuned on the challenge data. Our best entry scored 7.81% character
error rate on Test1, the first of two hidden test sets (official
baseline 15.26%). Our live entries ran on CPUs with a combined median
latency of about 0.9 seconds at 7.96–8.07% error. On new speakers,
fine-tuning reduced errors most for the speakers that an off-the-shelf
recogniser finds hardest to understand. Sentences already seen in
training had far lower error rates than new ones (2.58% vs 6.42%), a
larger difference than being a familiar speaker made. We close with
lessons for evaluating such systems.

## 1 Introduction

Voice interfaces are now everywhere, yet they make far more mistakes for
people with speech impairments, who may need them most. A leaderboard
tells us which system won, but not what a team building a product for
these users needs to know. We use our SAPC2 entry to study four
questions:

1.  1.  How much does fine-tuning a public model reduce errors, for which
        conditions and at which severity?
2.  2.  How do familiar speakers and familiar sentences relate to accuracy?
3.  3.  Can models of this kind serve both offline and live use on ordinary
        computers?
4.  4.  What general lessons did we learn about evaluating improvements?

We report _character error rate_ (CER): the share of characters that are
wrong after the official text normalisation. Lower is better. Tables
also give _word error rate_ (WER), the same measure counted in words.

Background. Early collections of impaired speech, such as UASpeech (Kim
et al., 2008) and TORGO (Rudzicz et al., 2012), are small and mostly
contain isolated words. Project Euphonia (MacDonald et al., 2021) and
the Speech Accessibility Project (SAP) (Hasegawa-Johnson et al., 2024)
collected speech from hundreds to thousands of people. SAP’s recordings
come from people with Parkinson’s disease, ALS, cerebral palsy, Down
syndrome and stroke, and its challenges (Zheng et al., 2025; SAPC2
organisers, 2026) measure accuracy on speakers who are not in the
training data. SAPC2 has two tracks: Track 1 scores offline
transcription, and Track 2 scores live (streaming) transcription on both
accuracy and speed.

## 2 Data and how we measured

Data. Each SAP recording is one prompt: a short command, a read sentence
or an answer to a question. As the challenge allows (SAPC2 organisers,
2026), we trained on the challenge training set together with the
training and development parts of the SAP research data release: about
950 hours from over 1,100 speakers. Our later entries also trained on
the challenge’s labelled development speakers. We used no speech from
outside SAP and no synthetic speech. The organisers’ published speaker
counts for the hidden test sets (both combined) are ALS 66, cerebral
palsy 58, Down syndrome 32, stroke 24 and Parkinson’s disease 13 (SAPC2
organisers, 2026).

Evaluation sets. We split the challenge’s labelled development set by
speaker and used four sets:

- •
  Selection panel: 41 speakers, 4,510 recordings, examined only through
  aggregate scores.
- •
  Former sealed group: 83 speakers, kept sealed at first and later added
  to training.
- •
  Held-back recordings of 300 training speakers, used only for the
  familiarity study (Section 4.3).
- •
  Test1: the official hidden test set. Entries trained on all labelled
  speakers can only be judged here.

The severity and familiarity analyses use all labelled recordings of the
124 new speakers (47,929 recordings), including the many extra
recordings of repeated prompts in the challenge’s development data, so
most of these recordings are sentences that also appear in training. All
scores use the official scoring tool. The ranges in our tables are 95%
intervals from resampling _speakers_ 2,000 times (“speaker intervals”),
because a product meets new speakers, not just new recordings. We never
accessed the second hidden test set and never adapted to test audio.

## 3 Our system in brief

We kept the system simple and built it from public parts.

- •
  Starting model. We compared several public recognisers and chose
  Parakeet TDT 1.1B (NVIDIA, 2024; Rekesh et al., 2023; Xu et al.,
  2023), a 1.1-billion-parameter model whose decoder also predicts how
  long each token lasts (“TDT”).
- •
  Fine-tuning. We fine-tuned the whole model on the impaired-speech data
  with NeMo (Kuchaiev et al., 2019).
- •
  Averaging. We averaged the weights of fine-tuned models (Wortsman
  et al., 2022). The result is one model, so this adds no cost when the
  system runs.
- •
  Live use. Our live entries run fine-tuned models of the same kind on
  CPUs.

### 4.2 Accuracy by severity

We grouped the 124 new speakers by how well an off-the-shelf recogniser
(Whisper) understands them, and scored every model on the same speakers
and recordings. This grouping is not a clinical severity rating. We
compare against the _other_ recogniser, the original Parakeet model
before fine-tuning, since grouping by Whisper’s own errors would bias
Whisper’s numbers. “Fine-tuned” is the single fine-tuned analysis model
of Section 4.3, not our best entry.

Table 3 leads with new sentences, the cleaner comparison. There, the
share of errors removed by fine-tuning grows with severity: 20% in the
mildest group, 27% and 36% in the middle groups, and 41% in the most
severe, where CER falls from 51.9% to 30.6%. In the mildest group the
intervals overlap. Over all recordings, which are mostly known
sentences, the original model’s error rate rises from 2.8% to 56% across
the groups, while the fine-tuned model’s rises from 1.2% to 16%.

### 4.3 Familiar speakers and familiar sentences

SAP prompts repeat across speakers, so a test recording may share its
speaker, its sentence, or both with training data. To study this, we
trained one analysis model with a single fine-tuning run while holding
back some recordings of 300 training speakers. This gives four groups:
(1) known speaker, known sentence; (2) known speaker, new sentence;
(3) new speaker, known sentence; (4) new speaker, new sentence. Group 1
is further split into (1a) the same person said this sentence in
training and (1b) only other people did. The new speakers are the 124
from the selection panel and the former sealed group. A sentence counts
as known if its text, ignoring case and punctuation, appears in
training. Each speaker can appear in several groups.

| Group (speaker / sentence) | Rec.   | Spk. | Fine-tuned          | Parakeet               | Whisper                |
| -------------------------- | ------ | ---- | ------------------- | ---------------------- | ---------------------- |
|                            |        |      | (analysis)          | original               | off the shelf          |
| 1: known / known           | 2,362  | 300  | 1.45 \[1.05, 1.87\] | 13.87 \[11.84, 16.11\] | 13.07 \[11.07, 15.19\] |
| 1a: same speaker           | 1,105  | 293  | 0.75 \[0.47, 1.08\] | 12.90 \[10.79, 15.29\] | 12.29 \[10.07, 14.72\] |
| 1b: other speakers only    | 1,257  | 300  | 1.86 \[1.38, 2.44\] | 14.45 \[12.35, 16.77\] | 13.54 \[11.51, 15.74\] |
| 2: known / new             | 1,500  | 300  | 5.02 \[4.34, 5.78\] | 9.32 \[7.84, 11.01\]   | 8.67 \[7.50, 9.95\]    |
| 3: new / known             | 37,281 | 124  | 2.58 \[1.71, 3.60\] | 16.18 \[12.68, 20.03\] | 15.20 \[11.90, 18.86\] |
| 4: new / new               | 10,648 | 124  | 6.42 \[5.26, 7.96\] | 9.89 \[7.90, 12.52\]   | 11.88 \[9.77, 14.49\]  |

Table 4: CER (%) by group, with 95% speaker intervals. Groups are
labelled speaker / sentence. Group 1 combines 1a (the same speaker said
the sentence in training) and 1b (only other speakers did). “Fine-tuned”
is the analysis model; “Parakeet” is Parakeet TDT 1.1B before
fine-tuning; “Whisper” is Whisper large-v3 off the shelf. “Known” refers
to the analysis model’s training data; for the other two models these
are simply the same recordings.

Table 4 shows three observations.

- •
  Familiar sentences were linked to a larger difference than familiar
  speakers. For new speakers, CER is 2.58% on known sentences against
  6.42% on new ones. A known speaker saying a new sentence has 5.02%.
  Known sentences are not easy in themselves: the original Parakeet
  model does _worse_ on them (16.18%) than on new ones (9.89%), so the
  low error comes from training on them. The groups also differ in
  speakers, prompt types and length, so this is an association, not a
  causal effect.
- •
  A person’s own sentences had the lowest error rate. CER was 0.75% on
  held-back recordings of sentences the same speaker had said in
  training. For the 30 held-back speakers with the highest
  analysis-model CER, it was 5.84% on such sentences against 29.03% on
  new sentences; because these speakers were chosen by this model’s own
  errors, the contrast is exaggerated.
- •
  Part of the gap is length. Known prompts are often short commands.
  After matching recording lengths, known sentences still have about 1.7
  times fewer errors than new ones (4.89% vs 8.32%), down from about 2.5
  times.

Evaluations that reuse training prompts may therefore show lower error
rates than a system achieves on new text.

### 4.4 Live transcription on CPUs

Track 2 reports two latencies: the time until the first word appears and
stays, and the time from the end of speech to the final text. Our live
entries run fine-tuned models of the same kind as our offline entries,
on CPUs without a GPU.

| ------------- | ------- | --------------------- | -------------- | ------- |
| Entry 1       | 8.05    | 7.35                  | 1.20           | 8.56    |
| Entry 2       | 7.98    | 0.82                  | 1.51           | 2.34    |
| Entry 3       | 8.02    | 0.41                  | 0.53           | 0.94    |
| Entry 4       | 7.96    | 0.41                  | 0.52           | 0.93    |
| Entry 5       | 7.97    | 0.42                  | 0.50           | 0.92    |
| Entry 6       | 8.07    | 0.42                  | 0.44           | 0.85    |

Table 5: Our six scored Track 2 entries on Test1, as reported by the
organisers: CER and the two median latencies in seconds. “Sum” adds the

In the challenge’s CPU evaluation (Table 5), the sum of the two median
latencies fell from 8.6 seconds for our first entry to between 0.85 and
0.93 seconds for the later ones. Live CER was 7.96–8.07%, close to our
offline entries (7.81–8.30%). The fastest entry was also the least
accurate, so speed and accuracy still trade off slightly.

### 4.5 Lessons for evaluating improvements

We tested many other changes. Most did not show consistent improvements,
and four general lessons stand out.

- •
  Count speakers, not recordings. On a 41-speaker panel, many measured
  gains were smaller than the speaker-level uncertainty, and those
  rarely held on other speakers or on Test1.
- •
  Check the hardest speakers separately. Several changes that were
  neutral overall made results worse for slow or severely affected
  speakers.
- •
  Fix the rules before looking. Setting pass/fail rules in advance, on
  panels separate from the selection panel, stopped us from adopting
  changes that only fit one panel.
- •
  Development panels are optimistic. Test1 error was about 1.29 times
  selection-panel error, partly because a small panel has few of the
  hardest speakers.

## 5 Implications

- •
  Fine-tuning substantially reduced character errors, most of all for
  the hardest-to-understand speakers. Whether the remaining error rates
  are usable in practice is untested.
- •
  Personal recordings may help. The low error rate on people’s own
  repeated sentences suggests that recordings from the user could be
  valuable, in line with earlier work on personalised models (Shor
  et al., 2019; Green et al., 2021; Tobin and Tomanek, 2022). This is a
  hypothesis, not a result of this paper.
- •
  Report accuracy in parts. Results by condition, by severity and by
  whether the sentence was known, with speaker-level uncertainty, are
  more informative than a single number.

## 6 Limitations and ethics

The selection panel has only 41 speakers (four with stroke), so
per-condition intervals are wide. The severity and familiarity analyses
use a single fine-tuned model, not our best entry. Severity is measured
by an off-the-shelf recogniser’s errors, not by a clinical rating.
Known-speaker test recordings come from the same sessions as that
speaker’s training recordings, which may overstate the speaker effect.
The hidden test sets report only overall scores, so we cannot break them
down by condition.

All speech comes from the SAP corpus under its data use terms. We report
only aggregate numbers: no audio, transcripts or individual recordings
appear here. Recognition is still much less accurate for some people,
especially with severe cerebral palsy, Down syndrome or stroke, so such
systems should support, not replace, human communication partners, and
should be evaluated with the people who will use them.

## 7 Conclusion

Fine-tuning a public speech recogniser and averaging fine-tuned models
roughly halved errors on our 41-speaker selection panel and gave 7.81%
CER on Test1, against 15.26% for the official baseline. In a separate
single-model analysis, the share of errors removed was largest for the
hardest-to-understand speakers. Live entries of the same kind ran on
CPUs with about 0.9 seconds of combined median latency at similar
accuracy. Having the sentence in training was linked to a larger drop in
errors than having the speaker in training, which matters for how such
systems are evaluated.
