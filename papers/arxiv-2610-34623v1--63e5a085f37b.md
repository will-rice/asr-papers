###### Abstract

Text injection trains speech recognition models on unpaired text
alongside paired audio. We feed the speech encoder synthetic input
derived from text so the whole model learns from it. Previously, we
synthesized this input with a frozen text-to-speech (TTS) model. Here, a
simple lookup table replaces the TTS model: a mean log-mel vector (or
one-hot encoding) per phoneme, repeated for a random duration drawn from
alignment statistics, with silence between words. Training is 1.8 times
faster than with the neural TTS, achieving comparable word error rates.
With an external language model, our system reaches
1.36 / 2.90 / 1.52 / 3.24 % WER on LibriSpeech {dev,test}-{clean,other},
to our knowledge state-of-the-art using only LibriSpeech paired data,
text, and lexicon. We analyze components of synthetic input (durations,
acoustic features), compare different HMM aligners, evaluate scaling on
the 25 000 h Loquacious dataset, and show that full-encoder injection
yields strong relative improvements compared to partial-encoder and
decoder methods. The code of the whole pipeline behind every number in
this paper is public:
[github.com/rwth-i6/returnn-experiments/tree/master/2026-text-injection](https://github.com/rwth-i6/returnn-experiments/tree/master/2026-text-injection)

## 1 Introduction

Text without audio exists in far larger amounts than transcribed audio.
Text corpora for language models exceed $`10^{13}`$ words \[11\],
whereas the largest weakly transcribed speech collection (680 000 h)
\[27\] contains fewer than $`10^{10}`$ words. Even at industrial scale,
transcribed speech is a small fraction of the text: 90 000 h (about
$`10^{9}`$ words) against 28 billion text sentences \[38\]. On the scale
of this paper, the 960 h of LibriSpeech carry 9.4 M words, while its
text corpus has 800 M \[23\]. Traditionally, text is used for an
external language model (LM) in search \[26\]. Text injection directly
trains the end-to-end model on unpaired text \[6, 29, 19\]. We inject
synthetic input derived from text at the speech encoder input, training
the whole model. Previously \[37\], a frozen TTS model synthesized this
input \[4\], yielding consistent gains but requiring TTS pretraining and
dominating training time. Here, a per-phoneme log-mel table replaces the
TTS model. The input enters where audio features enter, leaving the
model, losses, and search unchanged.

Text injection integrates unpaired text into ASR via decoder-side LMs
\[35, 19, 21, 12, 15\] or encoder-side modality matching, either
explicit via specific alignment losses \[7, 5, 34, 40, 10\] or implicit
via shared dynamics \[3, 2, 31, 33, 1, 39, 29, 30\]. While implicit
matching generally outperforms explicit matching, TTS synthesis
significantly outperforms both \[37, 28, 9, 12, 15\]. However, neural
TTS introduces severe system complexity and computational expense. To
overcome this, we propose a simplified synthetic acoustic approach,
replacing the neural TTS with a simple lookup table, allowing us to
easily scale down durations and accelerate ASR training.

The closest approach is the textogram family \[33, 9, 13, 8, 32, 30\],
where text enters the encoder as a symbol sequence, often masked. Our
input is instead acoustic: a mean log-mel vector per phoneme, held for a
duration drawn from alignment statistics. It takes the audio path with
SpecAugment, requiring no text encoder, masking, or extra losses.
Alignment-derived durations perform best (Section 3.2), though simpler
character textograms (fixed 4 frames) still retain 60 % of our gain
(Table 4). Previous textogram results report large relative gains but
rely on weak baselines (5.5–28 % WER) or out-of-domain text \[32, 8, 13,
30, 33, 9\]. Only \[32\] shares our strict condition of using solely
matched-domain text from scratch.

Our contributions are fourfold: (1) replacing neural TTS with a
synthesizer-free lookup table, accelerating training by 1.8$`\times`$;
(2) achieving a 12 % relative WER reduction on LibriSpeech, reaching
state-of-the-art for the constrained data case; (3) demonstrating
through ablations and textogram comparisons that simple duration and
acoustic models suffice for strong performance; and (4) comparing across
the literature, we show that full-encoder injection yields highly
competitive relative improvements over partial-encoder or decoder
methods.

## 2 Method

From a phoneme alignment of the paired audio, we extract a mean log-mel
vector and median duration $`m`$ per phoneme. To inject text, we map
sentences to phonemes via a lexicon. Each phoneme duration is sampled as
$`\operatorname{round}(m\cdot s\cdot\exp(\sigma\varepsilon))`$,
$`\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)`$, with scale $`s`$ and jitter
$`\sigma`$ (Table 3). We insert silence between words, repeat vectors
for their duration with linear interpolation at boundaries, and feed
this to the front-end exactly like real audio, applying SpecAugment.
Audio and text sentences are mixed randomly in batches, using the same
losses.

## 3 Experiments

### 3.1 Setup

We use LibriSpeech \[23\] (960 h paired, 800M-word LM corpus for
injection) with a pronunciation lexicon, and Loquacious \[24\] (25 000 h
English from five sources). Loquacious subsets include small (250 h),
medium (2 500 h), large (25 000 h), and a custom 1 000 h subset. We use
the large subset transcripts for text injection experiments.

Models use a 16-layer Conformer encoder and 6-layer Transformer decoder
(dimension 1024, 560M parameters; EncL24-DecL8 uses 24 and 8 layers). We
train with CTC+AED on 10k sentence pieces from 80-dim log-mel features
with a 6x subsampling front-end, using the Muon optimizer \[14\], packed
tensors and CUDA graphs on 4 GH200 GPUs. External LMs are standard
auto-regressive Transformers. The larger LibriSpeech model also uses
diffusion \[22\] and denoising \[15\] LMs.

For LibriSpeech we take the public phoneme alignments \[16, 17\] of the
Montreal Forced Aligner (MFA) \[18\], a speaker-adapted triphone
Gaussian-mixture HMM (GM-HMM) on MFCC features. Its pretrained HMM was
itself trained strictly on LibriSpeech, so no external or foreign audio
enters the tables; Loquacious is aligned with the same model. MFA relies
on a complex pipeline, so we also evaluate a simple, self-contained
aligner (Table 2): a monophone HMM with three states per phoneme and one
diagonal Gaussian per state, trained on the ASR log-mel features in
RETURNN \[36\]. Its state means directly form the log-mel table.

Decoding is single-pass without rescoring \[26, 15, 41\]. CTC, CTC+AED,
and CTC+LM use time-synchronous search, while CTC+AED+LM uses
label-sync. search over CTC prefix scores.

### 3.2 Results

#### Injection vs. baselines.

Table 1 compares text injection to audio-only baselines on LibriSpeech.
The table reduces WER by 12 % relative on test-other (4.16 to 3.66). The
gain persists with an external LM (3.38 to 2.93) and is not recovered by
simply doubling audio epochs. The larger model sees similar gains (3.71
to 3.26, and 2.90 with LM). The denoising LM slightly outperforms the
std. LM on test-other (3.22 vs. 3.24). The diffusion LM underperforms
CTC+LM (3.81 vs. 3.33) but is much faster, requiring only a single
non-autoregressive denoising step \[22\]. Compared to the GlowTTS
injection \[37\], our lookup table matches the WER while training 1.8
times faster (130 h vs. 235 h on 4 GPUs in an earlier implementation).

#### Durations.

Table 3 varies the duration model. The log-mel table is robust to
temporal compression. Scaling durations down to 0.7x (5.9 frames per
phoneme vs. 8.4 in the alignment) performs best (3.66 on test-other).
Even collapsing durations to a constant 6 frames per phoneme (60 ms)
incurs only a minor penalty (3.73). In contrast, neural TTS appears less
robust to larger modifications of its generated durations. While an
exact matched comparison is unavailable, randomly scaling GlowTTS \[37\]
durations by factors sampled uniformly between 0.2 and 0.5 degraded
test-other by roughly +0.5 % absolute WER.

Table 4 compares acoustic features. The acoustic features matter little:

#### Loquacious.

Table 8 repeats the comparison on Loquacious at different scales. The
relative gain depends strongly on the text-to-audio ratio and the
absolute amount of paired data. LibriSpeech achieved a 12 % relative
gain using an 86:1 text-to-audio ratio (960 h audio vs. an 800M-word
text corpus). On Loquacious, we scale the paired audio while keeping the
injected text fixed (the 25 000 h transcripts). The relative gain
shrinks predictably as the text-to-audio ratio drops: 24 % at 250 h
(100:1 ratio), 14 % at 1 000 h (25:1), 8 % at 2 500 h (10:1), and 3 % at
25 000 h (1:1 ratio). These gains are against the audio-only baseline at
the same number of audio passes. The controls with twice the passes show
that the medium and large baselines are not converged: there, twice the
audio passes give as much as the injection at a similar update count,
while on medium1k they give nothing (8.07 vs. 8.04 on dev) and on
LibriSpeech twice the epochs recover only 0.14 of the 0.45 gain
(Table 1). Crucially, at matched compute, text injection matches or
exceeds extended audio training, providing an efficient alternative
without extra audio passes.

## 4 Conclusions

A simple lookup table with durations from a phoneme alignment can
replace neural TTS for text injection. Training is 1.8 times faster and
requires no synthesizer. On LibriSpeech, it yields a 12 % relative WER
reduction over the baseline, reaching 1.36 / 2.90 / 1.52 / 3.24 % with a
large model. The duration statistics are the most critical component,
while the specific acoustic features matter less. The best results rely
on a phoneme lexicon. While characters retain 60 % of the gain without
one, generating robust inputs directly from subword alignments requires
further investigation. Finally, scaling experiments on Loquacious
confirm these trends: the overall relative gains scale predictably with
the text-to-audio ratio and paired data amount, the method is robust,
and acoustic features generalize across datasets without retraining.
Comparing relative gains across the literature (Figure 1) suggests a
clear trend: at equal scale, injecting text at the input to train the
full encoder generally outperforms injecting into a shared upper encoder
or the decoder alone.
