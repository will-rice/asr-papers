# Zero-Shot Voice Cloning for Synthetic Data Augmentation in Children’s ASR

Mohammad Mohsen Goodarzi, Stefan Wabnik, Matthias Stennes,  
Stephanus Volke, Alexander Schwarz Affiliation: Fraunhofer Institute for
Digital Media Technology IDMT,  
Oldenburg Branch for Hearing, Speech and Audio Technology HSA,
Oldenburg, Germany  
{mohammadmohsen.goodarzi, stefan.wabnik, matthias.stennes,  
stephanus.volke, alexander.schwarz}@idmt.fraunhofer.de Affiliation: 

###### Abstract

Children’s automatic speech recognition (ASR) lags behind adult ASR due
to the acoustic mismatch between adult-trained acoustic models and
children’s speech, as well as the scarcity of labelled children’s speech
corpora. We investigate whether zero-shot text-to-speech (TTS) synthesis
with voice cloning, requiring only a short reference clip per speaker,
can serve as a practical substitute for real children’s speech when
fine-tuning large pre-trained ASR models. Using the OmniVoice
multilingual TTS system we synthesise up to 16 000 child-like utterances
from two English corpora (ENNI, clinical narrative samples, ages 7–12,
and MyST, conversational science-tutor speech, grades 3–5) and fine-tune
Whisper-small under different experimental conditions covering real
data, synthetic data, cross-corpus data, and all-data combination. A
systematic sensitivity analysis over speaker count, text-pool size, and
utterances per speaker reveals that synthetic data alone reduces word
error rate (WER) from a 19.8 % zero-shot baseline to 16.4 % on ENNI,
recovering approximately 30 % of the gap to real-data fine-tuning
(8.8 %). Combining synthetic with real data is essentially
indistinguishable from real-data-only fine-tuning (9.2 % vs. 8.8 %).
Speaker count plateaus at $`{\approx}40`$ speakers for cross-domain
transfer, while multi-speaker acoustic diversity per transcript, rather
than raw text coverage, emerges as the primary determinant of
cross-domain generalisation; out-of-vocabulary (OOV) rate proves to be a
poor proxy for WER given Whisper’s pre-trained language modelling
capacity.

## I Introduction

Automatic speech recognition systems have reached near-human performance
on adult read speech benchmarks \[1\], yet children’s ASR remains a
substantially harder problem. Children differ from adults in their
fundamental frequency (higher F0, broader range), formant frequencies,
speaking rate, and co-articulation patterns; they also exhibit more
disfluencies and reduced phonetic precision, all of which cause ASR
systems to produce higher word error rates on children’s speech relative
to text \[2, 3\]. At the same time, labelled children’s speech corpora
are orders of magnitude smaller than adult corpora: collecting
clinical-grade narrative samples requires specialist annotation, and
freely distributable children’s data is scarce due to privacy
regulations.

Large self-supervised and weakly-supervised models such as
wav2vec 2.0 \[4\] and Whisper \[1\] have dramatically reduced the data
requirements for adult ASR by providing powerful pre-trained
representations. When fine-tuned on even small children’s datasets they
show significant improvement \[5, 6\]. However, the adaptation gains are
proportional to the amount of in-domain fine-tuning data, and obtaining
more children’s speech remains costly.

A natural response is data augmentation via TTS synthesis. Prior work
explored trainable children’s TTS \[7\], voice conversion from adult
speech \[8\], and acoustic feature perturbation \[3\]. The recent
generation of zero-shot voice cloning models, systems that require only
a few seconds of reference audio at _inference_ time, with no
speaker-specific training or enrolment \[9, 10, 11\], opens a
qualitatively new possibility: extract reference clips directly from an
existing corpus, clone each speaker’s voice, and synthesise as much
additional text in that voice as desired, without training a separate
TTS system. We use the term “zero-shot” in the sense standard in the TTS
literature: the model is not trained or adapted on the target speaker;
the reference clip serves only as a conditioning prompt at synthesis
time.

We make the following contributions:

1.  1.  We present an end-to-end zero-shot voice cloning pipeline for
        children’s ASR data augmentation that requires only a single short
        reference clip per child speaker, with no TTS model retraining, and
        demonstrate its effectiveness using OmniVoice \[11\] across two
        structurally distinct English children’s corpora.
2.  2.  Through a comprehensive comparison of different training conditions
        spanning real-only, synthetic-only, cross-corpus, and combined
        configurations, we show that synthetic data alone recovers
        approximately 30 % of the gap to real-data fine-tuning, while
        combining synthetic and real data yields performance statistically
        indistinguishable from real-only fine-tuning.
3.  3.  We conduct a systematic sensitivity analysis over synthesis budget
        factors (speaker count 5–80, text-pool coverage 10–100 %, and
        utterances per speaker 25–200), revealing that multi-speaker
        acoustic coverage per transcript, rather than raw text diversity or
        speaker count alone, is the primary driver of cross-domain
        generalisation; that speaker count plateaus at approximately 40 for
        out-of-domain transfer; and that 50–100 utterances per speaker
        suffice for near-saturation in-domain performance.

## II Related Work

### II-A Children’s ASR

Early children’s ASR relied on acoustic model adaptation using
maximum-likelihood or feature-space transforms \[3\]. The CSLU Kids
corpus \[12\] and the My Science Tutor (MyST) corpus \[13\] provided the
first large-scale benchmarks. More recent work has focused on
fine-tuning self-supervised models on children’s speech: Jain et
al. \[5\] fine-tuned wav2vec 2.0 models on MyST and demonstrated large
WER reductions; Attia et al. \[6\] (Kid-Whisper) showed that fine-tuning
Whisper on MyST closes much of the adult–child gap. Singh et al. \[14\]
provided a causal analysis of the factors driving children’s ASR
degradation, quantifying the contributions of physiological, cognitive,
and extrinsic factors.

### II-B TTS-Based Data Augmentation for ASR

Rosenberg et al. \[15\] were among the first to demonstrate that
TTS-generated speech can improve ASR training, particularly for
low-resource conditions. Vásquez-Correa et al. \[16\] showed that
Whisper models can be domain-adapted using only synthetic speech. For
children’s ASR specifically, Zhang et al. \[7\] augmented child-like
speech via voice conversion and showed WER reductions for Whisper-based
systems. Rolland and Abad \[17\] demonstrated per-child personalisation
via TTS voice cloning, achieving significant WER reduction for 10 target
children. However, their approach requires enrolment speech from each
test child and produces one adapted model per child. In contrast, we
investigate whether voice cloning of training-set children, requiring
_no test-child enrolment_, can improve speaker-independent ASR for
arbitrary unseen children, and conduct the first systematic analysis of
the synthesis budget factors (speaker count, text diversity, and
utterances per speaker) that drive this improvement.

### II-C Zero-Shot TTS and Voice Cloning

Modern zero-shot TTS systems can clone a speaker’s voice from a 3–10 s
reference clip, enabling on-demand speaker expansion of any text corpus.
VITS \[18\] introduced a fully end-to-end variational approach that
underpins many subsequent systems. YourTTS \[9\] extended VITS to
zero-shot multi-speaker and zero-shot voice conversion settings.
VALL-E \[10\] proposed a neural codec language model that preserves
speaker emotion and environment from a short prompt. OmniVoice \[11\],
the system used in this work, is a diffusion language-model-based
architecture trained on 581 k hours of multilingual open data that
supports over 600 languages via a non-autoregressive full-codebook token
prediction scheme, achieving state-of-the-art speaker similarity and
intelligibility.

## III Methodology

### III-A Corpora

ENNI (Edmonton Narrative Norms Instrument) \[19\] is a clinical corpus
of English narrative uttered by children aged 7–12 using picture
sequences. We use only the control (TD) subset. After standard
train/development/test splits and filtering, we retain 10 328 training
utterances (9.5 h, 116 speakers), 1 257 development utterances (1.2 h,
14 speakers), and 1 278 test utterances (1.1 h, 15 speakers).

MyST (My Science Tutor) \[13\] is a large corpus of conversational
science tutoring sessions with elementary school children (grades 3–5).
We use 75 324 training utterances (167.8 h, 567 speakers), 11 963
development utterances (25.3 h), and 12 915 test utterances (27.9 h, 91
speakers).

### III-B Synthesis Pipeline

Persona extraction. For each corpus we select up to 80 training speakers
as _personas_ for voice cloning. For each persona we identify a single
reference clip: the utterance closest to 4 s duration that is at least
2 s long. The clip and its transcription form the persona’s voice seed.

Text normalisation. Source transcriptions are normalised via a shared
pipeline: CHAT annotation markup, disfluency markers, noise tokens
(xxx/yyy/www), and punctuation are removed; text is lowercased and
whitespace-normalised. Filler words (_um_, _uh_, _hmm_) are removed so
the TTS model does not produce scripted hesitations.

Source filtering. Records are excluded from the synthesis pool if: (a)
the source utterance exceeds 20 s, (b) the normalised text contains more
than 30 words, or (c) the text is a duplicate.

Synthesis. The filtered, deduplicated text pool is passed to
OmniVoice \[11\] (k2-fsa/OmniVoice, April 2026 release), which
conditions on each persona’s reference clip. We synthesise up to $`N`$
utterances per persona at 16 kHz, parallelised across four NVIDIA GPUs.

Post-synthesis filtering. Each clip is validated with a heuristic
audio–text plausibility check: utterances are rejected if the inferred
speaking rate falls outside 20–600 WPM, or if the same word repeats for
ten or more consecutive tokens. Surviving clips are indexed in JSONL
manifests split 80 %/10 %/10 % train/development/test by persona.
Default synthesis produces 16 000 training utterances (80 personas
$`\times`$ 200 utterances each).

### III-C ASR Model and Training

We fine-tune Whisper-small \[1\] (244 M parameters), with language set
to english and task=transcribe. Training uses the HuggingFace
Seq2SeqTrainer with: batch size 32, gradient accumulation 2 (effective
batch 64), learning rate $`10^{-5}`$, cosine schedule with 500 warm-up
steps, maximum 4 000 steps, FP16. Features are extracted at 16 kHz and
padded/truncated to 30 s. We use 4-GPU data-parallel training via
HuggingFace Accelerate.

### III-D Evaluation

WER is computed over normalised text (lowercase, punctuation stripped)
on four test sets: enni_test (real children, in-domain for ENNI-trained
systems), myst_test (real children, cross-domain), synthetic_enni_test
(4 000 synthesised utterances from ENNI personas), and
synthetic_myst_test (4 000 synthesised utterances from MyST personas).
Confidence intervals (CIs) are estimated via non-parametric bootstrap
resampling (1 000 samples, 95 % CI). We additionally report the
out-of-vocabulary (OOV) rate: the fraction of test-set word tokens
absent from the training vocabulary.

## IV Results and Analysis

### IV-A Real vs. Synthetic Data

Table II summarises the main experimental results. The zero-shot
Whisper-small baseline achieves 19.8 % WER on enni_test and 22.2 % on
myst_test. Fine-tuning on real ENNI data (E1) reduces enni_test WER to
8.8 % but shows no generalisation (MyST WER $`=21.7`$ %, nearly
unchanged from baseline), as the model specialises to ENNI vocabulary.
Synthetic ENNI data (E2) yields 16.4 % on enni_test: it bridges
approximately 30 % of the real-data gap while maintaining similar
myst_test WER (21.0 %).

Combining real and synthetic ENNI (E7) achieves 9.2 % on enni_test,
which is within the 95 % CI of real-only fine-tuning ($`8.8\pm 0.8`$ %),
and 20.6 % on myst_test, indicating that synthetic data can help in
generalisation without hurting in-domain performance. The all-data
condition (E9) achieves 8.5 % on enni_test and 14.8 % on myst_test, the
best overall result.

The cross-dataset experiments (E10–E11) are instructive: using MyST
speakers with ENNI text (E10) achieves 17.7 % on enni_test, while using
ENNI speakers with MyST text (E11) degrades to 24.5 %. This asymmetry
indicates that, in our setting, matching the text domain of the target
corpus contributes more than speaker voice identity to in-domain WER.

### IV-B Speaker Count Sensitivity

Speaker diversity shows diminishing returns for in-domain performance:
all speaker-count sensitivity (SA) conditions (5–80 speakers) land in
17.0–17.9 % on enni_test with overlapping CIs, indicating that text and
acoustic coverage from 16 k utterances dominates over speaker count for
the matched test corpus.

For cross-domain generalisation (myst_test), speaker count matters more:
SA_5 achieves $`22.6\pm 0.9`$ %, while SA_40 achieves $`20.8\pm 0.8`$ %,
a 1.8 pp improvement whose CIs do not overlap. SA_80 ($`21.3\pm 0.7`$ %)
is within CI of SA_40, confirming that going beyond 40 speakers offers
no additional benefit when total utterances are fixed.

### IV-C Text-Pool Sensitivity

We vary the fraction of ENNI training transcriptions available as
synthesis text prompts while fixing 80 speakers and 100 utterances per
speaker (8 000 utterances total). With only 10 % of the text pool (967
unique texts), TA_10 achieves the lowest WER on both enni_test (16.6 %)
and myst_test (20.0 %), despite having the highest OOV rates (4.3 % and
29.0 % respectively). The TA_10 vs. TA_100 gap on myst_test (20.0 vs.
20.9) is at the boundary of statistical significance (CIs barely
overlap). The likely cause is that each of the 967 texts was synthesised
by $`{\approx}8.3`$ different speakers on average (vs.
$`{\approx}1.5\times`$ repetition in TA_100), providing richer
multi-speaker acoustic variability per transcript, a form of implicit
multi-speaker data augmentation.

Increasing text budget beyond 10 % produces no consistent WER
improvement. OOV rate is a poor proxy for WER in this setting: TA_10 has
the highest OOV rate yet the best WER, because Whisper’s decoder has
broad pre-trained language modelling capacity.

### IV-D Utterances-per-Speaker Sensitivity

With 80 speakers and the full ENNI text pool, we vary the number of
synthesised utterances per persona: 25, 50, 100, 200. ENNI-Real WER
decreases rapidly from 18.4 % (25 utt/speaker) to 17.0 %
(50 utt/speaker), then plateaus at 17.0–17.5 % for 100–200 utt/speaker.
Synthesis of approximately 50–100 utterances per speaker saturates
in-domain WER improvement, making it a cost-effective operating point.
Adding more utterances for the same 80 speakers slightly hurts
out-of-domain generalisation, possibly reflecting increased over-fitting
to speaker-specific TTS artefacts.

### IV-E Speakers-per-Text Sensitivity

The speakers-per-text (SPT) analysis decouples speaker diversity from
text diversity: with 8 000 utterances and 80 speakers, we vary how many
speakers read the same text item. When each text is read by only 1
speaker (SPT_1, 83 % text pool), ENNI-Real WER is 16.8 % with an OOV of
1.6 %. When every text is read by all 80 speakers (SPT_80, 1 % text
pool), WER rises modestly to 17.5 % but OOV explodes to 18.8 %.

Crucially, myst_test WER does not increase with higher OOV: SPT_8 (10 %
text pool, OOV 29.0 %) achieves the best cross-domain WER of 19.8 %,
outperforming SPT_1 (83 % text pool, OOV 22.7 %) at 20.9 %. The OOV–WER
relationship on myst_test is not monotonic; it is weakly U-shaped, with
a minimum near SPT_8. The results support the conclusion that
multi-speaker acoustic diversity per transcript is the primary driver of
cross-domain generalisation: having 8 speakers produce the same text
provides richer acoustic variation for the encoder to learn from. Beyond
SPT_8, further reduction in text pool (SPT_16, SPT_80) yields
diminishing returns.

### IV-F Synthetic vs. Real Evaluation Gap

An important confound is that evaluation on _synthetic_ test sets (4 000
clips from held-out personas) yields much lower WER than real test sets,
e.g. E2 gives 2.1 % on synthetic_enni_test but 16.4 % on real enni_test.
As expected, evaluating a synthetically trained model on matched
synthetic data does not suffer a domain gap; we report these numbers
mainly to quantify the magnitude of that mismatch. The synthetic-to-real
gap decreases when real data is added: E7 gives 1.9 % synthetic vs.
9.2 % real. This confirms that synthetic evaluation benchmarks
substantially overestimate ASR quality on real children’s speech and
should always be paired with real-children evaluations.

## V Conclusion

We have demonstrated that zero-shot voice cloning with OmniVoice
provides a practical path to synthesising children’s speech for ASR
fine-tuning from as few as 5 speaker reference clips (one clip per
speaker). Synthetic data alone recovers $`{\approx}30`$ % of the gap to
real-data fine-tuning; combined with real data it is statistically
indistinguishable from real-only fine-tuning on ENNI. The sensitivity
analysis yields three actionable insights:

- •
  Speaker count: plateau at $`{\approx}40`$ speakers for cross-domain
  generalisation; 5 speakers suffice for in-domain saturation.
- •
  Acoustic diversity per transcript: the primary driver of cross-domain
  generalisation. Having multiple speakers produce each text
  ($`{\approx}8`$ speakers/text) yields better out-of-domain transfer
  than maximising text coverage alone. OOV rate is a poor proxy for WER
  given Whisper’s pre-trained language modelling capacity.
- •
  Utterance count: 50–100 utterances per speaker suffice for
  near-saturation in-domain WER.

A remaining limitation is the real-to-synthetic gap: synthesised
children’s voices, while intelligible, differ from actual children in
prosody and articulatory characteristics, and our results confirm that
models trained solely on synthetic data still under-perform real-data
systems. Future work should explore TTS models explicitly tuned for
children’s voice characteristics, and extend the analysis to clinical
populations where data scarcity is most acute.
