###### Abstract

Audio-visual speech separation methods aim to integrate different modalities to generate high-quality separated speech, thereby enhancing the performance of downstream tasks such as speech recognition. Most existing state-of-the-art (SOTA) models operate in the time domain. However, their overly simplistic approach to modeling acoustic features often necessitates larger and more computationally intensive models in order to achieve SOTA performance. In this paper, we present a novel time-frequency domain audio-visual speech separation method: Recurrent Time-Frequency Separation Network (RTFS-Net), which applies its algorithms on the complex time-frequency bins yielded by the Short-Time Fourier Transform. We model and capture the time and frequency dimensions of the audio independently using a multi-layered RNN along each dimension. Furthermore, we introduce a unique attention-based fusion technique for the efficient integration of audio and visual information, and a new mask separation approach that takes advantage of the intrinsic spectral nature of the acoustic features for a clearer separation. RTFS-Net outperforms the prior SOTA method in both inference speed and separation quality while reducing the number of parameters by 90% and MACs by 83%. This is the first time-frequency domain audio-visual speech separation method to outperform all contemporary time-domain counterparts.

## 1 Introduction

The ‘cocktail party problem’ (Bronkhorst, [2000](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib5); Haykin & Chen, [2005](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib11); Cherry, [2005](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib7)) describes our ability to focus on a single speaker’s voice amidst numerous overlapping voices in a noisy environment. While humans effortlessly tackle this problem, replicating this ability in machines remains a longstanding challenge in the field of signal-processing. Audio-only Speech Separation (AOSS) methods (Luo & Mesgarani, [2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib24); Luo et al., [2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib27); Subakan et al., [2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib33)), solely utilizing the mixed-speaker audio signal, face limitations in scenarios with strong background noise, reverberation, or heavy voice overlap. To overcome these issues, researchers turned to a multi-modal approach: Audio-visual Speech Separation (AVSS) (Gao & Grauman, [2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib10); Lee et al., [2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib16); Li et al., [2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib18)). AVSS methods integrate additional visual cues into the paradigm and can be generally split into two main classifications: Time-domain (T-domain) and Time-Frequency domain (TF-domain) methods, each with their own benefits and challenges.

T-domain methods (Wu et al., [2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib39); Li et al., [2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib18); Martel et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib28); Lin et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib20)) work on the long, uncompressed and high-dimensional audio features returned by the 1D convolutional encoder design proposed by Luo & Mesgarani ([2018](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib23)). This approach facilitates fine-grained, high quality audio separation, but the high parameter count and computational complexity leads to extended training periods, intensive GPU usage and slow inference speeds. On the other hand, TF-domain methods (Afouras et al., [2018a](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib1); Alfouras et al., [2018](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib4); Gao & Grauman, [2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib10)) apply their algorithms on the complex 2D representation yielded by the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT). Typically, the STFT uses large windows and hop lengths to compress the data, resulting in more computationally efficient separation methods. However, from a historical perspective, all TF-domain methods have been substantially outperformed by T-domain methods. Based on our research, this gap in performance stems from three critical factors.

Firstly, while some attempts have been made (Afouras et al., [2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib3); Lee et al., [2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib16)) to model amplitude and phase separately, no TF-domain AVSS methods explore the independent and tailored modelling of the two acoustic dimensions (time and frequency) in order to exploit this domain’s advantage over T-domain methods. Indeed, recent research by Luo & Yu ([2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib25)) and Wang et al. ([2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib38)) in the AOSS domain have capitalized on this advantage and outperformed their T-domain counterparts by large margins. However, their usage of large LSTM (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, [1997](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib13)) and transformer (Vaswani et al., [2017](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib35)) architectures leads to a heavy computational burden, making them an unattractive solution in the AVSS space. Secondly, while existing AVSS studies (Li et al., [2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib18); Lin et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib20)) have explored various audio-visual fusion strategies, they neglect using visual features from multiple receptive fields to increase model performance. This type of visual information serves as important ā priōrī knowledge crucial for accurately extracting the target speaker’s voice. Thirdly, TF-domain AVSS studies (Afouras et al., [2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib3); Lee et al., [2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib16); Gao & Grauman, [2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib10)) often overlook the underlying complex nature of the features, and hence lose critical amplitude and phase information when extracting the target speaker’s voice from the audio mixture. This degrades the reconstruction performance of the inverse STFT (iSTFT) and leads to poorer model performance.

In this work, we propose a novel TF-domain AVSS method: Recursive Time-Frequency Separation Network (RTFS-Net, Figure [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#S3.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 3 Methods ‣ RTFS-Net: Recurrent Time-Frequency Modelling for Efficient Audio-Visual Speech Separation")) that provides a computationally efficient solution to the cocktail party problem using an STFT based audio encoder, a high-fidelity pretrained video encoder and a series of recursive RTFS Blocks to perform target speaker extraction. Our contributions are threefold:

1.  1.  Our RTFS Blocks tackle the first issue by projecting the features to a compressed subspace. There, we explicitly model both acoustic dimensions individually, then in tandem before subsequently applying an attentional mechanism (TF-AR) to restore the dimensions with minimal information loss, allowing us to reap the benefits of independent time-frequency processing without bearing a substantial computational cost.
2.  2.  Our Cross-dimensional Attention Fusion (CAF) Block provides a low parameter, computationally efficient solution to the second issue by aggregating the multi-modal information through a multi-head attention strategy in order to optimally fuse the visual cues of the target speaker into the audio features, facilitating high quality separation.
3.  3.  Our Spectral Source Separation ($`S^{3}`$) Block addresses the third issue by explicitly reconstructing the plural features of the target speaker, achieving a higher quality separation without increasing computational cost.

We conducted comprehensive experimental evaluations on three widely used datasets: LRS2 (Afouras et al., [2018a](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib1)), LRS3 (Afouras et al., [2018b](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib2)) and VoxCeleb2 (Chung et al., [2018](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib8)), to demonstrate the value of each contribution. To the best of our knowledge, RTFS-Net is the first TF-domain AVSS method to outperform all contemporary T-domain methods, achieving this while also exhibiting a clear advantage by reducing computational complexity by 83% and reducing the parameter count by 90%. We additionally provide a Web page where sample results can be listened to, and our code will be open-sourced after publication for reproducibility purposes.

## 2 Related work

Audio-only speech separation. Modern deep learning advances introduced neural networks for speaker-agnostic speech separation, with Conv-TasNet (Luo & Mesgarani, [2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib24)) and DualPathRNN (Luo et al., [2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib27)) making significant T-domain breakthroughs. However, T-domain methods (Luo & Mesgarani, [2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib24); Luo et al., [2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib27)) often exhibit a marked performance degradation in reverberant conditions, attributed to their neglect of explicit frequency-domain modeling. Consequently, recent research focuses on high-performance speech separation models in the TF-domain. For instance, TFPSNet (Yang et al., [2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib40)) incorporates the transformer from DPTNet (Chen et al., [2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib6)) to assimilate spectral-temporal information. TF-GridNet (Wang et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib38)) extends this by introducing a cross-frame self-attention module in order to achieve SOTA performance on the WSJ0-2mix (Hershey et al., [2016](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib12)). However, the inclusion of many LSTM and Transformer layers results in extremely high computational complexity, leading to increased training times and GPU memory requirements. Lightweight models like A-FRCNN (Hu et al., [2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib14)) and TDANet (Li et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib19)) strike a balance between performance and efficiency by using an encoder-decoder paradigm with recurrent connections and top-down attention. However, their separation prowess in noisy scenarios remains suboptimal compared to multi-modal approaches.

Audio-visual speech separation. Conventional AVSS statistical methods relied on knowledge-driven modeling (Loizou, [2013](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib21); Wang & Brown, [2006](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib37)). Early deep-learning explorations into AVSS mainly occurred in the TF-domain, focusing on tasks like amplitude and phase reconstruction for target speakers (Afouras et al., [2018a](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib1); Alfouras et al., [2018](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib4)). With the advent of AV-ConvTasNet (Wu et al., [2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib39)), it became a prevailing belief that T-domain AVSS methods consistently outperformed TF-domain methods. Notably, CTCNet (Li et al., [2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib18)), inspired by thalamic brain structures, introduced a unique multiple-fusion approach. Leveraging multiscale contexts from visual and auditory cues, this module greatly enhanced the spatial scale of fused features, thus improving the model’s separation capacity. However, as mentioned previously, T-domain methods come with a higher computational load. TF-domain AVSS techniques often use larger windows and hop sizes, which curtails computational complexity. Nevertheless, their full potential is yet to be realized. The recent Visualvoice model (Gao & Grauman, [2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib10)) combined a multi-task learning framework for both AVSS and cross-modal speaker embedding, incorporating facial expressions, lip movements, and audio cues. Despite its touted efficiency, Visualvoice lacks robust modeling, and it lags behind many modern T-domain methods.

## 3 Methods

Expanding on prior SOTA methods (Wu et al., [2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib39); Li et al., [2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib18)), we present our AVSS pipeline, illustrated in Figure [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#S3.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 3 Methods ‣ RTFS-Net: Recurrent Time-Frequency Modelling for Efficient Audio-Visual Speech Separation"). The mono-aural mixed-speech audio signal, $`{\mathbf{x}} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{1 \times L_{a}}`$, in conjunction with the video frames capturing the target speaker’s lip movements, $`{\mathbf{y}} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{1 \times L_{v} \times H \times W}`$, are used as inputs to RTFS-Net in order to derive the target speaker’s estimated audio signal, $`\hat{\mathbf{s}} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{1 \times L_{a}}`$. In this context, $`L_{a}`$ and $`L_{v}`$ signify the durations of the audio and video inputs, while $`H`$ and $`W`$ correspond to the dimensions of the single-channel (grey-scale) video frames.

![Refer to caption](https://arxiv.org/html/x1.png)

Figure 1: The overall pipeline of RTFS-Net. The red and blue solid lines signify the flow directions of auditory and visual features respectively. The snowflake indicates the weights are frozen and the component is not involved in training.

Firstly, the audio and video encoders extract auditory $`{\mathbf{a}}_{0}`$ and visual $`{\mathbf{v}}_{0}`$ features. These serve as the inputs for our separation network, which fuses these features and extracts the salient multimodal features, $`{\mathbf{a}}_{R}`$. Next, our Spectral Source Separation ($`S^{3}`$) method is applied to separate the target speaker’s audio $`\mathbf{z}`$ from the encoded audio signal $`{\mathbf{a}}_{0}`$ using $`{\mathbf{a}}_{R}`$. Finally, the target speaker’s estimated audio feature map $`\mathbf{z}`$ is decoded into the estimated audio stream $`\hat{\mathbf{s}}`$ and compared with the ground-truth signal $`\mathbf{s}`$ for training.

### 3.1 Encoders

Our encoders distill relevant features from both audio and visual inputs. For the video encoder $`E_{v}{( \cdot )}`$, we employ the CTCNet-Lip (Li et al., [2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib18)) pretrained network to extract the visual features $`{\mathbf{v}}_{0}`$ of the target speaker,

```math
{{{\mathbf{v}}_{0} = {E_{v}{({\mathbf{y}})}}},{{\mathbf{v}}_{0} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{C_{v} \times T_{v}}}}.
```

For the audio encoder, we firstly define $`\mathbf{α}`$ as the complex-valued hybrid TF-domain bins obtained using the STFT on $`\mathbf{x}`$. For a mixed audio of $`n_{spk}`$ speakers, we define $`{\mathbf{s}}_{i}`$ as the speech of speaker $`i`$ and $`\mathbf{\epsilon}`$ as the presence of some background noise, music, or other extraneous audio sources. Then,

```math
{{{{\mathbf{α}}{(t,f)}} = {{\mathbf{\epsilon}{(t,f)}} + {\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{n_{spk}}{{\mathbf{s}}_{i}{(t,f)}}}}},{{{\mathbf{α}}{(t,f)}} \in {{\mathbb{C}}{\forall{(t,f)}}}}},
```

where $`t \in {\lbrack 0,T_{a}\rbrack}`$ is the time dimension and $`f \in {\lbrack 0,F\rbrack}`$ is the frequency dimension. We concatenate the real (Re) and imaginary (Im) parts of $`\mathbf{α}`$ along a new ‘channels’ axis, then apply a 2D convolution $`E_{a}{( \cdot )}`$ with a $`3 \times 3`$ kernel and $`C_{a}`$ output channels across the time and frequency dimensions to obtain the auditory embedding $`{\mathbf{a}}_{0}`$ of $`\mathbf{x}`$. Using the symbol $`||`$ for concatenation, we write,

```math
{\mathbf{a}}_{0} = E_{a}\left( {Re}{({\mathbf{α}})}||{Im}{({\mathbf{α}})} \right),{\mathbf{a}}_{0} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{C_{a} \times T_{a} \times F}.
```

### 3.2 Separation network

The core of RTFS-Net is a separation network that uses recursive units to facilitate information interaction in the two acoustic dimensions, and efficiently aggregates multimodal features using an attention-based fusion mechanism. The initial step is to preprocess the auditory $`{\mathbf{a}}_{0}`$ and visual $`{\mathbf{v}}_{0}`$ features separately in preparation for fusion. For the Visual Preprocessing (VP) Block we adopt a modified version of the TDANet Block (Li et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib19)), see Appendix [A](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#A1 "Appendix A Video Preprocessing Block ‣ RTFS-Net: Recurrent Time-Frequency Modelling for Efficient Audio-Visual Speech Separation"). For the Audio Preprocessing (AP) Block we use a single RTFS Block, whose structure will be defined in Section [3.2.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#S3.SS2.SSS2 "3.2.2 RTFS Blocks ‣ 3.2 Separation network ‣ 3 Methods ‣ RTFS-Net: Recurrent Time-Frequency Modelling for Efficient Audio-Visual Speech Separation"). The outputs of the two preprocessing blocks are fed to our proposed CAF Block to fuse the multimedia features into a single enriched feature map (see Figure [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#S3.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 3 Methods ‣ RTFS-Net: Recurrent Time-Frequency Modelling for Efficient Audio-Visual Speech Separation") and [2](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.2.1 Cross-dimensional Attention Fusion Block ‣ 3.2 Separation network ‣ 3 Methods ‣ RTFS-Net: Recurrent Time-Frequency Modelling for Efficient Audio-Visual Speech Separation")). This audio-visual fusion is subsequently processed with an additional $`R`$ stacked RTFS Blocks. Following CTCNet (Li et al., [2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib18)), these $`R`$ sequential blocks share parameters (including the AP Block), which has been shown to reduce model size and increase performance, since it turns the series of blocks in to a recurrent neural architecture (see Appendix [B](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#A2 "Appendix B Sharing Parameters ‣ RTFS-Net: Recurrent Time-Frequency Modelling for Efficient Audio-Visual Speech Separation")).

#### 3.2.1 Cross-dimensional Attention Fusion Block

The CAF Block (Figure [2](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.2.1 Cross-dimensional Attention Fusion Block ‣ 3.2 Separation network ‣ 3 Methods ‣ RTFS-Net: Recurrent Time-Frequency Modelling for Efficient Audio-Visual Speech Separation")) is a depth-wise and group-convolution based architecture designed to consume as little resources as possible while fusing the 2D visual data into the 3D audio data. It involves two separate fusion operations that we call the attention fusion ($`{\mathbf{f}}_{1}`$) and the gated fusion ($`{\mathbf{f}}_{2}`$). The attention fusion considers multiple visual sub-representation spaces to aggregate information from a wide receptive field and apply attention to the audio features. The gated fusion up-samples the visual information’s time dimension, then expands the visual features into the TF-domain using $`F`$ gates produced from the preprocessed audio features. We use Vaswani et al. ([2017](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib35))’s ‘keys’ and ‘values’ nomenclature.

![Refer to caption](https://arxiv.org/html/x2.png)

Figure 2: Structure of our CAF Block. As in Figure [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#S3.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 3 Methods ‣ RTFS-Net: Recurrent Time-Frequency Modelling for Efficient Audio-Visual Speech Separation"), the red and blue solid lines signify the flow directions of auditory and visual features respectively.

Firstly, let both $`\mathcal{P}_{1}`$ and $`\mathcal{P}_{2}`$ denote a depth-wise convolution with a $`1 \times 1`$ kernel and following global layer normalization (gLN) (Luo & Mesgarani, [2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib24)). We generate the audio ‘value’ embeddings and the aforementioned ‘gate’ from the preprocessed audio signal $`{\mathbf{a}}_{1}`$.

```math
{{{\mathbf{a}}_{val} = {\mathcal{P}_{1}{({\mathbf{a}}_{1})}}},{{{\mathbf{a}}_{gate} = {{{ReLU}\left( {\mathcal{P}_{2}{({\mathbf{a}}_{1})}} \right)},{\mathbf{a}}_{val}}},{{\mathbf{a}}_{gate} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{C_{a} \times T_{a} \times F}}}}.
```

Attention Fusion. We apply a 1D group convolution $`\mathcal{F}_{1}`$ with $`C_{a}`$ groups and $`C_{a} \times h`$ output channels to $`{\mathbf{v}}_{1}`$, followed by a gLN layer. By chunking across the channels, we can decompose the visual features into $`h`$ distinct sub-feature representations, or attention ‘heads’, $`{\mathbf{v}}_{h}`$. Next, we take the ‘mean’ of the $`h`$ heads to aggregate the information from the different sub-feature representations into $`{\mathbf{v}}_{m}`$, then subsequently apply the Softmax operation in order to create a multi-head attention style set of features $`{\mathbf{v}}_{attn}`$ with values between 0 and 1. To align the video frame length $`T_{v}`$ with the audio’s time dimension $`T_{a}`$, we use nearest neighbor interpolation, $`\phi`$. This is written,

$`{\mathbf{v}}_{h}`$$`{= {\mathcal{F}_{1}{({\mathbf{v}}_{1})}}},`$$`{\mathbf{v}}_{h}`$$`{\in {\mathbb{R}}^{{({C_{a} \times h})} \times T_{v}}},`$$`{\mathbf{v}}_{m}`$$`= {mean}{({\mathbf{v}}_{h}{\lbrack 1\rbrack},\ldots,{\mathbf{v}}_{h}{\lbrack h\rbrack})}),`$$`{\mathbf{v}}_{m}`$$`{\in {\mathbb{R}}^{C_{a} \times T_{v}}},`$$`{\mathbf{v}}_{attn}`$$`{= {\phi{({{Softmax}{({\mathbf{v}}_{m})}})}}},`$$`{\mathbf{v}}_{attn}`$$`{\in {\mathbb{R}}^{C_{a} \times T_{a}}}.`$

Our attention mechanism is applied to each of the $`F`$ ‘value’ slices of $`{\mathbf{a}}_{val}`$ with length $`T_{a}`$,

```math
{{{{\mathbf{f}}_{1}{\lbrack i\rbrack}} = {{{\mathbf{v}}_{attn} \odot {\mathbf{a}}_{val}}{\lbrack i\rbrack}}},{{\forall i} \in {\{ 1,\ldots,F\}}}},
```

where $`{{\mathbf{f}}_{1}{\lbrack i\rbrack}} \in {{\mathbb{R}}^{C_{a} \times T_{a}}{\forall i}}\Longrightarrow{\mathbf{f}}_{1} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{C_{a} \times T_{a} \times F}`$.

Gated Fusion. We use a 1D convolutional layer $`\mathcal{F}_{2}`$ with kernel size 1, $`C_{a}`$ output channels and $`C_{a}`$ groups (since $`C_{a} < C_{v}`$), followed by a gLN layer to align $`C_{v}`$ with $`C_{a}`$. Next, we again use interpolation $`\phi`$ to align $`T_{v}`$ with $`T_{a}`$ and generate the visual ‘key’ embeddings.

```math
{{{\mathbf{v}}_{key} = {\phi\left( {\mathcal{F}_{2}{({\mathbf{v}}_{1})}} \right)}},{{\mathbf{v}}_{key} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{C_{a} \times T_{a}}}}.
```

Next, we utilize all $`F`$ of the $`T_{a}`$-dimensional slices of $`{\mathbf{a}}_{gate}`$ as unique gates to comprehensively expand the visual information into the TF-domain,

```math
{{{{\mathbf{f}}_{2}{\lbrack i\rbrack}} = {{{\mathbf{a}}_{gate}{\lbrack i\rbrack}} \odot {\mathbf{v}}_{key}}},{{\forall i} \in {\{ 1,\ldots,F\}}}},
```

where $`{{\mathbf{f}}_{2}{\lbrack i\rbrack}} \in {{\mathbb{R}}^{C_{a} \times T_{a}}{\forall i}}\Longrightarrow{\mathbf{f}}_{2} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{C_{a} \times T_{a} \times F}`$.

CAF Block. Finally, we sum the two fused features together. We can denote our CAF Block, $`\Phi`$, as:

```math
{{{\mathbf{a}}_{2} = {\Phi{({\mathbf{a}}_{1},{\mathbf{v}}_{1})}} = {{\mathbf{f}}_{1} + {\mathbf{f}}_{2}}},{{\mathbf{a}}_{2} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{C_{a} \times T_{a} \times F}}}.
```

#### 3.2.2 RTFS Blocks

Compared to previous TF-domain AVSS methods (Afouras et al., [2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib3); Gao & Grauman, [2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib10); Alfouras et al., [2018](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib4); Lee et al., [2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib16)), our RTFS Blocks use a dual-path architecture to explicitly model audio in both acoustic dimensions to improve the separation quality, as shown in Figure [3](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3.2.2 RTFS Blocks ‣ 3.2 Separation network ‣ 3 Methods ‣ RTFS-Net: Recurrent Time-Frequency Modelling for Efficient Audio-Visual Speech Separation"). We denote the auditory features input into the RTFS Block as $`\mathbf{A}`$. Given our recurrent structure, $`\mathbf{A}`$ represents either $`{\mathbf{a}}_{0}`$ (input to AP Block) or the output from the previous RTFS Block with a skip connection: $`{\mathbf{a}}_{j} + {\mathbf{a}}_{0}`$ for $`j \in {\{ 1,\ldots,R\}}`$. Note that in Figure [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#S3.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 3 Methods ‣ RTFS-Net: Recurrent Time-Frequency Modelling for Efficient Audio-Visual Speech Separation") the residual connection is not shown for simplicity. Our RTFS Block processes auditory features in the four steps discussed below.

![Refer to caption](https://arxiv.org/html/x3.png)

Figure 3: RTFS Block design. After compressing the data to a more efficient size, we process first the frequency dimension, then the time dimension, then both dimensions in tandem using TF-domain self-attention to capture inter-dependencies. We then carefully restore the data to its original dimensions using our Temporal-Frequency Attention Reconstruction units.

Compression of time and frequency resolution. We use a 2D convolution with a $`1 \times 1`$ kernel to convert $`\mathbf{A}`$ to a smaller channel dimension $`D < C_{a}`$. This means we can effectively employ a larger $`C_{a}`$ value for detailed fusion (CAF) and separation ($`S^{3}`$), while maintaining a lightweight and efficient block design. Similar to Li et al. ([2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib19)), in the compression phase we employ $`q`$ stacked 2D depth-wise convolutional layers with $`4 \times 4`$ kernels and stride 2, see Appendix [C](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#A3 "Appendix C Results for Various Compression Scales ‣ RTFS-Net: Recurrent Time-Frequency Modelling for Efficient Audio-Visual Speech Separation") for the effects of different $`q`$ values. The resultant multi-scale set with varying temporal and frequency resolutions can be denoted $`\left. \{{\mathbf{A}}_{i} \middle| {i \in {\{ 0,{q - 1}\}}}\} \right.`$, where $`{\mathbf{A}}_{i} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{D \times \frac{T_{a}}{2^{i}} \times \frac{F}{2^{i}}}`$. We use adaptive average pooling $`p`$ to compress each member of the set to the dimensions of the smallest member, $`{\mathbf{A}}_{q - 1}`$, then sum to obtain the compressed global features $`{\mathbf{A}}_{G}`$. This is written:

```math
{{{\mathbf{A}}_{G} = {\sum\limits_{i = 0}^{q - 1}{p{({\mathbf{A}}_{i})}}}},{{\mathbf{A}}_{G} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{D \times \frac{T_{a}}{2^{q - 1}} \times \frac{F}{2^{q - 1}}}}}.
```

Dual-path architecture. The Dual-path RNN architecture has been extensively deployed in AOSS tasks (Luo et al., [2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib27); Chen et al., [2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib6); Wang et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib38)). However, their usage of large LSTMs leads to high parameter counts and an elevated computational complexity. In natural language processing, Simple Recurrent Units (SRU (Lei et al., [2018](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib17))) were introduced to replace LSTMs by processing most of the operations in parallel, speeding up model training and inference time. Inspired by this, we adopt SRUs for the AVSS task, see Appendix [D](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#A4 "Appendix D Structures for Independently Modeling Time and Frequency ‣ RTFS-Net: Recurrent Time-Frequency Modelling for Efficient Audio-Visual Speech Separation") for a detailed analysis between different recurrent architectures.

As seen in Figure [3](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3.2.2 RTFS Blocks ‣ 3.2 Separation network ‣ 3 Methods ‣ RTFS-Net: Recurrent Time-Frequency Modelling for Efficient Audio-Visual Speech Separation"), we first process the frequency dimension, then the time dimension. As with all dual-path methods, the SRUs are applied across each slice in the time dimension to process the frequency dimension. Similar to (Wang et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib38)), we unfold¹¹1[https://pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.nn.Unfold.html](https://pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.nn.Unfold.html) the features by zero-padding the frequency dimension of $`{\mathbf{A}}_{G}`$, then unfolding with kernel size $`8`$ and stride $`1`$,

```math
{{\overset{˙}{\mathbf{R}}}_{f} = \left\lbrack {{{{Unfold}{({{\mathbf{A}}_{G}{\lbrack:,t\rbrack}})}},t} \in \left\{ 0,\ldots,{T_{a}/2^{q - 1}} \right\}} \right\rbrack \in {\mathbb{R}}^{{8D} \times \frac{T_{a}}{2^{q - 1}} \times F'}},
```

where $`F'`$ is the resulting padded and unfolded frequency dimension. Note that while this process results in high input dimensions for the SRUs, which significantly increases computational complexity, it is essential for achieving outstanding separation performance. This is why it is so important that we first compress the time-frequency resolution. After unfolding, layer normalization is applied in the channel dimension, and then a bidirectional, 4 layer $`SRU`$ with hidden size $`h_{a}`$ is applied,

```math
{{\overset{¨}{\mathbf{R}}}_{f} = \left\lbrack {{{{SRU}{({{\overset{˙}{\mathbf{R}}}_{f}{\lbrack:,t\rbrack}})}},t} \in {\{ 0,\ldots,{T_{a}/2^{q - 1}}\}}} \right\rbrack \in {\mathbb{R}}^{{2h_{a}} \times \frac{T_{a}}{2^{q - 1}} \times F'}}.
```

A transposed convolution $`\mathcal{T}`$ with kernel 8 and stride 1 is used to restore the unfolded dimensions,

```math
{{{\overset{˙˙˙}{\mathbf{R}}}_{f} = {{\mathcal{T}\left( {\overset{¨}{\mathbf{R}}}_{f} \right)} + {\mathbf{A}}_{G}}},{{\overset{˙˙˙}{\mathbf{R}}}_{f} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{D \times \frac{T_{a}}{2^{q - 1}} \times \frac{F}{2^{q - 1}}}}}.
```

We next process the time dimension using the same method, and then finally apply Wang et al. ([2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib38))’s TF-domain self-attention network, denoted $`Attn`$. These two steps are expressed below as:

```math
{{{\overset{˙˙˙}{\mathbf{R}}}_{t} = {{\mathcal{T}\left( {\overset{¨}{\mathbf{R}}}_{t} \right)} + {\overset{˙˙˙}{\mathbf{R}}}_{f}}},{{{\overline{\mathbf{A}}}_{G} = {{{{Attn}{({\overset{˙˙˙}{\mathbf{R}}}_{t})}} + {\overset{˙˙˙}{\mathbf{R}}}_{t}},{\overset{˙˙˙}{\mathbf{R}}}_{t}}},{{\overline{\mathbf{A}}}_{G} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{D \times \frac{T_{a}}{2^{q - 1}} \times \frac{F}{2^{q - 1}}}}}}.
```

Reconstruction of time and frequency resolution. Reconstruction of high-quality temporal and frequency features presents a formidable challenge. Delving into the underlying causes, the reconstruction process often relies on interpolation or transposed convolutions for up-sampling, resulting in the emergence of checkerboard artifacts in the reconstructed outputs. To solve this problem, we propose the Temporal-Frequency Attention Reconstruction (TF-AR) unit, denoted $`I{( \cdot , \cdot )}`$. This unit prioritizes the reconstruction of key features by exploiting an attention mechanism, thus reducing information loss. For two tensors $`\mathbf{m}`$ and $`\mathbf{n}`$, we define the TF-AR unit as:

```math
{{I{({\mathbf{m}},{\mathbf{n}})}} = {{{{\phi\left( {\sigma\left( {W_{1}({\mathbf{n}})} \right)} \right)} \odot W_{2}}({\mathbf{m}})} + {\phi\left( {W_{3}({\mathbf{n}})} \right)}}},
```

where $`W_{1}{( \cdot )}`$, $`W_{2}{( \cdot )}`$ and $`W_{3}{( \cdot )}`$ denote 2D depth-wise convolutions with $`4 \times 4`$ kernels followed by a gLN layer. We use the notation $`\sigma`$ for the sigmoid function, $`\odot`$ for element-wise multiplication and $`\phi`$ for nearest neighbour interpolation (up-sampling). To conduct the reconstruction, we firstly use $`q`$ TF-AR units to fuse $`{\overline{\mathbf{A}}}_{G}`$ with every element of $`{\mathbf{A}}_{i}`$,

```math
{{{\mathbf{A}}_{i}' = {I{({\mathbf{A}}_{i},{\overline{\mathbf{A}}}_{G})}}},{{\mathbf{A}}_{i}' \in {{\mathbb{R}}^{D \times \frac{T_{a}}{2^{i}} \times \frac{F}{2^{i}}}{\forall i}} \in {\{ 0,\ldots,{q - 1}\}}}}.
```

Next, the multi-scale features are continuously up-sampled and aggregated using $`q - 1`$ additional TF-AR units to obtain the finest-grained auditory features, $`{\mathbf{A}}_{0}^{\operatorname{\prime\prime}} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{D \times T_{a} \times F}`$. A residual connection to $`\left. \{{\mathbf{A}}_{i} \middle| {i \in {\{ 0,{q - 2}\}}}\} \right.`$ is crucial, and creates a U-Net (Ronneberger et al., [2015](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib31)) style structure:

```math
{{{\mathbf{A}}_{q - 1 - i}^{\operatorname{\prime\prime}} = {{I{({\mathbf{A}}_{q - 1 - i}',{\mathbf{A}}_{q - i}')}} + {\mathbf{A}}_{q - 1 - i}}},{{\forall i} \in {\{ 0,\ldots,{q - 2}\}}}}.
```

Finally, $`{\mathbf{A}}_{0}^{\operatorname{\prime\prime}}`$ is converted back to $`C_{a}`$ channels using a 2D convolution with a $`1 \times 1`$ kernel and a residual connection to the input of the RTFS Block is added. The output features are used as the audio input for the CAF Block after the AP Block, and as the input to the next RTFS Block during the repeated $`R`$ stacked RTFS Blocks stage of the separation network.

### 3.3 Spectral Source Separation

The majority of existing T-domain AVSS methods (Wu et al., [2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib39); Li et al., [2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib18); Martel et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib28)) generate a mask $`\mathbf{m}`$ from the refined features $`{\mathbf{a}}_{R}`$, then use element-wise multiplication $`\odot`$ between the encoded audio mixture $`{\mathbf{a}}_{0}`$ and the mask in order to obtain the target speaker’s separated speech $`\mathbf{z}`$. This is written,

```math
{{\mathbf{z}} = {{\mathbf{m}} \odot {\mathbf{a}}_{0}}}.
```

Some TF-domain AVSS methods (Afouras et al., [2018a](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib1); Gao & Grauman, [2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib10); Lee et al., [2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib16)) directly apply this approach to the TF-domain setting without modification, while other methods (Alfouras et al., [2018](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib4); Owens & Efros, [2018](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib29)) choose not to use masks at all, and directly passes the output of the separation network $`{\mathbf{a}}_{R}`$ to the decoder. However, we found both of these TF-domain methods for target speaker extraction to be suboptimal. We need to pay attention to the underlying complex nature of the audio features produced by the STFT in order to obtain a clearer distinction. This leads us to introduce our $`S^{3}`$ Block, which utilizes a high-dimensional application of the multiplication of complex numbers, Equation [21](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#S3.E21 "21 ‣ 3.3 Spectral Source Separation ‣ 3 Methods ‣ RTFS-Net: Recurrent Time-Frequency Modelling for Efficient Audio-Visual Speech Separation"), to better preserve important acoustic properties during the speaker extraction process, see Section [5.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#S5.SS2 "5.2 Ablation study ‣ 5 Results ‣ RTFS-Net: Recurrent Time-Frequency Modelling for Efficient Audio-Visual Speech Separation") and Appendix [E](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#A5 "Appendix E Loss of Amplitude and Phase Information in the Traditional Mask Approach ‣ RTFS-Net: Recurrent Time-Frequency Modelling for Efficient Audio-Visual Speech Separation").

```math
{{{({a + {bi}})}{({c + {di}})}} = {{{ac} - {bd}} + {i{({{ad} + {bc}})}}}}.
```

Firstly, a mask $`\mathbf{m}`$ is generated from $`{\mathbf{a}}_{R}`$ using a 2D convolution $`\mathcal{M}`$ with a $`1 \times 1`$ kernel,

```math
{{{\mathbf{m}} = {{ReLU}\left( {\mathcal{M}\left( {{PReLU}{({\mathbf{a}}_{R})}} \right)} \right)}},{{\mathbf{m}} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{C_{a} \times T_{a} \times F}}}.
```

Without loss of generality, we choose the top half of the channels as the real part, and the bottom half of the channels as the imaginary part. We hence define,

$`{\mathbf{m}}^{r}`$$`= {\mathbf{m}}\left\lbrack 0:C_{a}/2 - 1 \right\rbrack,`$$`{\mathbf{E}}^{r}`$$`= {\mathbf{a}}_{0}\left\lbrack 0:C_{a}/2 - 1 \right\rbrack,`$$`{\mathbf{m}}^{i}`$$`= {\mathbf{m}}\left\lbrack C_{a}/2:C_{a} \right\rbrack,`$$`{\mathbf{E}}^{i}`$$`= {\mathbf{a}}_{0}\left\lbrack C_{a}/2:C_{a} \right\rbrack.`$

Next, with $`||`$ denoting concatenation along the channel axis, we apply Equation [21](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#S3.E21 "21 ‣ 3.3 Spectral Source Separation ‣ 3 Methods ‣ RTFS-Net: Recurrent Time-Frequency Modelling for Efficient Audio-Visual Speech Separation") and calculate:

$`{\mathbf{z}}^{r}`$$`{= {{{\mathbf{m}}^{r} \odot {\mathbf{E}}^{r}} - {{\mathbf{m}}^{i} \odot {\mathbf{E}}^{i}}}},`$$`{\mathbf{z}}^{i}`$$`{= {{{\mathbf{m}}^{r} \odot {\mathbf{E}}^{i}} + {{\mathbf{m}}^{i} \odot {\mathbf{E}}^{r}}}},`$$`\mathbf{z}`$$`= {({\mathbf{z}}^{r}||{\mathbf{z}}^{i})},{\mathbf{z}} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{C_{a} \times T_{a} \times F},`$

to obtain $`\mathbf{z}`$, the target speaker’s separated encoded audio features.

### 3.4 Decoder

The decoder $`D{( \cdot )}`$ takes the separated target speaker’s audio features $`\mathbf{z}`$ and reconstructs the estimated waveform $`\hat{\mathbf{s}} = {D{({\mathbf{z}})}}`$, where $`\hat{\mathbf{s}} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{1 \times L_{a}}`$. Specifically, $`\mathbf{z}`$ is passed through a transposed 2D convolution with a $`3 \times 3`$ kernel and 2 output channels. Mirroring the encoder, we take the first channel as the real part and the second channel as the imaginary part and form a complex tensor. This tensor is passed to the iSTFT to recover the estimated target speaker audio.

## 4 Experimental Setup

Datasets. We utilized the same AVSS datasets as previous works (Gao & Grauman, [2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib10); Li et al., [2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib18)) in the field in order to create a fair comparison of performance: LRS2-2Mix (Afouras et al., [2018a](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib1)), LRS3-2Mix (Afouras et al., [2018b](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib2)) and VoxCeleb2-2Mix (Chung et al., [2018](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib8)). The models were trained and tested on two second 25fps video clips with an audio sampling rate of 16 kHz. This equates to 32,000 audio frames and 50 video frames, see Appendix [F](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#A6 "Appendix F Dataset Details ‣ RTFS-Net: Recurrent Time-Frequency Modelling for Efficient Audio-Visual Speech Separation") for more details.

Evaluation. Following recent literature (Li et al., [2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib18)), SI-SNRi and SDRi were used to evaluate the quality of the separated speeches, see Appendix [G](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#A7 "Appendix G Evaluation metrics for Audio-Visual Speech-Separation Quality ‣ RTFS-Net: Recurrent Time-Frequency Modelling for Efficient Audio-Visual Speech Separation"). We also provided PESQ (Rix et al., [2001](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#bib.bib30)) for reference. For these metrics, a higher value indicates better performance. The parameter counts displayed in the results tables are the number of trainable parameters, excluding the pretrained video model. Likewise, the number of Multiply-ACcumulate (MAC) operations indicates the MACs used while processing two seconds of audio at 16 kHz, excluding the pretrained video network. In our main results table, we also include inference time: the time taken to process 2 seconds of audio on a NVIDIA 2080 GPU. For parameters, MACs and inference speed, a lower value is preferable and is the main focus of this work. Model hyperparameter configurations are available in Appendix [H](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#A8 "Appendix H Model Hyperparameters ‣ RTFS-Net: Recurrent Time-Frequency Modelling for Efficient Audio-Visual Speech Separation"). All training settings are available in Appendix [I](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#A9 "Appendix I Training Hyperparameters ‣ RTFS-Net: Recurrent Time-Frequency Modelling for Efficient Audio-Visual Speech Separation").

## 5 Results

### 5.1 Comparisons with state-of-the-art methods

The goal of this research is to obtain SOTA performance while significantly reducing computational complexity and model size with a lightweight, streamlined approach. In the comprehensive comparison presented in Table [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17189v4#S5.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 5.1 Comparisons with state-of-the-art methods ‣ 5 Results ‣ RTFS-Net: Recurrent Time-Frequency Modelling for Efficient Audio-Visual Speech Separation"), we evaluated RTFS-Net against a range of SOTA AVSS methods. We explored three variants of RTFS-Net, corresponding to $`R = {\{ 4,6,12\}}`$ RTFS Blocks, including the AP Block. On the LRS2-2Mix dataset, RTFS-Net-4 demonstrated an SI-SNRi of 14.1 dB, only marginally lower than the 14.3 dB achieved by the previous SOTA, CTCNet. However, this was achieved with a tenfold reduction in model parameters and an eightfold decrease in computational cost. Meanwhile, RTFS-Net-6 surpassed CTCNet on the LRS2-2Mix dataset and delivered comparable results on VoxCeleb2-2Mix, striking a good balance between performance and efficiency. RTFS-Net-12 outperformed all other techniques across all datasets, showcasing its superiority in complex environments and the robustness of our TF-domain approach. Moreover, despite utilizing 12 layers, RTFS-Net-12 still manages to reduce the computational costs of CTCNet by threefold while using only a tenth of the parameters. To our knowledge, RTFS-Net is the first AVSS method to employ fewer than 1 million parameters, and the first TF-domain model to outperform all T-domain counterparts.

## 6 Conclusion

In this study we introduced RTFS-Net, a novel approach to AVSS that explicitly models time and frequency dimensions at a compressed subspace to improve performance and increase efficiency. Empirical evaluations across multiple datasets demonstrate the superiority of our approach. Notably, our method achieves remarkable performance improvements while maintaining a significantly reduced computational complexity and parameter count. This indicates that enhancing AVSS performance does not necessarily require larger models, but rather innovative and efficient architectures that better capture the intricate interplay between audio and visual modalities.
