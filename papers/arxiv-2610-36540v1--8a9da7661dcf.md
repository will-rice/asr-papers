Frugal HTR for Sub-Saharan civil-status registers

# Frugal Handwritten Text Recognition for Sub-Saharan Civil-Status Registers: A Smartphone-to-Records Pipeline with CPU-Only Inference

Ousmane Hamadou    Jean Louis Kedieng Ebongue Fendji

###### Abstract

Handwritten civil-status registers across Sub-Saharan Africa are being
photographed, but rarely transcribed, which leaves their content
unsearchable. Local administrations that hold these records typically
lack GPUs, reliable connectivity, and the budget for external
digitization services. We study whether a frugal pipeline, with
consumer-grade capture and CPU-only inference, can make such registers
machine-readable. We introduce a corpus of 1,568 smartphone images of
handwritten birth, marriage, and death records from a municipal registry
in Chad, with line-level transcriptions for 185 records. Our pipeline
combines a fully classical preprocessing stage, running at about 2.6
images per second on a laptop CPU, with a Kraken recognizer fine-tuned
from a pretrained model in about four hours on a free-tier GPU. On
manually corrected line segmentation and a single-registry test set
whose writers also appear in training, fine-tuning reduces the character
error rate from 14.53% to 0.50% and the word error rate from 56.93% to
3.37%. On a six-core laptop CPU, the recognizer weighs 61 MiB, peaks
below 2.6 GiB of RAM, and processes an image in under 6 s on average,
excluding line segmentation. Finally, a downstream
information-extraction task shows that residual recognition errors cost
about 9 F1 points on role resolution, compared with a 36.7-point gap
between the fine-tuned and pretrained recognizers. These results are
evidence of feasibility within one registry, not of performance on
unseen writers or sites; we discuss these limitations in detail.

## 1 Introduction

Civil registration is the documentary basis of legal identity. Birth,
marriage, and death records establish age, filiation, and nationality,
and they feed the demographic statistics that public policy depends
on \[40, 42\]. In Sub-Saharan Africa, a large share of these records
exists only as handwritten registers kept in municipal offices, often in
poor physical condition and exposed to humidity, fire, and loss \[1,
31\]. Coverage is also low: UNICEF estimates that only about half of
children under five in the region have their births registered \[41\].
For people without recent documents, these older registers can be the
only surviving proof of identity or filiation \[43\].

Several countries have launched large modernization programmes, such as
Senegal’s NEKKAL \[9\], which are funded at a scale most local
administrations cannot match. These efforts mainly computerize new
registrations or index scanned documents at the level of whole records.
The historical handwritten stock is typically photographed, when it is
digitized at all, but its content remains unreadable by machines: an
image without a transcription cannot be searched by name, date, or
place.

Handwritten text recognition (HTR) is the obvious remedy, but two
obstacles stand in the way in this setting. First, _domain mismatch_:
publicly available HTR models are trained predominantly on European
historical documents. They have never seen the handwriting styles,
administrative templates, or, most importantly, the personal and place
names that dominate African civil records. Second, _resource
constraints_: most recent HTR work assumes GPU training and inference,
and often cloud processing. That is incompatible with offices that have
only standard laptops, intermittent connectivity, and legitimate
concerns about sending identity data to external providers.

In this paper we ask whether a frugal pipeline, meaning one captured
with consumer hardware, adapted with a small amount of annotated data,
and run for inference entirely on a CPU, can reach transcription quality
good enough to be useful. We make the following contributions:

1.  1.  A new corpus of Sub-Saharan civil-status records. We collected 1,568
        smartphone images of handwritten birth, marriage, and death records
        from a municipal registry in Chad, using a low-cost capture
        protocol. The records follow five distinct administrative templates.
        We provide line-level transcriptions for 185 records (Section 3).
2.  2.  A classical, CPU-only preprocessing pipeline for smartphone RAW
        captures. It handles page detection, separation of two records
        captured in one image, binding-margin removal, and upside-down image
        detection without any learned model. It ran on the full corpus
        without errors at about 2.6 images per second on a laptop CPU; the
        accuracy of its outputs was not measured (Section 4.1).
3.  3.  An evaluation of frugal HTR adaptation. Fine-tuning a Kraken
        recognizer on this small ground truth for about four hours on a
        free-tier GPU reduces CER from 14.53% to 0.50% on corrected line
        segmentation and writers seen in training. The resulting model runs
        on a laptop CPU with 61 MiB of weights, under 2.6 GiB of peak
        memory, and under 6 s per image (Sections 4.2–5.3).
4.  4.  An error-propagation analysis on a downstream information-extraction
        task. It quantifies how much of the end-task error comes from
        recognition rather than from later stages (Section 5.4).

Capture, preprocessing, and inference run on consumer hardware without a
GPU; only fine-tuning used a free-tier cloud GPU. The stages are
evaluated separately: line segmentation was corrected manually, so the
pipeline is not evaluated end to end. We also report the limitations of
our study openly, including the single collection site, the small test
set, and the absence of inter-annotator agreement (Section 6), and we
describe how we handled the personal data involved (Section 7).

## 2 Related Work

#### Handwritten text recognition.

Handwriting recognition has long been harder than printed OCR because
cursive letters join, shapes vary across writers, and character
boundaries are ambiguous. This “segmentation problem” was the main
obstacle for offline recognition \[32\]. The field moved toward
_segmentation-free_ recognition of whole text lines: recurrent networks
trained with Connectionist Temporal Classification learn the alignment
between image positions and output characters implicitly \[17, 18\].
Convolutional-recurrent networks with CTC (CRNN-CTC) remain a standard,
lightweight baseline. Kraken \[26\] implements this design for
historical documents and, together with the eScriptorium
platform \[25\], supports the full workflow from segmentation to
transcription and training. More recently, encoder-decoder Transformers
such as TrOCR \[27\] have achieved strong results by combining a vision
Transformer encoder with a pretrained language-model decoder, at a
substantially higher computational cost. We use CRNN-CTC because its
cost fits our CPU inference setting. Comparing it under the same
resource constraints with a Transformer recognizer, with Kraken models
already trained on French handwriting (*e.g*from the HTR-United or
CATMuS collections), with Transkribus, and with compact vision-language
models run on a CPU without training remains future work.

#### Historical records and domain adaptation.

Much HTR research on historical material relies on curated ground truth
from European archives \[39\], typically produced by a limited number of
writers. Closest to our setting is work on demographic records: the
ESPOSALLES database of historical marriage licences \[36\] and the
associated ICDAR 2017 competition on information extraction from
historical handwritten records \[15\]. Both address recognition _and_
the extraction of names and roles from structured civil or parish
records, which is essentially the task we study downstream. The French
civil registers of births of Belfort (1807–1919) also combine
pre-printed forms with handwritten fields \[5\]. On Dutch Curaçao death
certificates (1879–1949) transcribed with a custom-trained Transkribus
model, Hoek *et al*found that names and professions still contain errors
when the rest of the transcription appears flawless, and that whole-text
quality indicators say little about the entities scholars need \[20\].
To our knowledge, comparable resources for Sub-Saharan African civil
registers do not exist. Our registers differ from European corpora in
three ways: many officers wrote them with no common calligraphic
standard, they mix pre-printed forms with handwritten fields, and they
are dominated by local personal and place names.

#### Name bias in pretrained models.

Pretrained language models such as CamemBERT \[28\] are trained on web
text in which Sub-Saharan African names are likely under-represented.
Subword tokenization splits rare names into many fragments, which
weakens their representation \[7, 4\]. Multilingual NER performance also
drops sharply for under-represented African languages \[2\]. Our setting
differs: the text is in French, and it is the _names_ rather than the
language that are out of distribution. The same concern plausibly
applies to recognition models trained mostly on European material; we
measure the effect of adaptation on the extraction model per entity
type, but not on name spans in the recognizer.

#### Efficient deployment.

The “Green AI” position argues that model quality should always be
reported alongside computational cost \[38\]. This matters especially
where access to compute and connectivity is limited, as in much of
Sub-Saharan Africa, where cloud-based AI risks widening existing digital
divides \[22, 13\]. Compression techniques such as post-training
quantization \[23, 29\], pruning \[19\], and distillation \[37\] are
complementary and have been applied, for example, to deep-learning
models for agricultural plants \[16\]; their speed-ups, however, depend
on hardware and implementation. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning \[21,
12\] reduces training memory, yet QLoRA’s 4-bit kernels typically assume
a CUDA-capable GPU. We therefore follow a simple recipe suited to
low-resource settings: full fine-tuning of a small model, with every
cost dimension reported.

#### Civil-registration digitization in Africa.

Continental and national programmes are modernizing civil
registration \[3, 10\], for example Senegal’s NEKKAL \[9\]. These
efforts focus mainly on computerizing new registrations and indexing
scanned documents at the record level, not on transcribing the content
of historical handwritten registers. Our work addresses this gap with an
approach sized for local administrations rather than national
programmes.

## 3 Dataset and Capture Protocol

#### Source and context.

The corpus comes from the civil-status archive of a municipal registry
in Chad. Chad’s recent civil-registration reforms focus on secure
identity documents, late registration of children, and a new biometric
register \[11, 33\]. None of them addresses the existing stock of
handwritten registers, which remains the only record of many past
births, marriages, and deaths. The registry we worked with lies outside
the urban areas targeted by these programmes and is representative of
the many local offices where paper registers are the sole copy.

The registers were kept by a succession of civil-status officers, each
with their own handwriting and no common calligraphic standard. This
makes the writer population far more varied than in many reference HTR
datasets. Most records are pre-printed forms, several of them bilingual
French–Arabic, whose handwritten fields are filled in French. The
handwritten fields are dominated by local personal and place names that
rarely appear in European training corpora. The registers also show
physical wear typical of such archives, including stains, tears, faded
ink, and damage from humidity and handling.

#### Capture protocol.

Collection took place on site over five consecutive days, producing
1,568 images, an average of about 314 per day. This throughput
requirement shaped the protocol, which uses only consumer hardware: a
Google Pixel 6a smartphone capturing in RAW (DNG) format at an image
resolution of $`2268\times 4032`$ pixels; a stand holding the phone
parallel to the register, whose height was adjusted for each record; and
a ThinkPad T460 laptop running scrcpy (v4.1), which mirrors the phone
screen for real-time framing control and remote triggering. We captured
in RAW rather than JPEG to avoid the phone’s built-in sharpening,
denoising, and tone mapping, which can destroy the fine intensity
variations our cropping pipeline relies on. Holding the phone parallel
to the page and triggering remotely reduce motion blur and perspective
distortion, which is therefore limited at capture time rather than
corrected in software. Lighting was not controlled: captures were made
under ambient conditions, so the page-detection stage must cope with
uneven illumination. The full setup (laptop, phone, and capture stand)
costs about 150,000 FCFA (roughly 230 EUR) and requires no specialized
scanning equipment.

#### Corpus composition.

Table 1 gives the distribution of images by record type. Birth records
dominate, and marriage records are scarce. The legal record type is only
a rough guide to layout: manual inspection revealed five distinct
templates, namely (G1) narrative marriage record, (G2) birth record by
direct declaration, (G3) birth record transcribing a supplementary court
judgment, (G4) narrative death record, and (G5) birth declaration on a
pre-printed form filled in by hand. Birth records alone span three
structurally different templates, so the recognizer must handle
different line lengths, field arrangements, and mixes of printed and
handwritten text.

#### Annotation.

We transcribed 185 records covering all three record types in
eScriptorium \[25\], producing line-level transcriptions aligned with
the image. Lines were first segmented automatically and then corrected
manually once; this corrected segmentation was stored and reused every
time it was needed (training, evaluation, benchmarking). A line is
transcribed if and only if it contains handwritten text: printed text is
part of the ground truth only when it appears on a line that also
contains handwriting, and purely printed lines are excluded. The data
were split 70/15/15 into training, validation, and test sets at the line
level, with the constraint that lines from the same record never appear
in more than one set. For the downstream task, a separate set of 179
records was annotated with eight entity types in Label Studio
(Section 4.4). All annotation was carried out by a single annotator, so
we cannot report inter-annotator agreement.

#### Data availability.

The records contain personal information about real individuals. Only
two isolated text lines, with personal names and record identifiers
masked, are shown in this paper (Figure 2); the corpus (images,
transcriptions, and annotations) is not publicly released (Section 7).
We will release the preprocessing code; the recognizer weights will be
released after a memorization check and the required authorization.

## 4 Method

Our pipeline has three stages (Figure 1): classical _preprocessing_ that
turns raw smartphone captures into cropped, correctly oriented images of
single records; _handwritten text recognition_ with a fine-tuned Kraken
model; and a _downstream extraction_ stage that we use only to measure
how recognition errors propagate. Inference in every stage runs on a
laptop CPU.

### 4.1 Classical preprocessing

We deliberately use no learned model in preprocessing: classical
operations need no training data or annotation, have predictable failure
modes, and run fast on commodity hardware.

#### Normalization and orientation.

Raw DNG files are renamed deterministically using their MD5 hash and
grouped into batches. A portrait/landscape check found no image that
needed rotation in practice. Upside-down captures occurred in a subset
of the images of the marriage sub-corpus: 87 images in total, or 5.5% of
the corpus. We detect them by normalized cross-correlation template
matching of the letter “e”, a frequent character in the corpus, with a
detection threshold of 0.80, and rotate the detected images by 180°.
Finally, we remove a fixed strip along the right edge that contains no
document content.

#### Page cropping.

Smartphone captures taken under uncontrolled lighting have uneven
illumination, so a single global threshold such as Otsu’s \[30\] is
unreliable. Instead, after smoothing with a $`7\times 7`$ Gaussian
kernel, we combine adaptive Gaussian thresholding with an HSV criterion
that keeps low-saturation, bright pixels ($`S<70`$ and $`V>110`$ on an
8-bit scale) to separate page from background. We then compute a Canny
edge map \[8\] (thresholds 30 and 90), reinforce it with directional
morphological closing (vertical $`3\times 60`$ and horizontal
$`60\times 3`$ kernels), and take horizontal and vertical projection
profiles of the result to locate the page boundaries.

#### Separating adjacent records.

When the detected region is too wide for a single record, because two
records were captured in one frame, a disambiguation step chooses the
split that best separates them, scoring candidates on fill ratio,
relative width, and edge strength. A second pass targeted at the left
margin then removes the binding area: after a $`21\times 21`$ Gaussian
blur and contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization (clip limit
3.0), a Canny edge map (thresholds 100 and 200) locates the binding, and
a fixed offset of 45 pixels is applied to the left boundary.

#### No perspective correction.

We do not apply homography-based rectification. The capture protocol
keeps the phone parallel to the page, so perspective distortion is
limited at the source: we prevent distortion during capture rather than
correct it with extra computation afterwards. Some residual curvature
near the binding remains (Section 6).

The full pipeline ran on all 1,568 images without errors, at about 2.6
images per second on the Dell 7750 laptop used for the benchmarks
(Section 5). We did not measure the accuracy of page crops, record
splits, or upside-down detection against manual references; the
parameters (Table 2) were tuned by hand on this corpus and may need
retuning elsewhere.

### 4.2 Handwritten text recognition

#### Architecture.

We use Kraken \[26\], an open-source HTR system built on a hybrid
convolutional-recurrent network trained with the CTC loss \[17\]. It is
small enough to run on a CPU, can be fine-tuned on a free-tier GPU,
integrates directly with the eScriptorium annotation platform, and is
widely used for historical documents.

#### Line segmentation.

Lines were segmented automatically and then corrected manually once
(Section 3); this corrected segmentation is reused throughout. All
reported CER and WER values are therefore computed on known,
ground-truth line boundaries and do not include segmentation errors.
End-to-end evaluation with automatic segmentation is left to future
work.

#### Fine-tuning.

Training from scratch is infeasible with a few hundred annotated
records. We therefore fully fine-tune all weights of the pretrained
Kraken model kraken-ppocrv6-medium (Hugging Face:
small-models-for-glam/kraken-ppocrv6-medium). Because the pretrained
output alphabet does not cover every character and diacritic in our
corpus, we merge it with the alphabet of our data using Kraken’s
--resize union option. We train for 40 epochs with a learning rate of
$`10^{-3}`$ and a batch size of 7. Training took about four hours on a
free-tier Google Colab GPU; it is the only stage of the pipeline that
used a GPU. We did not use parameter-efficient methods: the model is
small enough that full fine-tuning is already inexpensive.

#### Data split.

Ground truth is split 70/15/15 at the line level, Kraken’s native unit,
with the constraint that lines from one record never appear in more than
one split. This prevents record-level leakage but not writer-level
leakage (Section 6).

### 4.3 Cost measurement protocol

We report quality jointly with cost. We compare two configurations that
share the same architecture and FP32 precision: the pretrained model and
the fine-tuned model. Fine-tuning changes the weights but not the
network, so any difference in inference cost between them reflects
measurement noise rather than model size. We assume an operational
ceiling of 2% CER, above which we expect checking and correcting the
output to approach the effort of partial manual transcription. This
threshold is a working assumption, not a measured correction cost, and
the downstream results (Section 5.4) suggest that the error rate on
names matters more than overall CER.

#### Benchmark protocol.

We process 50 images drawn at random (seed 42) in five steps of ten
images. Both models process the same images, and two additional images
are processed beforehand as warm-up and excluded. Inference uses batches
of 8 lines, line-length bucketing (64) with sorting, a frozen
computation graph, channels-last layout, batch-normalization fusion, 6
threads, 2 line workers, and a prefetch of 2. The measured time covers
recognition on the stored, manually corrected line segmentation and
excludes automatic line segmentation.

#### Metrics.

We report weight file size, model loading time, one-time warm-up time
(first inference after loading), peak resident memory (RSS) of the
process, time per image and per line, throughput, and mean CPU
utilization. Time per image is the mean over the five steps, with its
standard deviation across steps. We also report the training cost of
fine-tuning.

We did not evaluate reduced-precision inference (e.g. INT8
quantization). Its benefit depends on hardware support and on the
inference runtime, and our target machines are ordinary laptop CPUs
without dedicated low-precision instructions; we leave it to future work
on the actual deployment hardware.

### 4.4 Downstream extraction (evaluation probe)

To measure how recognition quality affects a real task, we feed HTR
output into an extraction stage that identifies the people in each
record and their roles (father, mother, spouse, witness, etc.). We fully
fine-tune camembert-ner-with-dates \[24\], a French model of roughly
110M parameters derived from CamemBERT \[28\], on 179 annotated records,
with eight entity types: PER, LOC, ORG, and DATE (inherited from the
base model) and PROFESSION, NUM_REF (reference numbers), ACTE_TYPE
(record type), and ANCRE (lexical role markers such as _fils de_, “son
of”). The record’s template is inferred with simple rules from the
detected ACTE_TYPE entities. Roles are then assigned in linear time by a
deterministic algorithm that walks through the entities in reading order
and uses a template-specific dictionary mapping each marker to a role;
each person entity inherits the role set by the most recent marker, and
a block-counting mechanism handles the repeated field groups of template
G5. Because these rules are fixed, role errors come from only three
sources, namely recognition, NER, or template classification, which lets
us isolate the contribution of HTR (Section 5.4).

## 5 Experiments and Results

### 5.1 Setup

Preprocessing and inference benchmarks run on a Dell 7750 mobile
workstation with an Intel Core i7-10850H CPU (6 cores), 15.6 GiB of RAM,
and no GPU. This machine is faster than the ThinkPad T460 used for
capture, on which we did not run the benchmarks. Fine-tuning ran on a
free-tier Google Colab GPU. Software: Python 3.13, PyTorch 2.14.0 (CPU
build), Kraken 7.1.1, OpenCV 5.0.0.93, jiwer 4.0.0, seqeval 1.2.2.
Character error rate is

```math
\mathrm{CER}=\frac{S+D+I}{N}, \tag{1}
```

where $`S`$, $`D`$, $`I`$ are character substitutions, deletions, and
insertions and $`N`$ is the number of reference characters; WER is
defined analogously at word level.

### 5.2 Recognition quality

| Model                              | CER (%) $`\downarrow`$ | WER (%) $`\downarrow`$ |
| ---------------------------------- | ---------------------- | ---------------------- |
| Kraken, pretrained (no adaptation) | 14.53                  | 56.93                  |
| Kraken, fine-tuned                 | 0.50                   | 3.37                   |

Table 3: HTR results on the test set, computed on ground-truth line
segmentation.

Fine-tuning reduces CER by a factor of about 29 and WER by a factor of
about 17 (Table 3). In absolute terms WER falls by 53.6 points, against
14.0 points for CER: with the pretrained model more than half of the
words are affected by errors, whereas after fine-tuning fewer than 4%
are. On inspection, many of the pretrained model’s errors fall on local
personal and place names, as reported for other civil records \[20\],
but we did not measure error rates on name spans. Part of its 14.53% CER
also comes from characters absent from its output alphabet
(Section 4.2), which it cannot produce at all. Both error rates are
computed on the manually corrected line segmentation, so they measure
recognition alone, not segmentation followed by recognition. Figure 2
shows representative test lines. Two further factors make the 0.50% CER
optimistic: test lines are written by officers who also wrote training
lines, and the ground truth includes printed field labels (_e.g_“Né
(e)”) whenever they share a line with handwriting; these labels recur
across records and are easy to learn. We therefore read the figure as
evidence of feasibility within this registry rather than as an estimate
of performance on unseen writers or other registries (Section 6).

### 5.3 Frugality of the recognizer

| Model      | Disk  | Load / warm-up | Peak RSS | Time / image     | Time / line | Images / min |
| ---------- | ----- | -------------- | -------- | ---------------- | ----------- | ------------ |
|            | (MiB) | \(s\)          | (GiB)    | \(s\)            | (ms)        |              |
| Pretrained | 60.8  | 1.1 / 26.1     | 2.30     | $`5.84\pm 1.13`$ | 282         | 10.3         |
| Fine-tuned | 60.8  | 1.1 / 25.3     | 2.49     | $`5.60\pm 1.09`$ | 270         | 10.7         |

Table 4: Inference cost on a six-core laptop CPU (Intel Core i7-10850H,
no GPU), for 50 images in five steps of 10 images (1,037 lines). Peak
RSS is the mean over steps; $`\pm`$ is the standard deviation across
steps.

As expected for two models with the same architecture, their inference
costs are similar (Table 4); the table mainly describes what one Kraken
recognizer costs on a laptop CPU. Both models have the same weight file
(60.8 MiB) and load in about one second. Peak memory stays below 2.6 GiB
for both, far under the 15.6 GiB available; we did not test smaller
machines. The small gaps between the two models (about 4% in time, about
0.2 GiB in peak RSS) are of the same order as the step-to-step variation
and may depend on execution order, so we do not interpret them. The
first inference after loading takes about 25 s and is a one-time cost
paid at startup.

Throughput is about 10 images per minute, or 3.5–3.7 lines per second,
using on average 5.3 of the 6 cores. Extrapolated to the full corpus,
recognition of the 1,568 images takes roughly 2.5 hours on a single
laptop. The first two steps were slower for both models (95th-percentile
latency around 15 s, about 4.5 busy cores) than the last three (about
4.8–5.1 s per image, 5.8 busy cores); since both models process the same
images, we attribute this to system effects such as thermal throttling
or cache state rather than to image content.

#### Training cost.

Fine-tuning (40 epochs) took about four hours on a free-tier Google
Colab GPU. This is a one-off cost, but it is the only step that required
a GPU; we did not measure CPU-only training.

Since the fine-tuned model reaches 0.50% CER on this test set, below our
assumed 2% ceiling, and runs as a small recurrent-convolutional model on
a laptop CPU, it meets our working criteria on the dimensions we
measured, subject to the caveats of Section 5.2.

### 5.4 How recognition errors propagate

| Input text                               | Role-resolution F1 (%) |
| ---------------------------------------- | ---------------------- |
| Ground-truth transcription (upper bound) | 90.3                   |
| Fine-tuned Kraken                        | 81.2                   |
| Pretrained Kraken (no adaptation)        | 44.5                   |

Table 5: Role-resolution F1 as a function of input text quality
($`n=46`$ records, none from the HTR test set).

We keep the extraction stage fixed and vary only its input text, on a
subsample of 46 records that are not part of the HTR test set (Table 5).
Because these records are outside the test set, the fine-tuned
recognizer may have seen some of them during training, so its score is
best read as an optimistic estimate and the recognition cost as a lower
bound. Three observations follow. First, the extraction stage has its
own ceiling: even with perfect text, role resolution reaches only 90.3%
F1, owing to NER and template-classification errors. Second, residual
recognition errors cost about 9 F1 points despite a test-set CER of only
0.50%: we expect recognition errors to concentrate on names and role
markers, exactly the tokens the downstream task depends on. Third, with
the pretrained recognizer F1 drops by a further 36.7 points, making the
task essentially unusable. For information extraction, _where_
recognition errors fall matters as much as how many there are.

### 5.5 Downstream extraction (summary)

The fine-tuned NER model reaches 98.0% micro-F1 over the eight entity
types, evaluated on ground-truth transcriptions of a held-out test split
(1,242 entities; the 179 records are split 70/15/15 by record). On the
three entity types shared with the base model (PER, LOC, and DATE; ORG
is left out because it is too rare in our data to be informative),
adaptation improves weighted F1 from 86.8% to 98.6%, with most of the
gain on PER entities (+18.0 points, against +8.0 for DATE). This
suggests that the base NER model is weakest on local personal names.
Rule-based template classification reaches 87.0% accuracy ($`n=100`$); 8
of its 13 errors confuse templates G2 and G3, which share the same role
sequence, so this confusion does not harm role resolution.

## 6 Discussion and Limitations

#### What the CER gain does and does not show.

The 29-fold CER reduction shows that adaptation helps on this registry,
but it does not explain why. At least three factors are confounded: the
pretrained model is a general-purpose recognizer rather than a French
handwriting model; it cannot output some characters of our corpus; and
the fine-tuned model is tested on writers it has already seen. Our
observation that errors fall on names is qualitative, and the stronger
NER gain on PER than on DATE (+18.0 vs. +8.0 F1) concerns a different
model. That generic models are poorly suited to local names is therefore
a hypothesis to test, with name-span CER, an out-of-alphabet breakdown
and a handwriting-specific baseline, rather than a result of this paper.

#### Frugality is not a single number.

Fine-tuning leaves the architecture, and hence the inference cost,
unchanged by design. The costs that matter for deployment lie elsewhere:
a one-time warm-up of about 25 s, a peak memory of about 2.5 GiB, a
throughput of about 10 images per minute, and, for adaptation itself,
about four hours of GPU training plus the human effort of transcribing
185 records, correcting their segmentation and annotating 179 records
for NER, which we did not time. A record-holding administration can run
the resulting model on a laptop CPU, but adapting it to a new registry
currently requires access to a GPU, or CPU training that we did not
evaluate. Frugality claims should report every cost dimension, and we
did not test lower-end machines or reduced-precision inference.

#### Limitations.

_Single site and possible writer overlap._ All records come from one
registry. The line-level split keeps each record within one split, but
lines by the same officer, or from the same register, can appear in both
training and test data, so the 0.50% CER may overestimate performance on
unseen writers or other registries. A split by register or by writer,
and a second site, are the most important next steps. _Small test set._
The HTR test set contains about 28 records, and marriage and death
records are under-represented; per-type results are diagnostic at best,
and we report no confidence intervals. _What the CER measures._ Printed
field labels are part of the ground truth when they share a line with
handwriting, which lowers CER; we do not report CER on handwritten
characters only, on names, or per template. The recognizer was trained
once, with no repeated runs or variance estimate, and compared with a
single, general-purpose baseline. _Single annotator._ We cannot estimate
how much residual error reflects annotation ambiguity. _Line
segmentation._ CER and WER assume the manually corrected line
segmentation; errors of automatic segmentation are not measured, and the
timings exclude it. _Cost measurements._ Timings come from 50 images on
a single six-core laptop, with the two models run sequentially, and show
a systematic slowdown in the first two steps; they are orders of
magnitude, not precise figures. Older machines, such as the laptop used
for capture, will likely be slower. Quality and cost were measured on
different image samples, and the cost-benchmark images were drawn from
the corpus and may overlap with the annotated records. _Preprocessing
coverage._ Upside-down detection was developed and tested only on the
marriage sub-corpus, where such captures occurred, and no perspective
correction is applied near the binding. _Downstream probe._ The 46 probe
records may overlap the training data of the recognizer and of the NER
model, so its scores are optimistic. _Unablated choices._ The benefit of
RAW over JPEG capture was not tested. _Frozen downstream rules._ Role
resolution relies on rules tied to this registry’s five templates and
will likely need rewriting elsewhere.

## 7 Ethics Statement

The corpus consists of civil-status records of real people, containing
names, family relationships, dates and places of birth, marriage, and
death, and in some cases national identity numbers. Most of the people
concerned were not informed that their records would be used for
research. We did not anonymize or pseudonymize the working corpus:
models were trained and evaluated on the original, identifying text. To
limit risk, we (i) processed the data locally on a single machine,
except for fine-tuning, which ran on a free-tier cloud GPU and therefore
involved uploading the training images and transcriptions to that
service, (ii) show no full record in this paper, only two isolated text
lines in which personal names and record identifiers are masked
(Figure 2), and (iii) do not release the images, transcriptions, or
annotations. The cloud upload runs counter to the aim of this work,
which is to keep identity data under the control of the administration
and the community that hold it \[14\]; future adaptation should use
hardware controlled by the institution.

Chad’s data-protection framework requires processing of personal data to
be declared to, or authorized by, the national agency ANSICE \[35, 34,
6\]. Data collection was carried out with the written permission of the
Municipality of Koumra, which authorizes use of the records for academic
purposes only, requires personal information in published images to be
masked, and excludes any commercial use. We do not claim that this study
has been declared to or authorized by ANSICE, nor that research use of
public civil-status archives is exempt from individual consent under
this framework; we will seek the corresponding authorization before any
release of data or models.

Fine-tuning on a cloud GPU required uploading the training images and
transcriptions to that service. Future adaptation will run on hardware
controlled by the institution, so that identity data remain under the
control of the administration and the community that hold them \[14\].

Systems that make identity records searchable at scale could also
support surveillance or profiling. Local, CPU-only deployment under the
control of the record-holding administration reduces this risk compared
with outsourced processing, but does not remove it.

## 8 Conclusion

We asked whether a frugal pipeline, built from consumer capture
hardware, a few hundred annotated records, and CPU-only inference, can
make handwritten civil-status registers from Sub-Saharan Africa
machine-readable. On a new corpus of 1,568 images of records from a
registry in Chad, fine-tuning a Kraken recognizer for about four hours
on a free-tier GPU reduced CER from 14.53% to 0.50% on corrected line
segmentation and writers seen in training, and the model runs at under
6 s per image and under 2.6 GiB of memory on a laptop CPU. A downstream
extraction task showed that even this small residual error costs about 9
F1 points. These results suggest that recognition for such archives need
not require GPUs or cloud services at deployment time, but they come
from a single registry, a small test set with writer overlap, and a
single baseline. Future work includes evaluation across registers,
writers, and sites; error rates on handwritten text and on names;
handwriting-specific and Transformer baselines; a learning curve over
annotation size; a second annotator to measure agreement; end-to-end
evaluation with automatic line segmentation; and, above all, an
anonymization scheme that still permits record linkage, which must be in
place before any data or trained models are shared with, or deployed by,
the administrations that hold these records.
