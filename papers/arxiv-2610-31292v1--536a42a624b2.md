# Spark-Audio 1.0 Technical Report

iFlytek Spark Audio Team

###### Abstract

This paper introduces Spark-Audio 1.0¹¹ 1
[https://iflytekresearch.iflytek.com/experience/spark-audio](https://iflytekresearch.iflytek.com/experience/spark-audio),
a speech foundation model trained on domestically produced computing
infrastructure. The model accepts text and speech inputs and generates
text outputs. It supports multilingual (99 languages) and multi-dialect
(202 dialects) speech recognition, speech translation, audio perception
and understanding, and both text- and speech-based dialogue, marking a
shift from merely “hearing clearly” to genuinely “understanding” speech.
Architecturally, Spark-Audio 1.0 couples a 0.65B-parameter dense audio
encoder with a 30B-A3B Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) large language model
(LLM). The audio encoder is pretrained with representation-enhanced
BEST-RQ and then refined through multi-task supervised training. The
audio–LLM is trained through staged pretraining, followed by
post-training that combines supervised fine-tuning with on-policy
distillation. In total, the model is trained on 13 million hours of
audio data together with large-scale text data. We evaluate Spark-Audio
1.0 on mainstream audio and text benchmarks, as well as on in-house test
sets drawn from real-world application scenarios. Despite using
substantially less audio training data than Qwen3.5-Omni-Flash,
Spark-Audio 1.0 achieves comparable performance on most audio and text
tasks, outperforms it in multilingual speech recognition, spoken
dialogue, and real-world application scenarios, and approaches the
performance of larger closed-source models. Nevertheless, there remains
room for improvement in instruction following and complex audio
reasoning. Finally, the successful training of Spark-Audio 1.0 on
domestic hardware, including Huawei Ascend 910B and Hygon BW1000 chips,
demonstrates the feasibility of a domestic technical pathway for
building speech foundation models.

>

## 1 Introduction

Speech is one of the most natural and information-rich channels of human
communication, yet conventional automatic speech recognition (ASR)
systems have long been optimized for a single objective: converting
acoustic signals into verbatim text. Such systems excel at “hearing
clearly” but discard much of what makes speech meaningful:
paralinguistic cues such as emotion, speaker identity, age and gender,
and prosodic information that carries intent across languages and
dialects, as well as ambient sound events. As intelligent speech
technology permeates daily work and life through input methods,
translation devices, meeting transcription, customer service, and smart
cockpits, the demand has shifted from transcription accuracy alone
toward holistic speech understanding. Meeting this demand requires a
foundation model that unifies acoustic perception with linguistic
reasoning, rather than a collection of narrowly specialized ASR modules.

Recent progress in large language models (LLMs) (Liu et al., 2024; Guo
et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025; Qwen Team, 2026b; DeepSeek-AI et al., 2026) has made the speech foundation model paradigm (Chu et al., 2023;
Chu et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2024; Dinkel et al., 2025) increasingly
attractive: by projecting continuous audio representations into the
token space of a powerful LLM, a single model can be trained to perform
recognition, translation, audio understanding, and dialogue within one
unified framework. Representative multimodal systems such as the
Qwen-Omni series (Xu et al., 2025; Qwen Team, 2026a) have demonstrated
that a single model can maintain strong performance across text, image,
audio, and video modalities, achieving state-of-the-art results on a
large proportion of audio benchmarks. However, the practical deployment
of such models faces significant challenges, particularly in the Chinese
ecosystem. State-of-the-art speech foundation models are predominantly
trained on foreign computing stacks, creating a hard dependency on
restricted hardware and software ecosystems. The feasibility of training
a competitive speech foundation model end-to-end on purely domestic
infrastructure therefore remains an open and strategically important
question. At the same time, competitive performance is commonly assumed
to require ever-larger training data and model size, which makes
training prohibitively expensive and raises doubts about whether models
trained on less data can serve real-world applications.

This paper presents Spark-Audio 1.0, a speech foundation model that
demonstrates the feasibility of training a competitive model on domestic
infrastructure with substantially less data. The model accepts text and
audio as input and produces text output, and is designed as a
general-purpose base rather than a single-task system. Architecturally,
it pairs a 0.65B-parameter dense audio encoder with a 30B-A3B
Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) LLM, and is trained on 13 million hours of
audio together with large-scale text corpora. Three design decisions
distinguish our approach.

First, a semantically grounded, general-purpose audio encoder. The
encoder is pretrained in three progressively expanding stages. In the
first, self-supervised stage, we revise the BERT-based Speech
pre-Training with Random-projection Quantizer (BEST-RQ) (Chiu et al., 2022) framework into a representation-enhanced variant by replacing the
input of the quantizer with frozen deep semantic representations from a
pretrained multilingual ASR encoder based on the Whisper large-v3
architecture. This injects semantic priors into the prediction targets
without introducing an additional trainable tokenizer, and is designed
to yield more compact discrete targets and a more discriminative
prediction task that strengthens the modeling of fine-grained temporal
structure. In this stage, we progressively expand the pretraining corpus
from approximately 1.5 million hours to 2.6 million hours and ultimately
to 13 million hours. In the second stage, we use large-scale
multilingual speech recognition data for supervised training to
reinforce the learning of multilingual semantic information. In the
third stage, training is extended to multiple perception tasks—namely
speech recognition, emotion, gender, age, and sound event recognition—so
that the encoder acquires task-agnostic, general-purpose speech
representations suitable for the audio-LLM.

Second, a staged training recipe that preserves linguistic competence.
Audio–LLM pretraining proceeds in three stages: adapter-only alignment;
full-parameter joint optimization of the encoder, adapter, and LLM on
large-scale speech, audio, and text data; and long-context extension to
32K tokens with long-form speech and audio tasks. Post-training combines
supervised fine-tuning (SFT) with on-policy distillation (OPD), in which
a frozen text-only teacher supervises the responses that the model
generates from both audio and text inputs. This recipe is designed to
avoid the “cognitive degradation” on text-centric tasks that commonly
afflicts speech-augmented LLMs.

Third, domestic training. Spark-Audio 1.0 is trained on domestic AI
accelerators, including Huawei Ascend 910B and Hygon BW1000 chips, from
audio encoder pretraining through audio–LLM pretraining to
post-training. It establishes a domestic pathway for the entire training
pipeline.

We evaluate Spark-Audio 1.0 on both public benchmarks and in-house test
sets drawn from real-world application scenarios. The public evaluation
covers audio benchmarks (including ASR, speech translation, audio
understanding and spoken dialogue task) and text benchmarks (including
general knowledge, instruction following, math and code task). The
in-house test sets cover almost the same types of audio tasks as the
public benchmarks, together with Chinese ASR under challenging acoustic
conditions. Despite using only one-tenth of the training data of the
comparably sized Qwen3.5-Omni-Flash, it achieves comparable and, in some
cases, superior results on audio tasks, with clear advantages on
realistic, application-oriented tasks, and approaches the performance of
larger closed-source speech foundation models. Meanwhile, it performs
comparably to Qwen3.5-Omni-Flash on text tasks, including general
knowledge, mathematics, and coding. Spark-Audio 1.0 still has room for
improvement in instruction following and complex audio reasoning.

The contributions of this work are threefold:

- •
  Broad multilingual and multi-dialect coverage. We present a speech
  foundation model supporting speech recognition across 99 languages and
  202 dialects.
- •
  Data-efficient training. We show that the representation-enhanced
  BEST-RQ encoder pretraining and the staged training recipe enable a
  model trained on approximately one-tenth of the data of a comparably
  sized counterpart to match or exceed its performance.
- •
  Domestic training. We demonstrate that a competitive speech foundation
  model can be trained end to end on domestic infrastructure.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes
the audio encoder pretraining strategy, including the
representation-enhanced BEST-RQ framework and the multi-task supervised
training stages. Section 3 describes the pretraining and post-training
of Spark-Audio 1.0, covering the staged audio–LLM training recipe
together with SFT and OPD. Section 4 reports evaluation results on
public and in-house benchmarks. Section 5 evaluates the representational
capability of the Spark-Audio 1.0 audio encoder by comparing it with
mainstream audio encoders. Section 6 concludes the paper with a summary
of our findings, limitations, and future work.

## 2 Audio Encoder

### 2.1 Overview

The audio encoder serves as the acoustic front end of Spark-Audio model,
encoding input audio into contextual representations of linguistic
content and non-linguistic acoustic cues. We adopt the Whisper large-v3
encoder architecture (Radford et al., 2023), with a single set of
parameters supporting both streaming and non-streaming inference. As
illustrated in Figure 1, the encoder is trained in three stages. Stage I
performs self-supervised pretraining on up to 13 million hours of
unlabeled multilingual audio using a representation-enhanced variant of
BEST-RQ (Chiu et al., 2022). A fixed random-projection quantizer
converts contextual features from a frozen multilingual ASR teacher into
discrete targets for masked prediction. Stage II adapts the pretrained
encoder to multilingual speech recognition through supervised training.
Stage III introduces multi-task supervision, combining speech
recognition with speaker attribute prediction, emotion recognition, and
sound event recognition to support broader speech and audio perception.

Figure 1: Overview of the audio encoder architecture and training
curriculum. (a) Unified streaming and non-streaming audio encoder based
on the Whisper large-v3 architecture. (b) Representation-enhanced
BEST-RQ pretraining. (c) Three-stage training curriculum for the audio
encoder.

### 2.2 Representation-Enhanced BEST-RQ Pretraining

To learn general-purpose speech representations from unlabeled data at
scale, we build on BEST-RQ (Chiu et al., 2022), which trains an encoder
to predict discrete targets at masked positions. These targets are
generated by a random-projection quantizer whose projection matrix and
codebook are randomly initialized once and kept fixed throughout
pretraining. By avoiding quantizer optimization and iterative
clustering, BEST-RQ provides a simple framework for pretraining on large
speech corpora.

In the original formulation, the quantizer operates directly on
low-level acoustic features. These features reflect linguistic content
as well as speaker and recording characteristics, but their
representation space is not explicitly shaped by a speech recognition
objective. For an encoder intended to support a multilingual
speech–language model, we seek to incorporate information learned
through multilingual ASR supervision into target construction. By
quantizing contextual representations extracted from a pretrained
multilingual ASR model, we seek to improve downstream performance and
accelerate convergence during encoder pretraining, while retaining the
simplicity of fixed random quantization.

To this end, we introduce representation-enhanced BEST-RQ, which applies
the random-projection quantizer to contextual representations from a
frozen multilingual ASR teacher. The teacher is based on Whisper
large-v3 and is trained on approximately 1.5 million hours of
multilingual speech using an ASR objective. Given an input utterance
$`\mathbf{X}`$, the teacher encoder produces

```math
\mathbf{Z}=\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{X})=[\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{T^{\prime}}], \tag{1}
```

where $`\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{T}}`$ denotes the teacher encoder,
$`\mathbf{z}_{t}`$ is its representation at time step $`t`$ after the
final encoder layer normalization, and $`T^{\prime}`$ is the output
sequence length. The teacher and student use the same temporal
subsampling configuration, so the teacher representations match the
temporal resolution of the student outputs.

Each teacher representation $`\mathbf{z}_{t}`$ is passed directly to the
random-projection quantizer to obtain a discrete target for masked
prediction. The teacher parameters, projection matrix, and codebook
remain unchanged throughout pretraining, ensuring that the
target-generation mapping is independent of student updates. This
formulation incorporates knowledge from supervised multilingual ASR into
the prediction targets without introducing an additional trainable
tokenizer or representation-matching network.

##### Discrete Target Construction

Following BEST-RQ (Chiu et al., 2022), we discretize the teacher
representations using a fixed random-projection quantizer. Let
$`\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{q}\times D}`$ denote the projection
matrix, where $`D`$ is the teacher representation dimension and
$`D_{q}`$ is the projection dimension. Let
$`\mathcal{C}=\{\mathbf{c}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{c}_{K}\}`$ denote a
codebook of $`K`$ vectors, with $`\mathbf{c}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{q}}`$.
Both $`\mathbf{A}`$ and $`\mathcal{C}`$ are randomly initialized once
and held fixed throughout pretraining. For each teacher representation
$`\mathbf{z}_{t}`$, we compute

```math
\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{t} \displaystyle=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}_{t}, \tag{2}
```

```math
\displaystyle q_{t} \displaystyle=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}}\left\lVert\operatorname{norm}_{2}(\mathbf{u}_{t})-\operatorname{norm}_{2}(\mathbf{c}_{k})\right\rVert_{2},
```

where
$`\operatorname{norm}_{2}(\mathbf{v})=\mathbf{v}/\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert_{2}`$
denotes $`\ell_{2}`$ normalization. Applying this operation framewise
yields a sequence of discrete code indices,

```math
\mathbf{q}=Q_{\mathrm{RQ}}\bigl(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{X})\bigr)=[q_{1},\ldots,q_{T^{\prime}}], \tag{3}
```

where $`Q_{\mathrm{RQ}}`$ denotes framewise random-projection
quantization. As defined above, $`\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{T}}`$ includes
the teacher encoder’s final layer normalization.

The quantization procedure is retained from BEST-RQ. The change lies in
the representation supplied to the quantizer. Conventional BEST-RQ
constructs targets from low-level acoustic features, whereas our
formulation uses contextual representations produced by the teacher’s
32-layer encoder. We refer to the resulting labels as _semantic-informed
discrete targets_, reflecting the supervised multilingual ASR training
of the representation source.

##### Masked Prediction Objective

Targets are constructed from the unmasked acoustic input $`\mathbf{X}`$,
while the student receives a masked version of the same input:

```math
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}=\operatorname{Mask}(\mathbf{X}). \tag{4}
```

The student encoder produces

```math
\mathbf{H}=\mathcal{E}_{\theta}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}};\mathbf{M})=[\mathbf{h}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{h}_{T^{\prime}}], \tag{5}
```

where $`\theta`$ denotes the encoder parameters and $`\mathbf{M}`$
specifies the attention visibility pattern for streaming or
non-streaming computation. The same teacher-derived targets are used in
both modes.

A linear prediction head with parameters
$`\phi=\{\mathbf{W}_{q},\mathbf{b}_{q}\}`$ maps each student
representation to a categorical distribution over the $`K`$ codebook
entries:

```math
p_{\theta,\phi,t}\bigl(k\mid\widetilde{\mathbf{X}},\mathbf{M}\bigr)=\left[\operatorname{softmax}\left(\mathbf{W}_{q}\mathbf{h}_{t}+\mathbf{b}_{q}\right)\right]_{k}, \tag{6}
```

where $`[\cdot]_{k}`$ denotes the $`k`$-th component of the probability
vector.

Let $`\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{mask}}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,T^{\prime}\}`$
denote the valid positions selected for masked prediction at the encoder
output resolution. These positions are obtained by mapping the input
masking pattern to the encoder time grid, accounting for temporal
subsampling and excluding padding. The pretraining objective is the
average cross-entropy over these positions:

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\text{BEST-RQ}}=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{mask}}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{mask}}}\log p_{\theta,\phi,t}\bigl(q_{t}\mid\widetilde{\mathbf{X}},\mathbf{M}\bigr). \tag{7}
```

Only the student encoder and prediction head are optimized. The teacher
encoder, projection matrix, and codebook remain fixed throughout
pretraining. Supervision is provided through discrete code indices: the
objective involves neither regression to teacher hidden states nor
matching of the teacher’s ASR output distributions. Although the teacher
is trained on approximately 1.5 million hours of labeled multilingual
speech, transcripts are not required for the student pretraining corpus.

Table 1 summarizes the differences between the two target-construction
schemes. We retain the same target-generation procedure across unlabeled
pretraining corpora of 1.5 million, 2.6 million, and 13 million hours.
The data-scaling strategy and the subsequent supervised ASR and

### 2.3 Training Curriculum and Data Scaling

We train the audio encoder through a three-stage curriculum that
combines large-scale representation pretraining, supervised multilingual
ASR adaptation, and multi-task training with textual targets. Stage I
learns from unlabeled multilingual audio through semantic-informed
masked prediction. Stage II directly adapts the pretrained encoder to
multilingual speech recognition using transcribed speech. Stage III
extends the textual supervision beyond transcription to descriptions of
speaker attributes, emotion, and sound events. Table 2 summarizes the
training curriculum.

##### Stage I: Large-scale Representation-enhanced BEST-RQ Pretraining

We first pretrain the shared streaming/non-streaming encoder using the
representation-enhanced BEST-RQ objective described in Sec. 2.2. A
frozen multilingual ASR teacher provides contextual representations of
the unmasked input, and a fixed random-projection quantizer converts its
final layer-normalized outputs into discrete prediction targets. The
student encoder receives masked acoustic inputs and predicts these
targets at the designated masked positions. Transcripts are therefore
not required for the student pretraining corpus, although the target
construction incorporates information learned through the teacher’s ASR
supervision.

We expand the unlabeled pretraining corpus across three data scales:

```math
1.5\,\text{M hours}\;\longrightarrow\;2.6\,\text{M hours}\;\longrightarrow\;13\,\text{M hours}. \tag{8}
```

At the largest scale, the corpus contains approximately 13 million hours
of audio spanning 99 languages. The encoder architecture, frozen
teacher, random quantizer, and masked prediction objective remain
unchanged across these data scales.

Joint streaming and non-streaming training is maintained throughout
Stage I. For each training batch, we select full-context or streaming
computation with equal probability and update the shared encoder under
the selected attention configuration. This stage provides a common
initialization for subsequent supervised adaptation while exposing the
encoder to both attention configurations during large-scale pretraining.

##### Stage II: Multilingual ASR Adaptation

We adapt the pretrained encoder for multilingual ASR using approximately
1.3 million hours of transcribed multilingual speech, drawn from the
same data sources as those used to train the ASR teacher.

The transition from Stage I to Stage II changes the form of supervision:

```math
\underbrace{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{semantic-informed}\\
\text{masked prediction}\end{subarray}}_{\text{Stage I}}\quad\longrightarrow\quad\underbrace{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{direct multilingual}\\
\text{transcript supervision}\end{subarray}}_{\text{Stage II}}. \tag{9}
```

In Stage I, ASR supervision influences the student indirectly through
the teacher-derived discrete targets. In Stage II, the pretrained
encoder is optimized directly for recovering the linguistic content of
speech. The streaming and non-streaming configurations continue to share
a single set of encoder parameters.

##### Stage III: Multi-task Training with Textual Targets

Although ASR provides direct supervision for linguistic content,
transcripts alone do not explicitly describe speaker characteristics,
emotional expression, or non-speech sounds. These additional sources of
information are relevant to broader speech and audio understanding, as
reflected in prior work on universal speech representations and general
audio representation learning (Yang et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2022a;
Chen et al., 2022b; Chen et al., 2023).

Starting from the ASR-adapted encoder, we extend the training mixture to
five tasks:

```math
\mathcal{T}=\{\mathrm{ASR},\mathrm{Gender},\mathrm{Age},\mathrm{Emotion},\mathrm{SoundEvent}\}. \tag{10}
```

All five tasks use textual target sequences. For ASR, the target is the
speech transcript. For the additional tasks, the target expresses the
relevant speaker attribute, expressed emotion, or sound event in natural
language (e.g., _The speaker is a young adult._).

Together, the three stages establish a progression from large-scale
prediction of teacher-derived discrete targets to direct multilingual
ASR supervision and then to broader textual supervision of speech and
audio. The curriculum is designed to equip the shared encoder with
representations that support linguistic content, speaker attributes,
paralinguistic cues, and non-speech acoustic information for downstream
speech–language modeling.

| --------- | ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |

| Stage I   | Representation-enhanced BEST-RQ masked prediction | 1.5M $`\rightarrow`$ 2.6M $`\rightarrow`$ 13M hours of unlabeled multilingual audio                     | Learn multilingual speech representations under streaming and non-streaming attention |
| Stage II  | Supervised multilingual ASR                       | 1.3M hours of transcribed multilingual speech                                                           | Adapt the pretrained encoder for multilingual ASR                                     |

In Stage III, speaker attributes, emotion, and sound events are
expressed as textual targets.

The encoder described above combines a shared parameterization for
streaming and non-streaming computation with a three-stage training
curriculum. Representation-enhanced BEST-RQ pretraining on large-scale
unlabeled multilingual audio is followed by supervised multilingual ASR
adaptation and multi-task training with textual targets. This curriculum
is designed to learn contextual representations that capture linguistic
content alongside speaker attributes, emotional cues, and non-speech
acoustic information. The resulting encoder serves as the acoustic front
end of our audio-LLM. In the following section, we describe its
integration with the language model and the pretraining and
post-training procedures for the complete audio–language system.

## 3 Audio–LLM Pretraining and Post-Training

### 3.1 Pretraining

We combine the pretrained audio encoder with a 30B-A3B MoE language
backbone pretrained on large-scale text corpora. The audio encoder
produces representations at $`50\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}`$. An adapter
module, consisting of an MLP followed by CNN-based temporal downsampling
layers, transforms these representations into a
$`12.5\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}`$ sequence for input to the language model.

Our pretraining employs a three-stage curriculum: adapter alignment,
full-parameter joint training, and long-context extension. The
curriculum is designed to support multilingual and dialectal speech
recognition, speech translation, audio understanding, and long-context
modeling, while maintaining the language backbone’s text capabilities.

#### 3.1.1 Pretraining Data

The pretraining corpus includes ASR for Chinese, Chinese dialects,
English, and other languages; speech translation; audio understanding;
text translation; and general text. The corpus covers 99 languages and
202 dialects across diverse application domains and acoustic conditions,
including noisy and low-volume speech.

We formulate all tasks as conditional text generation. Depending on the
task, the conditioning inputs include audio, a task prompt, text, and
optional contextual information. The model autoregressively generates
the corresponding target text. For ASR, prompts specify the
transcription language; for speech translation, they specify the source
and target languages; and for Chinese-dialect ASR, they specify both the
language and the required output format.

We organize the data by task, language, source, annotation quality, and
duration, and adjust the training mixture across stages. Stage I uses a
relatively small amount of supervised data for adapter alignment. Stage
II primarily uses large-scale weakly supervised data, supplemented with
high-quality supervised data. Stage III primarily uses supervised data,
with greater emphasis on long-form audio, long text, and
context-dependent tasks.

#### 3.1.2 Multi-Stage Training

Our training curriculum comprises three successive stages that
progressively expand the scope of optimization, data coverage, and
context length. It begins with adapter alignment, proceeds to
large-scale full-parameter training for general capability acquisition,
and concludes with long-context extension using an increased proportion
of high-quality and long-form data.

$`\beta_{1}=0.9`$, $`\beta_{2}=0.95`$, $`\epsilon=10^{-8}`$, and a
weight decay of 0.01. We clip the gradient norm at 1 and use a
label-smoothing coefficient of 0.05.

##### Stage I: Encoder Alignment

We freeze both the audio encoder and the MoE language backbone and train
only the adapter, including its MLP and CNN-based downsampling layers.
This stage primarily uses ASR and audio understanding data to align
audio representations with the language model’s input space. We exclude
reasoning-oriented data and focus on establishing alignment between the
audio encoder and the LLM before joint training.

##### Stage II: Large-Scale Joint Training

We unfreeze all components and jointly optimize the audio encoder,
adapter, and language backbone. This stage primarily uses weakly
supervised data spanning diverse languages and acoustic conditions,
supplemented with high-quality supervised samples. The audio tasks
include multilingual and dialectal speech recognition, speech
translation, and audio understanding. The data cover diverse domains,
recording conditions, and annotation quality levels, including noisy and
low-volume speech.

The text-only corpus comprises general-domain, translation, science and
mathematics content, multilingual, and code data. We retain text-only
data during joint training to support continued learning of text
capabilities alongside speech and audio tasks.

##### Stage III: Long-Context Extension

We continue joint optimization of all components while extending the
maximum sequence length from 4K to 32K tokens. We increase the
proportion of high-quality speech data and introduce long-form tasks,
including long-form speech recognition, long-form audio understanding,
and speech recognition conditioned on textual context. The text-only
mixture places greater emphasis on long documents, science and
mathematics content, and code.

We also increase the maximum audio chunk duration from
$`30\text{\,}\mathrm{s}`$ to $`45\text{\,}\mathrm{s}`$. Longer
recordings are split into chunks, encoded independently, and
concatenated in temporal order at the embedding level. This stage is
designed to support tasks that require integrating information across
longer audio and text sequences.

#### 3.1.3 Batch Construction and Long-Context Computation

To preserve the desired mixture across data sources, we first stratify
the training data by source and then construct local batch sequences
whose source proportions approximate the prescribed mixture. We
deliberately avoid sorting samples by audio length to improve
distributional balance and training stability.

Because microbatches can contain different numbers of target tokens, we
normalize the training loss by the number of valid target tokens. Let
$`m_{b,t}\in\{0,1\}`$ indicate whether position $`t`$ in sample $`b`$
contributes to the loss, and let $`\ell_{b,t}`$ denote the corresponding
label-smoothed cross-entropy loss. The objective for each optimizer
update is

```math
\mathcal{L}=\frac{\sum_{b,t}m_{b,t}\ell_{b,t}}{\sum_{b,t}m_{b,t}}, \tag{11}
```

where the sums cover all valid target tokens contributing to one
optimizer update, across gradient-accumulation steps and data-parallel
workers.

For long-context training, Stage III combines eight-way expert
parallelism (EP) with four-way context parallelism (CP). Audio chunks
are first distributed across CP ranks along the batch dimension, and
each rank encodes its assigned chunks. The resulting audio embeddings
are combined with text-token embeddings to form the complete multimodal
sequence, which is then partitioned along the sequence dimension for
context-parallel computation (NVIDIA Corporation, 2026).

### 3.2 Post-Training

#### 3.2.1 Supervised Fine-Tuning

Following pretraining, we perform SFT using high-quality multi-task data
organized in ChatML format. This stage aims to consolidate the model’s
fundamental speech and language capabilities while enhancing its ability
to understand complex instructions, perform cognitive reasoning, and
engage in natural interactions. To this end, we construct a diverse SFT
dataset covering fundamental audio tasks, cognitive tasks, and
interaction tasks.

For fundamental audio tasks, we continue training the model on ASR,
translation, and audio understanding. We carefully select a high-quality
subset of the data employed in the third stage of pretraining to further
strengthen the model’s existing capabilities. Beyond these fundamental
tasks, we introduce additional supervised data to enhance the model’s
cognitive and interactive abilities. For cognitive tasks, we adopt two
complementary supervision formats: text-to-text (T2T) and speech-to-text
(S2T). The T2T data encompass reasoning, mathematics, and
knowledge-based tasks, while the S2T data are constructed by converting
the corresponding text instructions into spoken inputs using
text-to-speech (TTS) synthesis. By providing supervision across both
modalities, this design aims to narrow the gap in instruction-following
and reasoning capabilities between speech and text inputs. We also
incorporate interaction data to improve the naturalness and
expressiveness of the model’s responses.

With this diverse multi-task supervision, we train the model for one
epoch, employing a cosine learning rate decay schedule that gradually
reduces the learning rate from $`1\times 10^{-5}`$ to
$`1\times 10^{-6}`$.

#### 3.2.2 On-Policy Distillation

##### Dual-Trajectory Formulation

Following pretraining and supervised fine-tuning, we apply
dual-trajectory on-policy distillation (OPD) to transfer reasoning and
instruction-following behavior from a frozen text-only teacher to the
audio–language student. The two trajectories use the same student model
under audio and text conditioning, respectively. The audio-conditioned
trajectory transfers teacher supervision to spoken inputs, whereas the
text-conditioned trajectory provides continued supervision for the
student’s text-based capabilities. This joint design aims to improve
audio-conditioned response generation while limiting degradation in
text-conditioned performance.

##### Student Rollouts and Teacher Conditioning

Let $`\mathcal{B}=\{(S_{b},T_{b})\}_{b=1}^{B}`$ denote a minibatch of
paired inputs, where $`S_{b}`$ is a spoken input and $`T_{b}`$ is its
corresponding transcript. We write $`x_{b}^{a}=S_{b}`$ and
$`x_{b}^{t}=T_{b}`$ for the two input modalities; shared task
instructions and dialogue context are implicit in the conditioning
inputs. For each pair, the student generates a separate textual response
under each modality:

```math
\mathbf{y}_{b}^{m}\sim\rho_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x_{b}^{m}),\qquad m\in\{a,t\}, \tag{12}
```

where $`\rho_{\theta}`$ denotes the decoding distribution induced by the
current student and the sampling settings specified below. Both
trajectories produce text; their superscripts identify the input
modality.

The teacher evaluates each trajectory using the transcript and the
corresponding student-generated response prefix. At response position
$`i`$, the student and teacher logits for token $`v`$ are

```math
\displaystyle z_{\theta,b,i}^{m}(v) \displaystyle=z_{\theta}(v\mid x_{b}^{m},\mathbf{y}_{b,<i}^{m}), \tag{13}
```

```math
\displaystyle z_{\phi,b,i}^{m}(v) \displaystyle=z_{\phi}(v\mid T_{b},\mathbf{y}_{b,<i}^{m}),
```

where $`\theta`$ and $`\phi`$ denote the student and frozen teacher
parameters, respectively. The teacher therefore conditions on text in
both trajectories, but its next-token distributions are evaluated
separately because the two student-generated prefixes may differ. This
exposes the student to teacher supervision at prefixes visited by its
own decoding policy.

##### Distillation over Teacher-Selected Support

Following Fu et al. (2026), we compute a local reverse-KL objective on a
teacher-selected token set. Let $`\mathcal{V}`$ denote the shared text
vocabulary with aligned token identities between the two models. For
each trajectory and response position, we select

```math
\mathcal{K}_{b,i}^{m}=\operatorname{TopK}_{K_{\mathrm{KD}}}\left(\{z_{\phi,b,i}^{m}(v)\}_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\right), \tag{14}
```

where $`\operatorname{TopK}_{K_{\mathrm{KD}}}`$ returns the identities
of the $`K_{\mathrm{KD}}`$ tokens with the highest teacher logits.

For $`v\in\mathcal{K}_{b,i}^{m}`$, we independently normalize the
student and teacher logits on this same support using the distillation
temperature $`\tau_{\mathrm{KD}}`$:

```math
\displaystyle\widetilde{p}_{\theta,b,i}^{m}(v) \displaystyle=\frac{\exp(z_{\theta,b,i}^{m}(v)/\tau_{\mathrm{KD}})}{\sum_{u\in\mathcal{K}_{b,i}^{m}}\exp(z_{\theta,b,i}^{m}(u)/\tau_{\mathrm{KD}})}, \tag{15}
```

```math
\displaystyle\widetilde{q}_{\phi,b,i}^{m}(v) \displaystyle=\frac{\exp(z_{\phi,b,i}^{m}(v)/\tau_{\mathrm{KD}})}{\sum_{u\in\mathcal{K}_{b,i}^{m}}\exp(z_{\phi,b,i}^{m}(u)/\tau_{\mathrm{KD}})}.
```

The per-position distillation loss is

```math
\displaystyle d_{b,i}^{m} \displaystyle=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\widetilde{p}_{\theta,b,i}^{m}\,\|\,\widetilde{q}_{\phi,b,i}^{m}) \tag{16}
```

```math
\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{K}_{b,i}^{m}}\widetilde{p}_{\theta,b,i}^{m}(v)\log\frac{\widetilde{p}_{\theta,b,i}^{m}(v)}{\widetilde{q}_{\phi,b,i}^{m}(v)}.
```

This objective matches the conditional distributions within the selected
support. It limits the distribution comparison to $`K_{\mathrm{KD}}`$
candidates per position and does not equal the full-vocabulary reverse
KL.

##### Joint Optimization

Let $`n_{b}^{m}`$ denote the length of response $`\mathbf{y}_{b}^{m}`$,
and let $`M_{b,i}^{m}\in\{0,1\}`$ indicate whether a response position
contributes to the loss. Padding and designated control-token positions
are masked; input-prompt positions are not included in the response
loss. We normalize each trajectory loss by its own number of valid
response tokens:

```math
\mathcal{L}_{m}=\frac{\sum_{b=1}^{B}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{b}^{m}}M_{b,i}^{m}d_{b,i}^{m}}{\sum_{b=1}^{B}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{b}^{m}}M_{b,i}^{m}},\qquad m\in\{a,t\}. \tag{17}
```

The joint objective is

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPD}}=\mathcal{L}_{a}+\lambda\mathcal{L}_{t}, \tag{18}
```

with $`\lambda=1`$, giving equal coefficients to the two separately
normalized losses. The teacher remains frozen, and the sampled
trajectories are treated as fixed during each gradient update. Only the
student’s trainable parameters are updated through the distillation
objective.

##### Training Configuration

We use $`K_{\mathrm{KD}}=64`$ and $`\tau_{\mathrm{KD}}=0.5`$ for
distillation. Student rollouts are generated with temperature
$`\tau_{\mathrm{gen}}=0.5`$, a nucleus-sampling threshold of
$`p_{\mathrm{gen}}=0.9`$, a top-$`k`$ sampling cutoff of
$`k_{\mathrm{gen}}=50`$, and a repetition penalty of $`1.1`$. These
sampling settings define $`\rho_{\theta}`$ in Eq. (12) and are separate
from the support size and temperature used in the distillation loss.

Each optimization step combines the audio- and text-conditioned losses.
We use a learning rate of $`5\times 10^{-6}`$ with cosine decay, a
warmup ratio of $`0.5\%`$, a gradient-accumulation factor of $`1`$, and
gradient clipping. The maximum sequence length is $`8{,}192`$ tokens.
Training runs for three epochs, and the reported results use the
checkpoint obtained at the end of the second epoch.

##### Training Scope and Transfer

This stage uses tens of thousands of paired spoken and textual inputs
for reasoning and instruction following. No additional examples with ASR
or general audio-perception targets are introduced during this stage.
Nevertheless, we observe modest improvements on a subset of recognition
and audio-perception evaluations. These gains may reflect improved use
of linguistic information under audio conditioning, together with better
task interpretation and response generation.

## 4 Evaluation

We evaluate Spark-Audio 1.0 across speech recognition, speech-to-text
translation, audio understanding, spoken interaction, and text-only
tasks. Public benchmarks provide comparisons across a broad range of
capabilities, while in-house evaluations assess performance in the
product and developer scenarios targeted by the system. We additionally
evaluate Chinese ASR under challenging acoustic conditions. Tables 4–7
summarize the results.

### 4.1 Evaluation Setup

##### Public Audio Benchmarks

We evaluate multilingual ASR on FLEURS (Conneau et al., 2022).
Additional Chinese ASR benchmarks include AISHELL6-Whisper (Li et al.,
2026), which contains whispered and parallel normally voiced Mandarin
speech; the internet and meeting subsets of WenetSpeech (Zhang et al.,
2022); and SpeechIO (SpeechColab, 2024). KeSpeech (Tang et al., 2021)
provides a complementary evaluation of Mandarin and regional speech
varieties. English ASR is evaluated on FLEURS-en, LibriSpeech (Panayotov
et al., 2015), speechocean762 (Zhang et al., 2021), and the TED
(Hernandez et al., 2018) evaluation set, covering read speech,
non-native speech, and public talks. We also report average FLEURS error
rates over three language groups (14, 20, and 64 languages; see the
footnotes of Table 4).

Beyond transcription, we evaluate audio understanding on MMAR (Ma et
al., 2025), AIR-Bench (Yang et al., 2024), and MMSU (Wang et al., 2026),
and spoken interaction on VoiceBench (Chen et al., 2026). Speech-to-text
translation (S2TT) is evaluated in both Chinese-to-English and
English-to-Chinese directions using FLEURS.

##### In-house Audio Evaluations

Our in-house evaluations cover general Chinese ASR, a 202-dialect
recognition corpus, multilingual ASR, spoken interaction, and
Chinese–English S2TT. The evaluation data are drawn from real-world
application scenarios. Multilingual ASR results are reported for a
14-language group and an 18-language group. Spoken interaction is
evaluated on general interaction and instruction following. A separate
Chinese ASR evaluation focuses on high-noise and low-volume speech to
characterize performance under challenging acoustic conditions.

##### Text-only Benchmarks

We assess text-based knowledge with MMLU-Pro (Wang et al., 2024), CMMLU
(Li et al., 2024), and C-Eval (Huang et al., 2023); verifiable
instruction following with IFEval (Zhou et al., 2023) and IFBench
(Pyatkin et al., 2025); mathematical problem solving with MATH-500
(Hendrycks et al., 2021) and GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021); and code
generation with HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021). These evaluations
characterize the text capabilities of the final audio–language model
alongside its speech and audio performance.

##### Metrics and Comparison Systems

ASR performance is measured using word error rate (WER) or character
error rate (CER), expressed as percentages, and S2TT performance is
measured using BLEU. Public audio understanding and spoken interaction
results are reported using the metrics indicated in Table 4. For
in-house spoken interaction, a DeepSeek judge assigns each response a
score from 0 to 5. We report the _full-score rate_ (FSR), defined as the
percentage of responses receiving the maximum score:

```math
\mathrm{FSR}=\frac{100}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\{s_{i}=5\}, \tag{19}
```

where $`N`$ is the number of evaluated responses and $`s_{i}`$ is the
judge score assigned to response $`i`$.

## 5 Representation Quality of the Audio Encoder

We evaluate the Spark-Audio encoder on speech recognition and audio
understanding tasks. Specifically, we compare it with the Whisper
large-v3 encoder (Radford et al., 2023) and the Qwen3-Omni Audio
Transformer (AuT) encoder (Xu et al., 2025).

##### Experimental Setup

Each encoder is paired with the same Qwen3-4B language model (Yang et
al., 2025). Except for the starred multilingual AuT results, we freeze
the encoder and language model, training only an adapter to map encoder
representations into the language model’s input space. All adapter-only
experiments use the same training data (Chinese ASR, English ASR,
multilingual ASR, and audio understanding), optimization settings, and
trainable parameter budget.

The available AuT encoder weights were jointly optimized with the
Qwen3-Omni MoE backbone (Xu et al., 2025). When AuT is paired with
Qwen3-4B, we find that adapter-only training does not fully reveal its
multilingual capability. We therefore adopt a two-stage alignment for
the starred FLEURS results: the first stage trains only the adapter,
while the second stage jointly trains the encoder and adapter on the
same data and fine-tunes the language model with LoRA.

##### Speech Recognition

Table 8 shows the ASR results. In the adapter-only setting, the
Spark-Audio encoder outperforms Whisper large-v3 on all 20 Chinese and
English test sets and AuT on 19 of the 20, with improvements across both
public benchmarks and in-house evaluation sets. It achieves average
error rates of 8.93% on the 15 Chinese test sets and 6.17% on the five
English test sets, compared with 10.47% and 7.35%, respectively, for
AuT.

On FLEURS, the Spark-Audio encoder achieves a macro-averaged error rate
of 26.24% across 37 languages, an absolute reduction of 19.41 percentage
points relative to Whisper large-v3 and 12.22 percentage points relative
to AuT with two-stage adaptation. For the 16 languages that overlap with
Qwen3-Omni’s reported speech-input coverage, the average error rate is
16.40%, corresponding to reductions of 6.99 and 2.99 percentage points,
respectively. On the other 21 languages, it reduces the average error by
19.25 percentage points relative to AuT with two-stage adaptation. At
the language level, the Spark-Audio encoder obtains lower error rates
than Whisper on 36 of 37 languages and AuT on 34 of 37. These results
demonstrate the advantage of the Spark-Audio encoder in multilingual
speech recognition.

## 6 Conclusion

In this paper, we present Spark-Audio 1.0, a speech foundation model
trained entirely on domestic AI accelerators. It supports a broad range
of tasks, including multilingual speech recognition, multi-dialect
speech recognition, speech translation, audio perception and
understanding, and text- and speech-based dialogue. Notably, it supports
speech recognition in 99 languages and 202 dialects. The model couples a
0.65B-parameter dense audio encoder with a 30B-A3B Mixture-of-Experts
language model. The encoder is pretrained with representation-enhanced
BEST-RQ and then refined through multi-task supervised training. We
evaluate Spark-Audio 1.0 on public benchmarks as well as in-house test
sets derived from real-world application scenarios. Trained on 13
million hours of audio data with extensive text data, Spark-Audio 1.0 is
competitive with the similarly sized Qwen3.5-Omni-Flash on
Chinese–English speech-to-text translation, audio perception, and
text-based dialogue (knowledge, math, and code), and outperforms it on
multilingual speech recognition and spoken interaction. It shows
particularly clear advantages on realistic, application-oriented tasks.
Spark-Audio 1.0 also leaves room for improvement, particularly in
instruction following and complex audio reasoning. In future work, we
will explore more effective use of audio data and broaden coverage
across acoustic conditions and application domains. We will also extend
the model to hour-long speech recognition, multi-speaker speech
recognition, and spatially aware audio understanding.
