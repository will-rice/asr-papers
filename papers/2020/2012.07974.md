# Introduction

The advent of deep learning techniques has dramatically improved accuracy of speech recognition models . Deep learning techniques first saw success by replacing the Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) of the Acoustic Model (AM) part of the conventional speech recognition systems with the Feed-Forward Deep Neural Networks (FF-DNNs), further with Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) such as the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks or Convonlutional Neural Networks (CNNs). In addition to this, there have been improvements in noise robustness by using models motivated by auditory processing , data augmentation techniques , and beam-forming . Thanks to these advances, voice assistant devices such as Google Home and Amazon Alexa have been widely used at home environments.

Nevertheless, it was not easy to run such high-performance speech recognition systems on devices largely because of the size of the Weighted Finite State Transducer (WFST) handling the lexicon and the language model. Fortunately, all-neural end-to-end (E2E) speech recognition systems were introduced which do not need a large WFST or an n-gram Language Model (LM) . These complete end-to-end systems have started surpassing the performance of the conventional WFST-based decoders with a very large training dataset and a better choice of target unit such as Byte Pair Encoded (BPE) subword units.

In this paper, we provide a comprehensive review of the various components and algorithms of an end-to-end speech recognition system. In Sec. <a href="#sec:nn_components" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:nn_components">2</a>, we give a brief overview of the various neural building blocks of an E2E Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) model. The most popular E2E ASR architectures are reviewed in Sec. <a href="#sec:architectures" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:architectures">3</a>. Additional techniques used to improve the performance of E2E ASR models are discussed in Sec. <a href="#sec:enhance" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:enhance">4</a>. Techniques used for compression and quantization of the all-neural E2E ASR models are covered in Sec. <a href="#sec:compress" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:compress">5</a>. Sec. <a href="#sec:conclude" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:conclude">6</a> gives a summary of the paper.

One of notable advantages of gated RNNs is that they require relatively smaller memory footprint compared to other models as shown in Table <a href="#tbl:nn_components" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="tbl:nn_components">3</a>. Their another advantage is streaming capability if unidirectional models (*e.g.* uni-directional LSTMs or GRUs) are employed. Because of these advantages, at the time of writing this paper, most of the commercially available end-to-end on-device speech recognition systems are based on LSTMs . However, as shown in Table <a href="#tbl:nn_components" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="tbl:nn_components">3</a>, LSTMs have disadvantages in terms of *intra-sequence parallelism*, since the computation needs to be done sequentially.

Various CNN-based approaches have been successfully employed in building end-to-end speech recognition systems . These approaches are characterized by a group of filters and a nonlinear rectifier as shown in Fig. <a href="#fig:cnn_structure" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="fig:cnn_structure">2</a>. ``` math

Speech recognition is a task of finding the *sequence-to-sequence* mapping from an input sequence of acoustic features to a output sequence of labels. Let us denote the input and output sequences by $`%
  \bm{\mathsf{x}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
_{[0:M]}`$ and $`y_{0:L}`$ as shown below:
``` math
\begin{align}
    %
  \bm{\mathsf{x}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
_{[0:M]} & = \left[%
  \bm{\mathsf{x}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
_{[0]},\, %
  \bm{\mathsf{x}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
_{[1]},\,
    %
  \bm{\mathsf{x}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
_{[2]},\, \cdots, \,
    %
  \bm{\mathsf{x}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
_{[M-1]}\right], \\
    y_{0:L} & = \big[y_0,\,y_1,\,y_2,\, \cdots,\,y_{L-1}\big],
  \end{align}
  \label{eq:in_out}
```
where $`M`$ and $`L`$ are the lengths of the input acoustic feature sequence and the output label sequence, respectively. The sequence notation adopted in this paper including <a href="#eq:in_out" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:in_out">[eq:in_out]</a> follows the `Python` array slice notation. In this paper, by convention, we use a *bracket* to represent a periodically sampled sequence such as the acoustic feature, and use a *subscript* to represent a non-periodic sequence such as the output label. Fig. <a href="#fig:architectures" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="fig:architectures">8</a> shows structures of end-to-end all neural speech recognition systems. Even though we use a stack of LSTMs in Fig. <a href="#fig:architectures" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="fig:architectures">8</a>, any kinds of neural network components described in Sec. <a href="#sec:nn_components" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:nn_components">2</a> may be employed instead of LSTMs.

## Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC)

The simplest way of implementing an end-to-end speech recognizer is using a stack of neural network layers with a Connectionist Temporal Classification loss as shown in Fig. <a href="#fig:ctc" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="fig:ctc">5</a>. This model defines a probability distribution over the set of output labels augmented with a special blank symbol, $`{\ensuremath{\langle b \rangle}}`$. We define the set of all possible alignments $`\mathcal{B}^{\text{CTC}}\left(%
  \bm{\mathsf{x}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
_{[0:M]},\, y_{0:L}\right)`$, as the set of all label sequences $`%
  \bm{\mathsf{z}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
_{[0:M]}`$ where $`z[m] \in \mathcal{Y} \cup 
{\langle b \rangle},\,0\le m < M`$, such that $`%
  \bm{\mathsf{z}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
_{[0:M]}`$ is identical to $`y_{0:l}`$ after removing all blank symbols $`{\ensuremath{\langle b \rangle}}`$. $`\mathcal{Y}`$ is the set of the entire alphabet of the output labels. Under this assumption, the posterior probability of the output label sequence is given by the following equation:
``` math
\begin{align}
  P\left( y_{0:L} \big| %
  \bm{\mathsf{x}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
_{[0:M]} \right) = \sum_{
    \substack{z_{[0:M]} \in \\ \mathcal{B}^{\text{CTC}}\left(%
  \bm{\mathsf{x}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
_{[0:M]},\,
    y_{0:L}\right) }}
  { \Pi_{m=0}^{M-1} P\left(z_{[m]} \big| %
  \bm{\mathsf{x}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
_{[0:M]} \right)  }.
      \label{eq:ctc_prob}
\end{align}
```
The CTC loss is defined by the following equation:
``` math
\begin{align}
  \mathbbm{L}_{\text{CTC}} = - E 
    \Big[
      \log 
        \left( 
          P\left( y_{0:L} \big| %
  \bm{\mathsf{x}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
_{[0:M]} \right) 
        \right) 
    \Big].
\end{align}
```
The parameters of a model with the CTC loss are updated using the *forward-backward recursion* assuming conditional indepedence , which is similar to the forward-backward algorithm used for training Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) .

## Recurrent Neural Network-Transducer (RNN-T)

In spite of its simplicity, the model described in Sec. <a href="#sec:ctc" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:ctc">3.1</a> has several shortcomings including the conditional independence assumption during the model training and the lack of explicit feedback paths from the output label. An improved version of this model is the RNN-T model shown in Fig. <a href="#fig:rnn_t_diagram" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="fig:rnn_t_diagram">6</a>. In this model, there is an explicit feedback path from the output label to the prediction network which plays a similar role to an LM. The probability model of an RNN-T based model is similar to the CTC model:
``` math
\begin{align}
  P\left( y_{0:L} \big| %
  \bm{\mathsf{x}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
_{[0:M]} \right) = \sum_{
    \substack{z_{[0:M+L]} \in \\ \mathcal{B}^{\text{RNN-T}}\big(%
  \bm{\mathsf{x}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
_{[0:M]},\,
    y_{0:L}\big) }}
  { \Pi_{u=0}^{M+L-1} P\left(z_{[u]} \big| %
  \bm{\mathsf{x}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
_{[0:M]} \right)  },
      \label{eq:ctc_prob}
\end{align}
```
where $`\mathcal{B}^{\text{RNN-T}}\left(%
  \bm{\mathsf{x}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
_{[0:M]},\, y_{0:L}\right)`$ is the set of all possible label sequences $`%
  \bm{\mathsf{z}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
_{[0:M+L]}`$ where $`z[u] \in \mathcal{Y} \cup 
{\langle b \rangle},\, 0\le u < M+L`$, such that after removing blank symbols $`\langle
b \rangle`$ from $`%
  \bm{\mathsf{z}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
_{[0:M+L]}`$, it becomes the same as $`y_{0:L}`$. As in Sec. <a href="#sec:ctc" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:ctc">3.1</a>, $`\mathcal{Y}`$ is the set of the entire alphabet of the output labels.

## Attention-based Models

Another popular end-to-end speech recognition approach is employing the attention-mechanism as shown in Fig. <a href="#fig:attention_diagram" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="fig:attention_diagram">7</a> . In attention-based approaches, we use the attention between the encoder and decoder hidden outputs. The equation for encoder-decoder attention is basically the same as <a href="#eq:attention_eq" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:attention_eq">[eq:attention_eq]</a>:
``` math
\begin{align}
    e_{[m],\, l} & = \text{\it Energy}(%
  \bm{\mathsf{h}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
^{(\text{\it enc})}_{[m]},%
  \bm{\mathsf{h}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
^{(\text{\it
    dec})}_{l-1}) \label{eq:att_energy} \\
    a_{[m],\, l} & = \text{softmax}(e_{[m],\, l}) \\
    %
  \bm{\mathsf{c}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
_l & = \sum_{m=0}^{M-1} a_{[m],\, {l}} %
  \bm{\mathsf{h}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
^{(\text{\it
    enc})}_{[m]},
  \end{align}
```
where $`%
  \bm{\mathsf{c}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
_l`$ is the *context vector* which is used as the input to the decoder. $`%
  \bm{\mathsf{h}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
^{\text{({\it enc})}}[m]`$ and $`%
  \bm{\mathsf{h}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
^{\text{({\it dec})}}_{l-1}`$ are hidden outputs from the encoder at time $`m`$ and from the decoder at the label index $`l-1`$, respectively. $`e_{[m],\, l}`$ in <a href="#eq:att_energy" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:att_energy">[eq:att_energy]</a> is the *energy* for the input time index $`m`$ and the output label index $`l`$.

## Monotonic Chunkwise Attention(MoChA)-based Models

Although the attention-based approach in <a href="#sec:attention" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:attention">3.3</a> has been quite successful, on-line streaming recognition with this approach has been a challenge. This is because the entire sequence of input features must be encoded before the decoder starts generating the first output label. Several variations of the attention including Monotonic Chunkwise Attention (MoChA) have been proposed to resolve this problem. In the MoChA model, there are two attention mechanisms: a hard monotonic attention followed by a soft chunkwise attention. The hard monotonic attention is employed to determine which element should be attended from a sequence of hidden encoder outputs $`%
  \bm{\mathsf{h}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
^{\text{({\it enc})}}_{[m]}`$. The hard monotonic attention is obtained from the hidden encoder output $`%
  \bm{\mathsf{h}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
^{\text{({\it enc})}}_{[m]}`$ at the time index $`m`$ and the hidden decoder output $`%
  \bm{\mathsf{h}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
^{(\text{\it dec})}_{l-1}`$ at the output label index $`l-1`$ as follows:
``` math
\begin{align}
    e^{\text{({\it mono})}}_{[m],\,l} & = \text{\it MonotonicEnergy}
      (%
  \bm{\mathsf{h}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
^{(\text{\it enc})}_{[m]}, %
  \bm{\mathsf{h}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
^{(\text{\it dec})}_{l-1}) \\
    a^{\text{({\it mono})}}_{[m], l} & = \sigma(e^{\text{(\it mono)}}_{[m],\,
    {l}}) \\
    z_{[m], l} & \sim \text{\it Bernoullli}
      \left(a^{(\text{\it mono})}_{[m],\,{l}}\right),
    \label{eq:hard_output}
  \end{align}
```
where $`\sigma(\cdot)`$ is a logistic sigmoid function and *MonotonicEnergy* is the energy function defined as follows :
``` math
\begin{align}
  \text{\it MonotonicEnergy}&(%
  \bm{\mathsf{h}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
^ {\text{({\it enc})}}_{[m]},\;%
  \bm{\mathsf{h}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
^{\text{({\it dec})}}_{l-1})
  \nonumber \\
  \;  = g \frac{v^{\intercal}}{\| v \|} & \tanh (W^{\text{({\it dec})}}
  %
  \bm{\mathsf{h}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
^{\text{({\it dec})}}_{l-1} + W^{\text{({\it enc})}}  %
  \bm{\mathsf{h}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
^
  {\text{({\it enc})}}_{[m]} + b ) + r,
\end{align}
```
where $`v, W^{\text{({\it dec})}}, W^{\text{({\it enc})}}, b, g`$, and $`r`$ are learnable variables. After finding out the position to attend using <a href="#eq:hard_output" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:hard_output">[eq:hard_output]</a>, a soft attention with a fixed chunk size is employed to generate the context vector that will be given as an input to the decoder. We refer readers interested in the more detailed structure of MoChA to . A block schematic of an on-device speech recognizer based on MoChA is shown in Fig. <a href="#fig:asru_2019_attention_diagram" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="fig:asru_2019_attention_diagram">9</a>.

## Combination with a non-streaming model

Even though Bidirectional LSTMs (BLSTMs) perform significantly better than unidirectional counterparts for on-device applications , latency requirement usually prohibits their usage. To get the advantage of BLSTMs without significantly affecting overall latency, we proposed a recognition system combining the streaming MoChA model with a batch full attention model in . The entire structure of this system is shown in Fig. <a href="#fig:shallow_bfa_diagram" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="fig:shallow_bfa_diagram">10</a>. On the other side, one backward LSTM layer on top of the shared unidirectional layer comprise a BLSTM layer, which is followed by a full attention model. When the user finishes speaking, then this full attention model generates low-latency batch speech recognition result. The latency impact is relatively small since there is only one layer of backward LSTM layer in the encoder. ## Shallow-fusion with language models

End-to-end speech recognition models discussed in Sec. <a href="#sec:architectures" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:architectures">3</a> are trained using only paired speech-text data. Compared to traditional AM-LM approaches where an LM is often trained using a much larger text corpus possibly containing billions of sentences , the end-to-end speech recognition model sees much limited number of word sequences during the training phase. To get further performance improvement, researchers have proposed various techniques of incorporating external language models such as *shallow-fusion* , *deep-fusion* , and *cold-fusion* . Among them, in spite of its simplicity, shallow-fusion seems to be more effective than other approaches . In shallow-fusion, the log probability from the end-to-end speech recognition model is linearly interpolated with the probability from the language model as follows:
``` math
\begin{align}
  \log p_{\text{sf}}\left( y_l \big| %
  \bm{\mathsf{x}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
_{[0:m]}  \right) & =  
    \log p \left(y_l \big| %
  \bm{\mathsf{x}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
_{[0:m]}, \hat{y}_{0:l} \right) \nonumber  + \lambda  \log p_{\text{lm}} \left( y_l \big|  \hat{y}_{0:l}\right)
\end{align}
```
where $`p_{\text{lm}} \left( y_l \big|  \hat{y}_{0:l}\right)`$ is the probability of predicting the label $`y_l`$ from the LM, and the $`p \left(y_l \big| %
  \bm{\mathsf{x}}%
 % {\mathbf{#1}}%
_{[0:m]}\right)`$ is the posterior probability obtained from the end-to-end speech recognition model.

Shallow-fusion with an LM is helpful in enhancing speech performance in general domains, thus, most of the state of the art speech recognition results on publicly available test sets (*(*e.g) LibriSpeech) are obtained with this technique . In addition to this, this technique is also very useful for enhancing performance in special domain like personal names, geographic names, or music names by employing domain-specific LMs. In case of n-gram LMs, they can be easily built on-device to support personalized speech recognition . For on-device command recognition, shallow-fusion with a WFST is also useful for specific domains since WFST contains a list of words not just subword units .

## Improving NER performance using a spell corrector

Even though end-to-end all neural speech recognition systems have shown quite remarkable speech recognition accuracy with small memory footprint, it has been frequently observed that performance is poorer in recognizing named entities . Compared to conventional speech recognizers built with a WFST containing dictionary information, an end-to-end speech recognition system does not explicitly contain a list of named entities. Therefore, the speech recognition accuracy of such systems is generally low for specialized domains handling song names, composers’ names, personal names, and geographical names. Without dictionary information, spelling errors may also occur with all neural speech recognizers. This named entity recognition issue can be somewhat relieved by applying shallow-fusion with an LM as described in Sec. <a href="#sec:lm" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:lm">4.2</a>. However, more drastic performance improvement is usually obtained by classifying the domain from the speech recognition output and applying spell correction using a list of named entities found in that domain. In , a multi-stage spell correction approach was proposed to handle a large list of named entities with on-device speech recognizers.

# Compression

To run speech recognition models on embedded processors with limited memory, we often need to further reduce the parameter size in order to satisfy computational cost and memory footprint requirements. There are many known techniques to reduce the model size such as quantization, factorization, distillation, and pruning. The simplest way of accomplishing compression may be applying 8-bit quantization, which is supported by `TensorflowLite`$`^{\text{TM}}`$. Several commercially available speech recognition systems have been built using this technique. In feed-forward neural networks, 2-bit or 3-bit quantization has been successfully employed . However, for general purpose processors, there are relatively small gains in going below 8-bit quantization, since Arithmetic Logic Units (ALUs) usually do not support sub 8-bit arithmetic. Another popular technique in reducing the model size of LSTMs is applying Low Rank Approximation (LRA) . As an example, the *DeepTwist* algorithm, which is based on LRA, is employed to reduce the parameter size in our previous work . More specifically, we were able to reduce the size of MoChA-based models from 531 MB to less than 40 MB without sacrificing the performance using 8-bit quantization and LRA. Pruning may be a good choice if hardware supports sparse matrix arithmetic. In , authors propose a three-stage pipeline: pruning, trained quantization and Huffman coding, that work together to reduce the storage requirement of neural networks by 35x to 49x without affecting their accuracy. In , the authors studied effectiveness of different compression techniques for end-to-end speech recognition models. They conclude that pruning is the most effective with proper hardware support. In the absence of this, for small models, distillation appears to be the best choice, while factorization appears to be the best approach for larger models.

# Conclusions

In this paper, we reviewed various end-to-end all neural automatic speech recognition systems and their optimization techniques for on-device applications. On-device speech recognition has huge advantages compared to the server-side ones in terms of user privacy, operation without internet, server-cost, and latency. To operate speech recognition systems on embedded processors, we need to consider several factors such as recognition accuracy, computational cost, latency, and the model size. We compared pros and cons of different neural network components such as Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), and attention mechanism. We explained and compared different end-to-end neural speech recognition architectures such as a stack of LSTM layers with the Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) loss , Recurrent Neural Network-Transformer(RNN-T) , attention-based models, and models based on Monotonic Chunk-wise Attention (MoChA) . Further improvement is achieved by combining a streaming model with a low-latency non-streaming model, by applying shallow-fusion with a Language Model (LM), and by applying spell correction using a list of named entities . We also discussed several model compression techniques including quantization, singular value decomposition, pruning, and knowledge distillation. These recent advances in all neural end-to-end speech recognition made it possible to commercialize all neural on-device end-to-end speech recognition systems .
