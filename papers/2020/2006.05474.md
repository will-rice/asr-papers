# Improving Transfer Learning for Low-Resource ASR with Speech Translation

###### Abstract

Transfer learning from high-resource languages is known to be an efficient way to improve low-resource end-to-end ASR. Pretrained or jointly trained models, however, do not share all the components due to language-specific vocabularies. We introduce speech-to-text translation (ST) as an auxiliary task to smooth the transfer process and enable full model sharing. Specifically, we first machine-translate high-resource ASR transcripts into target low-resource language, with which a ST model is trained. Both ST and target ASR share the same attention-based encoder-decoder model architecture and vocabulary. The former task then provides a fully pretrained model for the latter, bringing more gains than direct transfer from high-resource ASR. We show that ST with human translations is not necessary. Pseudo-labels from machine translation (MT) perform comparably to human translations given decent amount of training data. The downstream ASR benefits from further improvements of upstream ST, which can be achieved by semi-supervised learning or more accurate pseudo-labels.

Index Terms: speech recognition, speech translation, low resource, transfer learning, machine translation

## 1 Introduction

Attention-based encoder-decoder architecture \[sutskever2014sequence, bahdanau2014neural\] has recently witnessed rapidly increased applications in end-to-end automatic speech recognition (ASR). It provides a generic framework for general speech-to-text generation tasks, and is achieving state-of-the-art performance on ASR \[chiu2017stateoftheart, park2019specaugment, synnaeve2019end\] as an alternative to conventional CTC (Connectionist temporal classification) model \[graves2006connectionist\].

The recent surge of end-to-end speech-to-text translation (ST) studies \[berard2016listen, duong2016attentional, weiss2017sequence, vila2018end, berard2018end\] also thanks to the application of attention-based encoder-decoder model. And very recent works have demonstrated the potential of combining the two tasks, ASR and ST, under the same encoder-decoder architecture \[anastasopoulos2018tied, di2019one, liu2019synchronous\] to improve at least one of them. When targeting at ST only, transfer learning from ASR models \[bansal2018pre, di2019one\] is very helpful to model training as well as model performance given learning end-to-end ST models is difficult.

In this paper, we instead target at ASR only and focus on low-resource directions. We explore how to use ST models to improve high-resource to low-resource transfer learning for ASR. Unlike previous ideas for leveraging translation data \[anastasopoulos2018leveraging, hayashi2018back, Wiesner2019\], our approach does not require any modification to the ASR model architecture. It utilizes ST data instead of text-to-text translation data, which avoids speech-to-text modality adaption in the encoder. Moreover, our ST model training only relies on pseudo-labels from machine translation (MT) of high-resource ASR transcripts, which is easy to obtain compared to real human translations. The use of MT pseudo-labels simplifies ST model training (knowledge distilled data) and they are easy to extend to larger high-resource ASR corpora.

We also show that the downstream low-resource ASR benefits from the improvements of upstream ST. The latter can be achieved by the use of more MT pseudo-labeled data as well as self-training data (semi-supervised learning). Under this framework, the problem of low-resource ASR is converted to the problem of high-resource ST.

## 2 Methods

### 2.1 Attention-Based Encoder-Decoder Model

For ASR and ST, we use the same BLSTM-based encoder-decoder architecture \[berard2018end\] with attention mechanism that is similar to the Listen, Attend and Spell model \[chan2016listen, chiu2017stateoftheart, park2019specaugment\]. Specifically, for the encoder side, audio features $`\text{x} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{T \times d_{0}}`$ are first fed into a two-layer DNN with $`t\hspace{0pt}a\hspace{0pt}n\hspace{0pt}h`$ activations and hidden sizes of $`d_{1}`$ and $`d_{2}`$. Then two 2D convolutional layers with kernel size $`3`$x$`3`$ and stride $`2`$x$`2`$ are applied to shrink the sequence length to $`\frac{T}{4}`$. Both convolutional layers have 16 output channels and project the features to $`4\hspace{0pt}d_{2}`$ dimensions after flattening. Finally, the features are passed to a stack of three bidirectional LSTM layers of hidden size $`d_{3}`$ to form encoder output states $`\text{h} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{{T \times 2}\hspace{0pt}d_{3}}`$. For the decoder side, there are two LSTM layers with vanilla additive attention \[bahdanau2014neural\], followed by an output linear projection layer.

For MT, we use vanilla Transformer base and big models \[vaswani2017attention\] with original hyper-parameter settings.

### 2.2 Speech Translation with Pseudo-Labels

Knowledge distillation (KD) has been proven to be helpful for MT \[kim2016sequence\] and ST \[liu2019end\] by reducing noise and simplifying data distribution in the training set. It is known that training end-to-end ST models is difficult, given the fact that models need to learn recognition and translation at the same time. When data distribution is complex, end-to-end ST models are likely to fit the data worse than cascading ASR and MT models. Training ST with MT pseudo-labels can be viewed as a sequence-level KD process (potentially out-of-domain depending on MT training data). Although the existence of inaccurate MT pseudo-labels may be harmful to model training, pseudo-labels are easier to fit by ST models, which compensates its gap with real human translations.

### 2.3 Pretraining ASR on Speech Translation

![Refer to caption](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2006.05474/assets/x1.png)

Figure 1: An overview of proposed transfer learning pipeline.

We use the same encoder-decoder model architecture for ASR and ST, so that they can be transferred between each other with only small incompatible output components replaced. Figure 1 provides an overview of our proposed transfer learning pipeline. In the monolingual setting, instead of finetuning high-resource ASR model directly to the target low-resource language, we add a high-resource to low-resource ST model in between to smooth the transfer process. The ST model is finetuned from high-resource ASR, so that it starts from a decent acoustic model and can be more focused on the translation (decoder). It has the same output language as low-resource ASR and transfer learning to the latter is hence more focused on the encoder part, which is likely to be easier. Moreover, the ST model leverages larger amount of data in the target language (MT pseudo-labels on high-resource ASR), which is additional to the low-resource ASR data it is finetuned on later. The multilingual setting (a universal model) has a similar pipeline.

## 3 Experiments

### 3.1 Data

For high-resource (English) ASR, We use Librispeech \[panayotov2015librispeech\], Common Voice \[ardila2019common\] (v4, 2019-12-10 release) and MuST-C \[di2019must\]. Librispeech is from public domain audio books and a de-facto standard corpus for English ASR evaluation. Common Voice is a fast-growing crowdsourced multilingual ASR corpus having multiple speakers on the same contents. MuST-C is a multilingual speech-to-text translation corpus built on TED talks. We only use its Dutch subset for experiments on Dutch.

For low-resource ASR, we use 7 languages from Common Voice v4: Dutch (Nl), Mongolian (Mn), Portuguese (Pt), Chinese (Zh-CN), Russian (Ru), Turkish (Tr) and Tamil (Ta). We also use 3 languages from IARPA Babel datasets (conversational telephone speech): Ka (Georgian), Ht (Haitian), Vietnamese (Vi).

For MT, we use a variety of datasets from OpenSubtitles, JW300, WikiMatrix, MultiUN and WMT19.

### 3.2 Experimental Setup

We convert raw MP3 audio files from Common Voice into mono-channel waveforms, and downsample them to 16,000 Hz. For transcripts, we normalize the punctuation, we tokenize the text with sacreMoses, we lowercase it and remove all punctuation markers except for apostrophes. We use character vocabularies on all the tasks, with 100% coverage of all the characters. Preliminary experimentation showed that character vocabularies provided more stable training than BPE. For MT, the vocabulary is created jointly on both transcripts and translations. We extract 80-channel log-mel filterbank features, computed with a 25ms window size and 10ms window shift using Kaldi. The features are normalized to 0 mean and 1.0 standard deviation. We remove samples having more than 3,000 frames or more than 256 characters for GPU memory efficiency (less than 25 samples are removed for all languages).

Our ASR and ST models follow the architecture in \[berard2018end\], but have 3 decoder layers. We pretrain their encoders on 120-hour English ASR data from Common Voice (2019-06-12 release). For MT, we use a Transformer *base* architecture \[vaswani2017attention\], but with 3 encoder layers, 3 decoder layers and 0.3 dropout. We use a batch size of 10,000 frames for ASR and ST, and a batch size of 4,000 tokens for MT. We train all models using Fairseq \[ott2019fairseq\] for up to 200,000 updates. We use SpecAugment \[park2019specaugment\] (LD policy without time warping) for both ASR and ST to alleviate overfitting.

We use a beam size of 5 for all models. We use the best checkpoint by validation loss for MT, and average the last 5 checkpoints for ASR and ST. For MT and ST, we report case-insensitive tokenized BLEU \[papineni2002bleu\] using sacreBLEU \[post-2018-call\]. For ASR, we report word error rate (WER) and character error rate (CER) using VizSeq \[Wang_2019\] where both the hypothesis and reference are tokenized, lowercased and with punctuation removed.

## 4 Related Work

End-to-end models, such as CTC and attention-based encoder-decoder models, work well for low-resource ASR \[rosenberg2017end\]. It is known that multilingual training or pretraining with related high-resource or low-resource languages improves low-resource end-to-end ASR signficantly \[dalmia2018sequence, yi2018adversarial, cho2018multilingual, zhou2018multilingual\]. Recently, meta learning methods have been introduced to improve the efficiency of multilingual pretraining \[hsu2019meta\]. Besides cross-lingual transfer learning, incorporating real or synthetic text translation data as additional inputs to the model is another approach to improve low-resource ASR \[hayashi2018back, anastasopoulos2018leveraging, Wiesner2019\].

## 5 Conclusions

We show that high-resource to lower-resource transfer learning for end-to-end ASR can be improved by adding a ST model as an intermediate step. It helps to smooth the transfer process and inject additional knowledge on the target language to improve the performance. Currently our approach is based on encoder-decoder models. Our future work includes extending proposed transfer learning pipeline to other models: Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC), RNN Transducer (RNN-T) or hybrid models.
