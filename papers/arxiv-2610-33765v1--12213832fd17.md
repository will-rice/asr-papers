# RoleHear: Pseudo-Labeling for Semi-Supervised Role-Attributed Streaming Speech Recognition

Zefang Liu    Chenyang Zhu    Sangwoo Cho    Xujun Peng    Rongqing
Huang    Shi-Xiong Zhang    Sambit Sahu

###### Abstract

Streaming automatic speech recognition transcribes at low latency but
produces an unattributed word stream, while real-time applications such
as customer-service analytics need each word tied to a speaker role.
Role attribution is usually a separate stage, either an offline or
streaming speaker-diarization model aligned to the words or a text-based
large language model applied to the transcript, and role labels for
training are scarce. We present RoleHear, a semi-supervised pipeline
that makes a single cache-aware streaming transducer emit roles inline
through lightweight in-vocabulary role tokens: a fast turn-boundary
marker followed by a per-role token a few words later. An offline
transducer teacher, fine-tuned into a role-attributed recognizer,
relabels unlabeled audio, and a multimodal large language model supplies
silver role labels where gold annotations are unavailable. The streaming
student is fine-tuned on the mixture in a single pass. Across
customer-service calls with gold roles and earnings calls with silver
roles, RoleHear attributes roles accurately while preserving
transcription quality and narrowing the gap to the offline teacher.

## 1 Introduction

Pretrained streaming automatic speech recognition (ASR) models, such as
the cache-aware FastConformer Recurrent Neural Network Transducer
(RNN-T) \[21, 19\], deliver low-latency transcription for real-time
applications. Their output, however, is an unattributed stream of words.
Real-time call-center applications such as live summarization \[2\] and
agent assistance need each word tied to a conversational role (for
example, customer versus agent) inline with the transcript, not as an
after-call step.

Role attribution has traditionally been a separate stage on top of the
transcript: an offline or streaming speaker-diarization model aligned to
the words, or a post-hoc text-based large language model (LLM). Such
approaches build on speaker diarization and speaker-attributed ASR
(SA-ASR) \[7, 11, 20, 22\], which target speaker identity under
overlapping speech with auxiliary machinery (a diarization model, a
speaker inventory, or serialized decoding) hard to reconcile with
cache-aware streaming. Emitting roles inline from a single streaming
recognizer avoids this multi-stage complexity but is largely unexplored.
Moreover, role annotations are costly to obtain at scale, whereas
in-domain unlabeled audio is abundant.

In this work, we present RoleHear, a semi-supervised pipeline for
role-attributed streaming ASR, with three contributions. First, we
introduce decoupled role emission on a cache-aware streaming transducer:
a fast turn-boundary marker followed by a per-role token emitted a few
words later, separating turn detection from role commitment so a single
streaming model emits roles inline without a separate diarizer. Second,
we obtain role-attributed targets by single-pass pseudo-labeling: an
offline transducer teacher, fine-tuned into a role-attributed
recognizer, relabels unlabeled audio, and the streaming student is
fine-tuned on the resulting mixture of labeled and pseudo-labeled data
without iteration. Third, we generate silver role labels for
transcript-only corpora with a multimodal large language model (MLLM)
that conditions on chunk audio and the surrounding transcript context,
validated against gold labels, making public data usable without human
role annotation. We evaluate RoleHear on customer-service calls with
gold roles and earnings calls with silver roles, measuring transcription
quality, role accuracy, and the role-latency trade-off.

## 2 Related Work

Attributing words to speakers, the “who said what” problem, has been
studied as SA-ASR and joint diarization. El Shafey et al. \[7\] add
speaker-role tokens to an RNN-T output vocabulary, the closest precedent
for RoleHear’s in-vocabulary role tokens. Serialized Output Training
(SOT) underlies a line of end-to-end SA-ASR for overlapped
speech \[11\], later extended to streaming with token-level speaker
embeddings \[12\]. A complementary direction couples ASR with dedicated
diarizers such as Sortformer and its streaming variant \[20, 18\], or
applies LLMs to speaker attribution \[4, 1\]. These methods target
speaker identity under overlap and rely on speaker inventories,
serialized decoding, or a separate diarizer. For supervision, RoleHear
draws on pseudo-labeling, a dominant paradigm for semi-supervised
ASR \[10, 3\] formalized by iterative pipelines \[27, 14\] and refined
with moving-average or momentum teachers \[17, 8\] and dynamic
caches \[23\]. Pseudo-labeling has also been applied to neural
diarization \[24\]. The closest streaming precedents pair a large
offline teacher with a small streaming student through noisy-student
training \[9\] or distill Whisper into a streaming transducer trained
from scratch \[26\], but neither targets cache-aware streaming \[19\]
nor speaker roles. ReHear \[16\] and StreamHear \[15\] apply audio-aware
LLM correction and single-pass domain-adapted pseudo-labeling only to
plain transcription. Departing from these paradigms, RoleHear attributes
a closed set of roles with in-vocabulary role tokens on a cache-aware
streaming transducer, adapted by single-pass pseudo-labeling and
requiring no speaker enrollment, clustering, diarizer, or iterative
refinement.

## 3 Methodology

RoleHear adapts a pretrained cache-aware streaming transducer into a
role-attributed ASR (RA-ASR) model through single-pass pseudo-labeling.
We denote the offline teacher as $`M_{T}`$, the streaming student as
$`M_{S}`$, and the MLLM used for silver labeling as $`M_{L}`$. The data
comprise a gold-role set $`D_{G}=\{(x,y,r)\}`$ of audio, transcript, and
roles, a transcript-only set $`D_{O}=\{(x,y)\}`$ whose roles are
missing, and an unlabeled pool $`D_{U}=\{x\}`$. As summarized in
Algorithm 1, the pipeline labels $`D_{O}`$ with $`M_{L}`$, fine-tunes
the teacher into an RA-ASR model, generates role pseudo-labels on
$`D_{U}`$, encodes the role tokens at a fixed word-delay, and fine-tunes
the streaming student on the mixture, running each step once.

1: Models: Offline teacher $`M_{T}`$, streaming student $`M_{S}`$, MLLM
$`M_{L}`$

2: Data: Gold-role $`D_{G}=\{(x,y,r)\}`$, transcript-only
$`D_{O}=\{(x,y)\}`$, unlabeled $`D_{U}=\{x\}`$

3: // Silver role labeling

4: $`D_{S}\leftarrow\{(x,y,M_{L}(x,y,c))\mid(x,y)\in D_{O}\}`$

5: // Teacher role encoding and fine-tuning

6: Encode role tokens in $`D_{G}\cup D_{S}`$, then fine-tune $`M_{T}`$

7: // Role pseudo-label generation

8: $`D^{\prime}_{U}\leftarrow\{(x,M_{T}(x))\mid x\in D_{U}\}`$

9: // Student role-token encoding at word-delay $`n`$

10: Encode $`\langle\text{role}\rangle`$ and delayed per-role tokens in
$`D_{G}\cup D_{S}\cup D^{\prime}_{U}`$

11: // Student fine-tuning

12: Fine-tune $`M_{S}`$ on the encoded mixture

13: return $`M_{S}`$

Algorithm 1 RoleHear: Pseudo-Labeling for Streaming RA-ASR

Decoupled role emission. We express streaming role attribution as two
in-vocabulary tokens \[7\] on the cache-aware transducer \[19, 21\]. At
each turn boundary the model emits a fast $`\langle\text{role}\rangle`$
marker, and $`n`$ words later a per-role value token
($`\langle\text{role:customer}\rangle`$,
$`\langle\text{role:agent}\rangle`$, and so on). The marker fires on the
acoustic turn change and requires no lexical lookahead, whereas the
value token is deferred so the role decision can draw on $`n`$ words of
in-turn context, and decoupling the two lets turn detection proceed
without waiting on role commitment. Targets are built deterministically,
inserting $`\langle\text{role}\rangle`$ at each boundary and the
per-role token $`n`$ words later, a fixed word-delay $`n`$ that leaves
the word stream unchanged. For example, at delay $`n=2`$ an agent turn
and the customer’s reply are encoded as

```math
\displaystyle\langle\text{role}\rangle\;\;\text{Thank you}\;\;\langle\text{role:agent}\rangle\;\;\text{for calling.}
```

```math
\displaystyle\langle\text{role}\rangle\;\;\text{I have}\;\;\langle\text{role:customer}\rangle\;\;\text{a question.}
```

with each $`\langle\text{role}\rangle`$ marker at a turn boundary and
its per-role token two words later, while $`n=0`$ puts the role token
right after the marker, and $`n<0`$ places it relative to the turn’s
end. A non-negative delay keeps the student’s targets causal, while the
full-context teacher may place the token turn-final ($`n=-1`$).

Silver role labeling. For corpora with gold transcripts but no role
labels, we recover roles without human annotation using the MLLM
$`M_{L}`$ (Figure 1). For each chunk, $`M_{L}`$ is prompted with the
chunk audio, the chunk transcript as a numbered token list, and the
transcripts of the neighboring chunks on both sides, and returns a list
of role segments, each a role from the closed set and the token index at
which it starts; slicing the transcript at these boundaries gives
contiguous role-labeled spans, so a chunk may carry more than one role.
Each role is decided from both the chunk audio (voice and tone) and the
words, including how each speaker refers to themselves and others, with
the neighboring transcripts keeping assignments consistent across chunk
boundaries. Because only the target chunk carries audio, the prompt
marks that chunk in the context and states that its neighbors are text
alone. Where the recording is stereo, each context line is tagged with
its channel and the roles are restricted to those of the target channel;
the prompt drops this rule on mono recordings. Conditioning on the chunk
audio, not the transcript alone, separates this from text-only LLM role
assignment \[4\]. The procedure needs only a transcript, so it labels
transcript-only corpora to form the silver-role set $`D_{S}`$ and,
applied to the gold-labeled set, is scored against the gold roles to
estimate its reliability.

System: You label speaker roles in a recording. You are given the audio
of one chunk, that chunk’s transcript as a numbered list of tokens
(\[index\] token), and the neighboring chunks’ transcripts for context.
Split the chunk into consecutive segments, each spoken by a single role,
and report where each segment starts. Rules: (1) The audio is the chunk
to label (marked \>\>\> in the context), not the neighboring chunks,
which are given as text only. (2) Decide each segment’s role from both
the audio (voice and tone) and what is said: the words themselves and
how each speaker refers to themselves and to others; use the neighboring
transcripts to stay consistent across the recording. (3) When the chunk
is marked with a channel, use only roles that belong to that channel.
(4) Report one entry per segment: its role and the token index where it
starts; the first segment starts at index 0, and a new segment begins
only where the role changes, otherwise keep the chunk as one segment.
(5) Do not change the transcript; report only indices and roles, and use
only the roles listed below (never invent one). Allowed roles: One role:
description line per role of the closed set. User: The neighbor context
in time order, one line per chunk as Channel \<c\> \[MM:SS - MM:SS\]:
\<text\>, with \>\>\> marking the chunk to label, then that chunk’s
channel and its numbered tokens \[0\] Thank \[1\] you \[2\] for \[3\]
calling. \[4\] I \[5\] have \[6\] a \[7\] question. The chunk audio is
attached to this turn. Output: role_segments, a list of entries, each
giving a role and the start token index, constrained to the role set by
a JSON schema. The chunk above returns \[(agent, 0), (customer, 4)\].

Figure 1: Role-labeling prompt for the MLLM $`M_{L}`$: a system
instruction carrying the closed role set, a user turn with neighbor
context and the target chunk’s numbered tokens and audio, and a
schema-constrained role-segment output.

Teacher fine-tuning and pseudo-labels. The offline teacher $`M_{T}`$, a
full-context transducer, extends the base vocabulary with the
$`\langle\text{role}\rangle`$ marker and the per-role tokens, adding
embeddings for the new tokens without re-initializing the pretrained
parameters. Fine-tuning it on the union of gold- and silver-role data
$`D_{G}\cup D_{S}`$ under the transducer loss yields an offline RA-ASR
model. Because it sees the whole utterance, it commits roles at turn
granularity without a latency constraint. It then transcribes the
unlabeled pool $`D_{U}`$ once with greedy decoding, producing
role-attributed pseudo-labels $`D^{\prime}_{U}`$ that extend supervision
to the abundant in-domain audio.

Student fine-tuning. The streaming student $`M_{S}`$ extends its
vocabulary the same way. After encoding role tokens at the target
word-delay $`n`$, it is fine-tuned on the mixture
$`D_{G}\cup D_{S}\cup D^{\prime}_{U}`$ under the standard RNN-T loss, in
a single pass without iteration. The gold and silver labels anchor the
role tokens on trusted transcripts, while the teacher’s pseudo-labels
supply the scale of in-domain audio needed to learn them, so one
fine-tuning run adapts transcription and role attribution together. At
inference the student decodes in cache-aware streaming mode and emits
the $`\langle\text{role}\rangle`$ marker and per-role tokens inline with
the word stream, adding no model beyond the recognizer.

## 4 Experiments

This section describes the datasets and their role labels, the training
and inference setup, the main role-attributed ASR comparison across the
teacher and streaming student, and two ablations on the role-emission
delay and the right context.

### 4.1 Datasets

We evaluate on two public earnings-call corpora with silver role labels
and one proprietary call-center corpus with gold role labels; Table 1
lists per-split hours. Earnings-21 \[5\] is 44 quarterly earnings calls
across nine financial sectors with entity-dense transcripts, and
Earnings-22 \[6\] is 125 English earnings calls from global companies
spanning seven world-English regions, of which four predominantly
non-English files are dropped at prepare time, leaving 121. Both are
transcript-only, so an MLLM silver labeler, conditioning on each chunk’s
audio and the neighboring-chunk transcripts, assigns their three native
roles: operator (who runs the call), presenter (the company speakers),
and questioner (the analysts). We reuse the chunk-level preparation of
StreamHear \[15\]: voice-activity detection (VAD) segmentation,
CTC-Segmentation forced alignment, and prior-regularized
dynamic-programming realignment. ServiceCall is a proprietary
human-annotated set of stereo customer-service calls (channel 2 the
primary agent, channel 1 the customer and any other parties) with gold
speaker-role labels and chunk-level timestamps. From the gold roles we
form a 3-role set, customer, agent, and system (the automated prompts),
and a 5-role set that splits the agent into a primary agent (the lead),
an internal agent (other staff), and an external agent (a third party).
Its unlabeled pool draws additional in-domain calls segmented by VAD.

### 4.2 Experimental Setup

Models. The offline teacher $`M_{T}`$ is Parakeet-TDT-0.6B-v3 \[21\], a
0.6B-parameter full-context FastConformer transducer with
token-and-duration decoding. The streaming student $`M_{S}`$ is
Nemotron-Speech-Streaming-EN-0.6B \[19\], a 0.6B cache-aware
FastConformer-RNN-T trained with multi-latency right-context (RC)
sampling; unless noted we decode at $`\text{RC}{=}1`$ (an $`80`$ ms
chunk plus one $`80`$ ms lookahead frame, for $`160`$ ms of algorithmic
latency). The silver labeler $`M_{L}`$ is Gemma-4-12B-IT \[25\], a
multimodal LLM prompted per chunk with the chunk audio and
neighboring-chunk text within $`5`$ minutes on each side. Both
recognizers extend their vocabularies with the
$`\langle\text{role}\rangle`$ marker and the per-role tokens of
Algorithm 1, grafting fresh rows for the new tokens while preserving the
pretrained parameters; the new tokens’ joint-output bias is initialized
to $`-100`$ so they stay silent until fine-tuning moves them.

Training. All fine-tuning runs share the same recipe: AdamW optimizer
with learning rate $`5\times 10^{-4}`$, betas $`[0.9,0.98]`$, weight
decay $`10^{-3}`$; cosine schedule with $`10\%`$ warmup and minimum
learning rate $`10^{-6}`$; bf16 precision; SpecAugment ($`2`$ frequency
masks $`\times`$ $`10`$ time masks). Effective batch size $`64`$ ($`4`$
A100-SXM-40GB GPUs, per-GPU batch $`4`$, gradient accumulation $`4`$)
for the streaming student; teacher fine-tuning uses per-GPU batch $`2`$
with gradient accumulation $`8`$ to fit the transducer joint on
Earnings-length chunks. Training runs $`20`$ epochs on ServiceCall and
Earnings-22, and $`50`$ on Earnings-21, whose smaller labeled split
yields fewer optimizer steps per epoch. Checkpoints are selected on
validation RWER (Role-attributed WER, introduced below), so role
attribution drives selection. Each configuration is repeated for $`5`$
seeds and we report mean and standard deviation. All training and
inference use the NVIDIA NeMo framework \[13\].

Inference and metrics. Inference uses greedy decoding, full context for
the teacher and under the cache-aware streaming attention mask for the
student. All metrics are computed after a text-normalization pipeline:
lowercasing; normalizing numbers, contractions, spelling, and compounds;
and removing bracketed annotations, diacritics, filler words, and
punctuation. Word error rate (WER) measures transcription quality.
Role-attributed WER (RWER) measures role attribution carried in the word
stream, following speaker-attributed WER \[11\] with roles named rather
than clustered: for each utterance $`u`$ we bucket the reference and
hypothesis words by role $`r\in\mathcal{R}`$ and score each role’s
reference words against its hypothesis words, giving

```math
\text{RWER}=\frac{\sum_{u}\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}}(S_{u,r}+I_{u,r}+D_{u,r})}{\sum_{u}\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}}N_{u,r}}, \tag{1}
```

where $`S_{u,r}`$, $`I_{u,r}`$, $`D_{u,r}`$ are the substitutions,
insertions, and deletions between role $`r`$’s reference and hypothesis
words and $`N_{u,r}`$ is its reference word count; a role present on
only one side contributes pure insertions or deletions, and, because
words are scored per role within each utterance, a cross-turn role swap
counts as errors in both roles rather than canceling. We also report
macro-averaged precision, recall, and F1 over the decoded per-word role
labels, with precision and recall computed per role and the per-role F1
averaged equally across roles so rare roles weigh as much as frequent
ones. For the streaming student we additionally report the algorithmic
latency (AL), $`(1{+}\text{RC})\times 80`$ ms.

### 4.4 Ablation Studies

We run two ablations on the streaming student, both on Earnings-21: its
role-emission delay and its right context, which set the role latency
and algorithmic latency, respectively.

#### 4.4.1 Role-Emission Delay

We sweep the role-token delay on Earnings-21 for both the offline
teacher and the streaming student, the student decoded at
$`\text{RC}{=}1`$ (Table 3). The offline teacher has no
corpus-consistent optimum: Earnings-21 mildly favors delay $`0`$, while
Earnings-22 favors delay $`-1`$ decisively ($`25.9\%`$ RWER at delay
$`-1`$ vs $`32.3\%`$ at delay $`0`$, matched WER, not shown). Full
context lets it label a turn from either end, and as only the
Earnings-22 gap is large, we place the teacher token at delay $`-1`$.
The streaming student, which must commit to a role before the turn ends,
behaves oppositely: emitting at the boundary (delay $`0`$) collapses
attribution ($`46.1\%`$ F1) because no turn-internal words have decoded
yet, while a small positive delay restores it, delays $`1`$ and $`3`$
tying for the best RWER (near $`31.8\%`$). Delay $`-1`$ is best on F1
but worst on RWER, and releases the role only after the turn ends, the
highest latency of all settings. They diverge because macro-F1 scores
only the per-word role prediction and tolerates word errors, whereas
RWER is a role-bucketed WER and demands the right words under the right
roles. WER stays flat throughout, so we adopt delay $`1`$ for the
student, the smallest value that avoids collapse and thus the lowest
role latency (RL) at the best RWER: $`{\approx}360`$ ms, the mean word
duration.

We then vary the right context at each delay (Table 4). Right context
sets the algorithmic latency $`\text{AL}=(1{+}\text{RC})\times 80`$ ms
for every word, whereas the role delay adds a role latency
$`\text{RL}\approx\text{delay}\times 360`$ ms for the role token alone.
A larger right context helps on every metric at both delays, and at
delay $`0`$ it rescues the boundary-emission collapse, lifting F1 from
$`42.5\%`$ to $`65.9\%`$ as RC grows from $`0`$ to $`13`$. But this gain
is paid by the whole stream, since $`\text{RC}{=}13`$ raises AL from
$`80`$ to $`1120`$ ms on every word. A role delay is the cheaper source
of role quality because it defers only the role token: at
$`\text{RC}{=}0`$, moving from delay $`0`$ to delay $`1`$ alone lifts F1
to $`63.4\%`$ at $`80`$ ms AL. The two latencies exchange rather than
stack, so we keep the student at delay $`1`$ with a small
$`\text{RC}{=}1`$ ($`160`$ ms AL).

## 5 Conclusion

We presented RoleHear, a semi-supervised pipeline that makes a single
cache-aware streaming transducer attribute conversational roles inline,
through a fast turn-boundary marker followed by a per-role token a few
words later. An offline teacher, fine-tuned into a role-attributed
recognizer with the same in-vocabulary tokens, relabels unlabeled audio
in one pass, and an MLLM supplies silver role labels where gold
annotations are missing, so the streaming student learns transcription
and role attribution together, with no separate diarizer or iterative
refinement. Across earnings calls with silver roles and customer-service
calls with gold roles, adding role tokens is nearly free on
transcription, and single-pass pseudo-labeling substantially narrows the
streaming student’s role-attribution gap to the offline teacher, while a
small role-emission delay keeps algorithmic latency low by deferring
only the role token, rather than widening the right context and delaying
every word.
