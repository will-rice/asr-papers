# Synthetic-to-Real Code-Switched ASR: A Spontaneous Nepali–English Podcast Benchmark

Firoj Paudel Affiliation: Wiseyak Inc.
Email: [](mailto:)[firoj.paudel@wiseyak.com](mailto:firoj.paudel@wiseyak.com)

###### Abstract

Nepali–English code-switched conversational speech presents severe
challenges for automatic speech recognition (ASR), chiefly through
_script confusion_ (inappropriate mixing of Devanagari and Latin
orthography) and elevated error rates at language switch points.
Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) can adapt multilingual foundation models,
but human code-switched audio is expensive to collect and annotate. We
curate a new benchmark of 13,044 spontaneous podcast utterances (15.45 h
total; 10,214 test, 2,830 dev), audited and verified to eliminate
machine translation leakages and acoustic loops. We investigate a
two-stage synthetic-to-real adaptation framework: Stage 1 applies SFT on
0.95 h of speech synthesized via a multi-speaker, dual-engine TTS
pipeline; Stage 2 explores Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
with transcription and script rewards. We find that synthetic SFT
provides the primary cross-domain transfer to spontaneous human speech,
reducing Mixed Error Rate (MER) from 23.40% to 21.52%
($`-1.88\,\text{pp}`$) and switch-point error rate (SPER) from 35.89% to
28.77% ($`-7.12\,\text{pp}`$). Subsequent GRPO yields small,
statistically significant adjustments on 173 test utterances (1.69%),
driving English script confusion ($`\text{SCR}_{\text{en}}`$) from 6.45%
to 6.36% ($`p\leq 0.001`$) and SPER to 28.70% ($`p=0.022`$ clustered).
Crucially, ablation reveals that in this SFT-converged encoder–decoder
setting, an auxiliary script reward term yields performance identical to
MER-only optimization because rollout candidate groups exhibit near-zero
script variance on synthetic data. On monolingual benchmarks, SFT
improves Nepali MER by 2.71 pp (stabilizing at 18.42% after GRPO, a
2.38 pp gain over baseline) while preserving English lexical WER (3.38%
vs. 3.33%). Verified transcripts, timestamps, episode metadata, and
reconstruction scripts are publicly released.

## 1 Introduction

Code-switching, the alternation between two or more languages within a
single conversational turn, is ubiquitous across South Asia. In spoken
Nepali, speakers frequently embed English lexical items, technical
vocabulary, and colloquial phrases within a Devanagari grammatical
matrix.

Despite the recent scaling of multilingual speech foundation models \[1,
2\], code-switched ASR systems consistently suffer from two structural
error modes: (1) script confusion, transcribing English loanwords into
Devanagari phonetics (e.g., rendering English “doctor” phonetically in
Devanagari) or erroneously transliterating native Nepali words into
Latin characters; and (2) switch-point brittleness, where acoustic and
prosodic shifts at language boundaries cause elevated insertion,
substitution, and deletion rates immediately surrounding switch points.

Traditional approaches attempt to resolve script confusion through
auxiliary token-level language identification or diarization
heads \[3\]. However, token-level classification objectives during
autoregressive generation often introduce excessive stiffness at
boundary tokens; in our experiments, auxiliary token-level gating
actually degrades transcription fidelity.

Meanwhile, sequence-level policy optimization such as Group Relative
Policy Optimization (GRPO) \[4\] has recently emerged as an effective
framework for speech alignment \[5\]. Kumar et al. \[6\] demonstrated
that GRPO on synthetic speech reduces insertion errors for decoder-only
speech LLMs on domain speech. Ye and Vickers \[7\] showed that GRPO with
a joint error-rate and script-fidelity reward reduces cross-script
contamination on synthetic audio and transfers zero-shot to a
human-recorded code-switching corpus across ten language pairs. However,
both prior efforts evaluated unconstrained decoder-only audio language
models (e.g., Qwen2-Audio). Whether sequence-level policy optimization
provides measurable orthographic gains in dedicated encoder–decoder
foundation architectures on unconstrained, spontaneous conversational
code-switched speech remains an open empirical question.

In this work, our contributions are threefold: (1) synthetic-to-real
transfer, demonstrating that a dedicated FastConformer encoder–decoder
model fine-tuned exclusively on 0.95 h of synthetic code-switched audio
effectively transfers zero-shot to unconstrained human conversational
speech; (2) empirical analysis of GRPO and script rewards, formulating
sequence-level RL objectives and showing that GRPO yields small,
statistically significant adjustments on 173 test utterances
($`-0.07\,\text{pp}`$ SPER, $`-0.09\,\text{pp}`$
$`\text{SCR}_{\text{en}}`$) while the script reward term contributes no
measurable gain over MER-only optimization on an SFT-converged model;
and (3) open benchmark and systematic evaluation, releasing 15.45 h
(13,044 utterances; 10,214 test, 2,830 dev) of verified Nepali–English
podcast transcripts, timestamps, and alignment code on Hugging Face,
validating all experimental claims with paired bootstrap confidence
intervals (1,000 resamples), episode-clustered analysis, and monolingual
retention checks.

## 2 Related Work

#### Code-switched speech recognition.

Early investigations into South Asian code-switching focused on hybrid
acoustic models and balanced monolingual pre-training \[8, 9\]. Modern
foundation architectures such as IndicTranscribeFlex \[2\] and
Canary \[1\] incorporate massive multilingual vocabularies, yet remain
susceptible to script flipping when alternating between non-Latin Indic
orthographies and English.

#### Language identification and token gating.

Prior work addressed script ambiguity by attaching auxiliary frame- or
token-level language identification heads \[3\]. While effective on
scripted speech, hard token-level constraints can restrict
autoregressive beam search when phonetic boundaries are ambiguous,
occasionally forcing premature orthographic commitments that degrade
conversational recognition.

#### Reinforcement learning in speech.

Policy-gradient and minimum word error rate (MWER) training have an
extensive history in ASR \[10\]. GRPO \[4, 5\] eliminates the need for a
separate critic network by estimating advantages from sampled candidate
groups. Furthermore, training speech recognition models on synthetic
acoustic data has proven effective for low-resource domains \[11, 12,
6\]. Ye and Vickers \[7\] demonstrated that script-aware rewards curb
script confusion in audio LLMs. We extend this inquiry to investigate
whether sequence-level rewards provide complementary boundary
regularization when adapting a pre-trained encoder–decoder model to
spontaneous conversational speech.

## 3 Dataset Construction

### 3.1 Synthetic Training Corpus

To eliminate the requirement for expensive human recordings during
training, we synthesized a controlled code-switched corpus. Sentences
containing natural intra-sentential Nepali–English code-switching were
generated by prompting Gemini 3.8 Flash with conversational templates
reflecting modern urban discourse. All sentences were filtered against
strict lexical criteria and a 5-gram non-leakage check against the real
benchmark references.

Audio was synthesized utilizing two complementary acoustic engines:
(1) OmniVoice \[13\], an open-source diffusion acoustic model fine-tuned
on 100+ hours of native Nepali speech rendered across two distinct male
timbres, capturing authentic Devanagari phonology and colloquial
cadence; and (2) Gemini TTS, multi-speaker neural synthesis
(gemini-3.8-flash-tts) providing diverse English accentuation across
four distinct vocal timbres. Pairing these engines provides rich
phonetic coverage across both languages and prevents the ASR encoder
from overfitting to vocoder artifacts. Each text template was
synthesized across multiple voices and engines. Audio was sampled at
16 kHz mono, augmented with telephony bandpass filtering (300–3400 Hz)
and 10–30 dB SNR noise, and screened through an independent round-trip
filter (whisper-large-v3-turbo in Devanagari-only scope, CER
$`\leq 0.20`$). The resulting verified synthetic training split
comprises 517 audio utterances across 211 unique sentences (0.95 h total
duration).

### 3.2 Real Spontaneous Benchmark

To rigorously assess generalization, we curated a spontaneous
conversational benchmark from long-form public Nepali podcasts across 28
diverse episodes. Unlike read speech, podcast audio contains spontaneous
turn-taking, ambient noise, conversational laughter, and natural speech
rate variations.

Transcripts adhere to a strict orthographic convention: native Nepali
matrix words are transcribed in Devanagari, English words and embedded
phrases are transcribed in Latin script as spoken (not transliterated),
and attached Nepali postpositions are separated into distinct Devanagari
tokens (e.g., Latin root “phone” followed by separated Devanagari
postposition “-ma”, rather than fused into a single compound).

#### Dataset verification protocol.

Initial candidate transcriptions were generated via an automated
acoustic pipeline using gemini-3.5-transcribe with word-level timestamps
and speaker diarization, followed by loanword orthographic normalization
via gemini-3.8-flash. To establish a clean reference standard, every
clip in the initial pool of 13,277 raw segmented clips was individually
audited against raw 16 kHz PCM audio by two bilingual Nepali–English
annotators. The verification pipeline: (i) quarantined 233 clips
exhibiting acoustic background loops or music, leaving 13,044 clean
clips; (ii) eliminated 161 machine translation leakages where spoken
Nepali was mistakenly translated into English; (iii) corrected 819
token-doubling glitches caused by diarization timestamp overlaps; and
(iv) normalized 86 digit instances into verbalized spoken words. On an
independent dual-audit sample of 500 clips, inter-annotator character
agreement reached 98.4%. The remaining 13,044 clean clips constitute the
verified benchmark.

#### Data statement and ethics.

The benchmark audio originates from public Nepali conversational
podcasts broadcast on YouTube under standard commentary and discussion
formats. Speakers are adult conversationalists discussing
socio-technical, educational, and entrepreneurial topics in urban
Kathmandu colloquial speech. The corpus is partitioned into: (i) Real
Dev Split (2,830 utterances, 3.55 h, 16 episodes, 20 speakers); and
(ii) Frozen Real Test Split (10,214 utterances, 11.90 h, 25 episodes, 52
speakers). To ensure true speaker disjointness across the 28 podcast
episodes, speaker identities were linked across episodes using acoustic
speaker embeddings and manual cross-verification of recurring show
hosts. Speakers were partitioned strictly between dev (20 speakers) and
test (52 speakers) with zero overlapping speakers. In the 13 episodes
shared across splits, conversational turns were partitioned by speaker
role: recurring podcast hosts were assigned exclusively to dev (20
speakers, averaging 2.65 switch points per utterance), while invited
guest conversationalists were assigned exclusively to test (52 speakers,
averaging 2.11 switch points per utterance). Consequently, while
acoustic speaker identities remain strictly disjoint, dev exhibits a
higher conversational code-switching density (2.65 vs. 2.11 switch
points/utt) reflecting host interviewing style. Test audio was frozen
with SHA-256 integrity verification prior to model evaluation. Verified
transcripts, segment timestamps, pseudonymous speaker labels, YouTube
video identifiers, and reproducible alignment scripts are published on
Hugging Face under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 at
[https://huggingface.co/datasets/SamaFiroz/nepali-english-codeswitch-podcasts](https://huggingface.co/datasets/SamaFiroz/nepali-english-codeswitch-podcasts).
Content creators or speakers who prefer not to have their audio indexed
may contact firoj.paudel@wiseyak.com for prompt clip removal.

## 4 Methodology

### 4.1 Base Architecture and Adaptation

We adopt indic-transcribe-flex \[2\], a 1.22-billion parameter
multilingual model built on the Canary-1B-v2 architecture comprising a
frozen FastConformer acoustic encoder (32 Conformer blocks with 8
attention heads, $`d_{\text{model}}=1024,d_{\text{ffn}}=4096`$) and an
autoregressive Transformer decoder (24 layers with 8 attention heads,
$`d_{\text{model}}=1024,d_{\text{ffn}}=4096`$) with a 7,152-token
SentencePiece aggregate vocabulary. Given an acoustic feature sequence
$`X`$, the encoder produces representations
$`H=\text{Encoder}(X)\in\mathbb{R}^{T^{\prime}\times D}`$. During
adaptation, all acoustic encoder parameters remain completely frozen,
preserving the pre-trained multilingual acoustic representations. We
fine-tune the entire autoregressive Transformer decoder across all 24
layers along with the output projection head (transf_decoder and
log_softmax), updating 410,455,024 trainable parameters (410.46M,
representing exactly 33.60% of the 1,221,439,472 base parameters). This
adaptation strategy isolates bilingual language modeling and
script-routing adaptation entirely within the generative decoder while
avoiding acoustic degradation.

#### Mixed Error Rate (MER).

Evaluates Word Error Rate (WER) on Latin-script segments and Character
Error Rate (CER) on Devanagari segments:
$`\text{MER}=(E_{\text{w,en}}+E_{\text{c,ne}})/(N_{\text{w,en}}+N_{\text{c,ne}})`$,
where $`E_{\text{w,en}}=S_{\text{en}}+D_{\text{en}}+I_{\text{en}}`$ is
the word edit distance over English segments, $`E_{\text{c,ne}}`$ is the
character edit distance over Devanagari segments, and $`N`$ denotes
reference token count. This hybrid formulation reflects the linguistic
reality that English is written with explicit word-boundary spacing
where whole-word recognition governs intelligibility, whereas Nepali is
a morphologically rich, agglutinative Indo-Aryan language where
inflectional postpositions and compound verb morphology make
character-level edits a more perceptually and grammatically faithful
measure of recognition fidelity.

#### Script Confusion Rate (SCR).

Computed over word-level Levenshtein alignments between hypothesis and
reference token sequences (Appendix C). Let $`\mathcal{S}_{\text{sub}}`$
denote the set of aligned word pairs $`(w_{\text{ref}},w_{\text{hyp}})`$
mapped under substitution operations where both reference and hypothesis
tokens possess unambiguous single-script identities, i.e.,
$`\text{Script}(w)\in\{\text{Devanagari},\text{Latin}\}`$. Then
$`\text{SCR}=\frac{1}{N_{\text{elig}}}\sum_{(w_{\text{ref}},w_{\text{hyp}})\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{sub}}}\mathbb{I}\bigl[\text{Script}(w_{\text{ref}})\neq\text{Script}(w_{\text{hyp}})\bigr]`$,
where eligible count $`N_{\text{elig}}`$ is the total number of
single-script reference words in $`\{\text{Devanagari},\text{Latin}\}`$.
$`\text{SCR}_{\text{en}}`$ restricts the numerator and denominator
strictly to Latin-script reference words, measuring English terms
erroneously transcribed into Devanagari characters. Crucially, tokens
exhibiting mixed scripts (such as hybrid roots with Latin morphemes or
unseparated compounds like model\devfontहरु or phone\devfontमा) evaluate
to Script.MIXED and are excluded from the confusable set; furthermore,
word deletions and insertions are penalized under MER but contribute
zero to the substitution-based SCR numerator and denominator.

#### Switch-Point Error Rate (SPER).

The MER computed strictly over reference tokens within a local context
window of $`w=\pm 1`$ tokens around language switch points.

### 4.3 Stage 1: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

In Stage 1, the model is adapted on synthetic pairs $`(x,y)`$ using
cross-entropy loss over target token sequences:
$`\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(\theta)=-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log P_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid y_{<t},x)`$,
optimized with AdamW ($`\text{lr}=1.0\times 10^{-5}`$, effective batch
size 8, 1,000 steps).

### 4.4 Baseline: Token-Level Script Gating ($`S_{2}`$)

As an architectural comparison, an auxiliary script classification head
is attached atop the decoder hidden states $`h_{t}`$:
$`P_{\text{gate}}(s_{t}\mid h_{t})=\text{sigmoid}(W_{g}h_{t}+b_{g})`$,
where $`s_{t}\in\{0,1\}`$ denotes Devanagari versus Latin script.
Training minimizes
$`\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}+\lambda_{g}\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}`$
with $`\lambda_{g}=0.1`$. At inference, logits for candidate tokens
matching the predicted script are biased by
$`\gamma\log P_{\text{gate}}`$ ($`\gamma=2.0`$, applied when
$`P_{\text{gate}}>0.9`$).

### 4.5 Stage 2: Sequence-Level Policy Optimization (GRPO)

While SFT aligns the decoder with code-switched acoustics, cross-entropy
penalizes errors at the individual token level without evaluating
candidate sequence alternatives. To incentivize sequence-level accuracy
and boundary fidelity, we implement Group Relative Policy Optimization
(GRPO) \[4, 5\] trained on the 517 synthetic utterances.

For each acoustic sequence $`x`$, the policy $`\pi_{\theta}`$ samples a
group of $`G=8`$ candidate transcriptions $`\{y^{(1)},\dots,y^{(G)}\}`$
at temperature $`\tau=1.0`$. A scalar reward $`R(y^{(g)},y^{*})`$ is
computed against ground truth $`y^{*}`$. Group advantages are
normalized:
$`\hat{A}^{(g)}=(R(y^{(g)},y^{*})-\mu_{G})/(\sigma_{G}+\delta)`$, where
$`\mu_{G}`$ and $`\sigma_{G}`$ are the mean and standard deviation of
rewards within the sampled group ($`\delta=10^{-8}`$). The GRPO
objective maximizes:

```math
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\biggl[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{g=1}^{G}\sum_{t=1}^{|y^{(g)}|} \displaystyle\min\Bigl(r_{t}^{(g)}(\theta)\hat{A}^{(g)}, \tag{1}
```

```math
\displaystyle\clip(r_{t}^{(g)}(\theta),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat{A}^{(g)}\Bigr)
```

```math
\displaystyle-\beta D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\parallel\pi_{\text{ref}})\biggr],
```

where
$`r_{t}^{(g)}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}^{(g)}\mid y_{<t}^{(g)},x)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y_{t}^{(g)}\mid y_{<t}^{(g)},x)}`$
denotes the per-token importance sampling ratio at step $`t`$. Here,
$`\pi_{\theta}`$ represents the active autoregressive decoder policy
parameterized by weights $`\theta`$, and $`\pi_{\text{old}}`$ is the
rollout snapshot. Initializing directly from Stage 1 SFT, we set
$`\beta=0.0`$, relying on group advantage normalization and PPO clipping
($`\epsilon=0.2`$) without reference policy penalty. Training runs for
500 total steps using AdamW with peak learning rate
$`1.0\times 10^{-6}`$ (cosine decay, 10-step warmup) over 4 prompt
groups per batch ($`G=8`$, 32 rollouts/update).

#### Reward formulation.

Our joint reward combines sequence accuracy with orthographic fidelity:
$`R_{\text{joint}}(y,y^{*})=\bigl(1-\text{MER}(y,y^{*})\bigr)+\lambda_{\text{scr}}\bigl(1-\text{SCR}(y,y^{*})\bigr)`$
with $`\lambda_{\text{scr}}=0.5`$ ($`\lambda_{\text{scr}}=0.0`$ for
MER-only ablation $`S_{3}`$).

## 5 Experiments and Results

### 5.1 Primary Benchmark Evaluation

Table 2 and Figure 1 present performance on the 10,214-utterance frozen
real test split. Synthetic SFT ($`S_{1}`$) yields an immediate absolute
drop of $`1.88\,\text{pp}`$ in MER and $`7.12\,\text{pp}`$ in
switch-point error rate (SPER) over the unadapted zero-shot baseline
($`S_{0}`$). Initializing from $`S_{1}`$, GRPO with joint reward
($`S_{4}`$) achieves modest fine-grained improvements across evaluated
dimensions, reaching $`21.49\%`$ MER, $`28.70\%`$ SPER, and $`6.36\%`$
English script confusion ($`\text{SCR}_{\text{en}}`$).

### 5.2 Statistical Significance

To assess whether improvements are statistically reliable, we executed
paired bootstrap hypothesis tests with 1,000 resamples ($`B=1{,}000`$,
seed 42) on the frozen test split. Comparing $`S_{4}`$ vs. $`S_{0}`$,
our adapted pipeline achieves an absolute MER reduction of
$`-1.90\,\text{pp}`$ (95% CI: $`[-2.37,-1.42]`$), a SPER reduction of
$`-7.19\,\text{pp}`$ (95% CI: $`[-7.97,-6.46]`$), and an English script
confusion reduction of $`-0.35\,\text{pp}`$ (95% CI: $`[-0.65,-0.04]`$),
with all intervals strictly excluding zero ($`p\leq 0.001`$ for MER and
SPER, $`p=0.026`$ for $`\text{SCR}_{\text{en}}`$).

Comparing $`S_{4}`$ vs. $`S_{1}`$, sequence-level policy optimization
yields modest but statistically significant gains in boundary fidelity
($`\Delta\text{SPER}=-0.07\,\text{pp}`$, 95% CI: $`[-0.12,-0.03]`$,
$`p<0.05`$) and English script confusion
($`\Delta\text{SCR}_{\text{en}}=-0.09\,\text{pp}`$, 95% CI:
$`[-0.13,-0.06]`$, $`p\leq 0.001`$), with a mean MER difference of
$`-0.03\,\text{pp}`$ (95% CI: $`[-0.05,-0.01]`$, $`p<0.05`$). Exactly
173 of 10,214 test utterances ($`1.69\%`$) differ between $`S_{1}`$ and
$`S_{4}`$; the remaining 10,041 are identical. An episode-clustered
block bootstrap ($`B=1{,}000`$ clusters across 25 episodes) confirms
robustness: $`\Delta\text{SPER}=-0.07\,\text{pp}`$ (95% CI:
$`[-0.15,-0.01]`$, $`p=0.022`$) and
$`\Delta\text{SCR}_{\text{en}}=-0.09\,\text{pp}`$ (95% CI:
$`[-0.11,-0.06]`$, $`p\leq 0.001`$). The 173 diffs span 20 episodes (at
most 25 per episode), ruling out single-speaker artifacts.

### 5.3 Ablation Study: Gating vs. Policy RL

Ablation on dev (2,830 utterances; Table 2) shows that adding an
auxiliary token-level script gate ($`S_{2}`$) degrades performance
relative to standard SFT ($`S_{1}`$), increasing MER from $`18.92\%`$ to
$`19.14\%`$ and $`\text{SCR}_{\text{en}}`$ from $`5.35\%`$ to
$`5.75\%`$. Greedy logit biasing forces premature script assignment at
loanword prefixes, penalizing valid Devanagari assimilations.

In contrast, GRPO directly optimizes candidate probability mass.
Comparing $`S_{3}`$ ($`\lambda_{\text{scr}}=0.0`$) against $`S_{4}`$
($`\lambda_{\text{scr}}=0.5`$), both attain identical performance
($`18.89\%`$ MER, $`25.53\%`$ SPER, $`5.24\%`$
$`\text{SCR}_{\text{en}}`$) and select the identical optimal checkpoint
at step 50 (the earliest evaluation checkpoint, after which synthetic
dev MER plateaus at 2.00% tied with SFT through step 150 and marginally
drifts to 2.20%–2.32% through step 500 as policy updates exhaust
synthetic signal). Because SFT already reaches $`0.0200`$ MER on
synthetic prompts, rollout groups exhibit near-zero script variance
($`>75\%`$ of mini-batches had zero within-group script variance). Thus,
while script rewards prevent unconstrained hallucination in generative
audio LLMs \[7\], in an SFT-converged encoder–decoder setting sequence
exploration is dominated by the primary transcription loss.

#### English (LibriSpeech test-clean \[14\], 1,000 utterances, 1.50 h).

Lexical accuracy is statistically preserved: WER shifts from $`3.33\%`$
($`S_{0}`$) to $`3.42\%`$ ($`S_{1}`$) and recovers to $`3.38\%`$ under
$`S_{4}`$ (95% CI: $`[-0.17,+0.30]`$, $`p>0.05`$). MER increases from
$`4.21\%`$ to $`5.27\%`$ and SCR from $`0.59\%`$ to $`1.03\%`$,
reflecting occasional Devanagari insertions on pure English speech
induced by code-switched prior adaptation. S1 and S4 exhibit identical
profiles, confirming trade-offs stem from SFT rather than GRPO.

### 5.5 Qualitative Analysis and Error Inspection

On the 173 utterances where $`S_{1}`$ and $`S_{4}`$ diverge, GRPO
selectively rectifies switch-point script assignments with minimal
alterations elsewhere. For example, in utterance -IE5q6*CMnU_0189,
$`S*{1}`$ compounds the English brand name into the Devanagari
postposition (\devfontटाटाको model), whereas $`S*{4}`$ cleanly separates
the Latin brand from the Nepali postposition (tata \devfontको model). In
utterance -IE5q6_CMnU_0244, $`S*{1}`$ produces a phonetic Devanagari
substitution (\devfontदे were running); $`S\_{4}`$ restores Latin script
orthography (they were running). Detailed qualitative diffs across
operational regimes are analyzed in Appendix D and Table 7.

## 6 Limitations

While synthetic SFT with GRPO yields statistically reliable gains,
several limitations should be noted:

1.  1.  Modest RL gains: While prior work reported large relative gains
        ($`>40\%`$) with decoder-only speech LLMs \[6\], post-SFT GRPO gains
        on spontaneous speech are modest ($`-0.07\,\text{pp}`$ SPER,
        $`-0.09\,\text{pp}`$ $`\text{SCR}_{\text{en}}`$). In encoder–decoder
        architectures, we hypothesize that the frozen Conformer encoder
        already provides rigid acoustic grounding, and rollout candidate
        groups exhibit near-zero script variance on synthetic prompts.
        Furthermore, without an extended SFT compute control, the marginal
        gain cannot be separated with certainty from extra gradient steps.
2.  2.  Synthetic training volume: Training relies on 517 synthetic
        utterances (0.95 h) across two male timbres, with the acoustic
        encoder frozen throughout adaptation.
3.  3.  Reference annotation bias: Draft transcripts were seeded using
        automated foundation models (gemini-3.5-transcribe with
        gemini-3.8-flash normalization); despite dual-annotator verification
        (98.4% character agreement on a 500-clip audit), corrected
        references may retain slight residual bias.
4.  4.  Monolingual orthographic side-effects: MER and SCR slightly increase
        on monolingual English despite preserved WER.
5.  5.  Conversational role asymmetry across splits: Partitioning the 13
        shared episodes by speaker role assigned recurring hosts to dev (20
        speakers, 2.65 switch points/utt) and invited guests to test (52
        speakers, 2.11 switch points/utt). While speaker disjointness is
        strictly maintained, the higher switch-point density in dev reflects
        host interviewing style.
6.  6.  External baselines and multi-seed variance: While significance is
        verified with clustered block bootstrapping, evaluating multi-seed
        training variance and benchmarking external foundation models (e.g.,
        Whisper) remain valuable future work.

## 7 Conclusion

We presented an empirical benchmark and analysis of synthetic-to-real
adaptation for Nepali–English code-switched ASR. Supervised fine-tuning
on purely synthetic speech provides the primary cross-domain transfer to
spontaneous conversational speech ($`-1.88\,\text{pp}`$ MER,
$`-7.12\,\text{pp}`$ SPER). Sequence-level GRPO yields small,
statistically significant adjustments on 173 test utterances
($`-0.07\,\text{pp}`$ SPER, $`p=0.022`$; $`-0.09\,\text{pp}`$
$`\text{SCR}_{\text{en}}`$, $`p\leq 0.001`$) without degrading English
lexical accuracy (WER preserved at 3.38% vs. 3.33%), while improving
native Nepali MER by 2.38 pp. Crucially, ablation reveals that
script-aware rewards provide no measurable advantage over MER-only
optimization in this SFT-converged encoder–decoder setting.

Transcripts, timestamps, and reconstruction scripts for the benchmark

#### Speaker partitioning and episode distribution.

The 28 podcast episodes involve 72 distinct identified speakers (54
male, 18 female). To guarantee that speaker characteristics do not leak
across evaluation boundaries, speaker identities were linked using
x-vector acoustic embeddings cross-checked against visual and verbal
identity markers. All 72 speakers are strictly partitioned between dev
(20 speakers) and test (52 speakers), yielding zero speaker overlap.

episodes were partitioned between dev and test by assigning host turns
to dev and guest turns to test, ensuring complete acoustic disjointness
while preserving topical diversity.

#### Auditing and verification protocol.

Initial candidate transcriptions were generated using
gemini-3.5-transcribe with word-level timestamps and speaker
diarization, followed by English loanword orthographic normalization via
gemini-3.8-flash. Every utterance in the candidate pool was then
manually audited against 16 kHz PCM audio by two bilingual annotators:

1.  1.  Acoustic loop and music quarantine: 233 clips containing musical
        introductions, synthesizer jingles, or looped background tracks were
        isolated and removed from the benchmark.
2.  2.  Translation leakage removal: 161 instances where an automated system
        had translated spoken Nepali into English rather than transcribing
        verbatim Devanagari were corrected.
3.  3.  Timestamp overlap correction: 819 boundary errors resulting from
        diarization timestamp collisions were realigned.
4.  4.  Spoken digit normalization: 86 numeric digits (e.g., “2024”) were

## Appendix C Algorithmic Specification for Script Confusion Rate

Table 6 details the deterministic formulation used to evaluate
orthographic fidelity. Levenshtein alignment between hypothesis and
reference word sequences with deterministic tie-breaking isolates
substitutions between Devanagari and Latin alphabets. Two explicit
boundary rules apply: (1) *Mixed-script tokens* (Script.MIXED) and
non-alphabet characters are excluded from substitution confusion counts
($`C_{\text{ne}},C_{\text{en}}`$) and single-script denominators
($`N_{\text{ne}},N_{\text{en}}`$); (2) *Deletions and insertions* are
penalized under MER but contribute zero confusion to SCR because no
substitution alignment occurred.

| Algorithm: Deterministic Script Confusion Rate (SCR) Calculation                                                                                                                                  |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Input: Reference sequence $`Y^{*}=(w_{1}^{*},\dots,w_{M}^{*})`$, Hypothesis sequence $`Y=(w_{1},\dots,w_{N})`$                                                                                    |
| Output: Overall Script Confusion Rate $`\text{SCR}_{\text{all}}`$, English Script Confusion Rate $`\text{SCR}_{\text{en}}`$                                                                       |
| 1: Tokenize sequences into whitespace-delimited word tokens: $`W_{\text{ref}}\leftarrow\text{words}(Y^{*}),\;W_{\text{hyp}}\leftarrow\text{words}(Y)`$                                            |
| 2: Assign script labels: $`S_{\text{ref}}\leftarrow[\text{Script}(w)\text{ for }w\in W_{\text{ref}}]`$, where $`\text{Script}(w)\in\{\text{Devanagari},\text{Latin},\text{Mixed},\text{Other}\}`$ |
| 3: Compute Levenshtein distance alignment matrix $`\mathcal{A}\leftarrow\text{Align}(W_{\text{ref}},W_{\text{hyp}})`$ using unit edit cost ($`\text{cost}=1`$)                                    |
| Deterministic backtrace tie-breaking: $`(\text{diagonal match/substitution})\succ\text{deletion}\succ\text{insertion}`$                                                                           |
| 4: Initialize confusion accumulators: $`C_{\text{ne}}\leftarrow 0,\;C_{\text{en}}\leftarrow 0`$                                                                                                   |
| 5: for each aligned word pair $`(i,j)\in\mathcal{A}`$ associated with edit operation SUBSTITUTION:                                                                                                |
| $`s_{\text{ref}}\leftarrow S_{\text{ref}}[i],\quad s_{\text{hyp}}\leftarrow\text{Script}(W_{\text{hyp}}[j])`$                                                                                     |
| if $`s_{\text{ref}}\in\{\text{Devanagari},\text{Latin}\}`$ and $`s_{\text{hyp}}\in\{\text{Devanagari},\text{Latin}\}`$ and $`s_{\text{ref}}\neq s_{\text{hyp}}`$:                                 |
| if $`s_{\text{ref}}==\text{Latin}`$: $`C_{\text{en}}\leftarrow C_{\text{en}}+1`$   (Latin reference substituted by Devanagari)                                                                    |
| if $`s_{\text{ref}}==\text{Devanagari}`$: $`C_{\text{ne}}\leftarrow C_{\text{ne}}+1`$   (Devanagari reference substituted by Latin)                                                               |
| (Insertions, deletions, and pairs involving Mixed or Other are excluded from $`C_{\text{ne}},C_{\text{en}}`$)                                                                                     |
| 6: Calculate eligible single-script counts: $`N_{\text{ne}}\leftarrow\text{count}(S_{\text{ref}},\text{Devanagari}),\quad N_{\text{en}}\leftarrow\text{count}(S_{\text{ref}},\text{Latin})`$      |
| 7: return $`\text{SCR}_{\text{all}}=\frac{C_{\text{ne}}+C_{\text{en}}}{N_{\text{ne}}+N_{\text{en}}},\quad\text{SCR}_{\text{en}}=\frac{C_{\text{en}}}{N_{\text{en}}}`$                             |

        English loanwords, acronyms, and pronouns, transliterating them into
        phonetically equivalent Devanagari characters (e.g., dollar $`\to`$
        \devfontडलर, youtube $`\to`$ \devfontयुट्युब, kfc $`\to`$
        \devfontकेएफसी, and they $`\to`$ \devfontदे). Sequence-level RL
        ($`S_{4}`$) successfully recovers authentic Latin orthography in
        these isolated diff instances, driving the modest
        $`0.09\,\text{pp}`$ global $`\text{SCR}_{\text{en}}`$ reduction.
2.  2.  Switch-point boundary disambiguation: In utterance -IE5q6*CMnU_0189
        (referencing the Tata business model), SFT compounds the Latin brand
        name directly into the Devanagari postposition (\devfontटाटाको),
        obliterating the orthographic switch boundary. Policy optimization
        ($`S*{4}`$) successfully disambiguates the Latin entity from the
Nepali postposition (tata \devfontको). Notice that $`S\_{4}`$ also
        introduces a subtle substitution at the sentence end (\devfontको
        instead of reference \devfontकै).
3.  3.  SFT regressions on base model successes: Synthetic adaptation does
        not uniformly improve all acoustic patterns. In -IE5q6*CMnU_0107,
        base model $`S*{0}`$ transcribed the technical acronym opex
        operating expenditure with complete fidelity. In contrast, synthetic
        SFT corrupted the acronym into open expenditure, and GRPO only
        partially recovered the sequence (opens operating expenditure). This
        demonstrates that synthetic fine-tuning on a small prompt
        distribution can occasionally overwrite rare vocabulary
        representations preserved in the pre-trained base model.
4.  4.  Monolingual orthographic stability: In pure Nepali segments such as
        -IE5q6_CMnU_0082, all models decode entirely in Devanagari script
        with zero spurious Latin intrusions, illustrating script stability
        under bilingual adaptation.

## Appendix E Datasheet, Ethics, and Reproducibility

Following recommended documentation standards for speech corpora, this
section provides the comprehensive datasheet for the curated
Nepali–English podcast benchmark.

#### Motivation and intended use.

The corpus was curated to establish an audited, reproducible benchmark
for spontaneous South Asian code-switched speech recognition, addressing
the absence of open-access conversational datasets for low-resource
language pairs with disparate orthographic systems. The primary intended
use is evaluating ASR models on spontaneous, unscripted bilingual speech
containing high-frequency intra-sentential language alternation.

#### Composition and coverage.

The verified benchmark contains 13,044 audio clips totaling 15.45 hours
across 28 podcast episodes. All speakers are fluent bilingual Nepali
speakers residing in urban Nepal. Conversations cover modern
socio-technical discourse containing 183,013 spoken words (67.3% native
Nepali in Devanagari script, 32.7% English in Latin script) and 29,033
inter-sentential and intra-sentential language switch points (averaging
2.23 switch points per utterance). Table 8 summarizes the distribution

#### Audio preprocessing and quality control.

All raw audio tracks were standardized to single-channel (mono) 16 kHz
16-bit linear PCM format and peak-normalized to $`-1`$ dBFS. Voice
activity boundaries were computed by trimming silence intervals
exceeding 0.8 s, producing utterance units between 1.0 s and 15.0 s.
Studio setups include dynamic and condenser broadcast microphones,
capturing natural acoustic nuances such as conversational overlaps,
laughter, and room acoustics without synthetic degradation.

#### Collection and licensing.

Audio was acquired from publicly broadcast YouTube video podcasts under
commentary and educational categories. The benchmark release contains
segment timestamps, pseudonymous speaker labels, verified
transcriptions, YouTube video identifiers, per-clip SHA-256 audio
checksums, and an automated reconstruction pipeline. Audio tracks are
fetched using yt-dlp pinned to version 2026.08.19 (tested October 2026)
requesting audio format 140 (128 kbps AAC audio), decoded and resampled
to 16 kHz mono linear PCM via ffmpeg 6.1.1 (swresample). At release,
100% of the 13,044 benchmark clips (15.45 h across 28 public YouTube
broadcasts) are fully reconstructable. Because YouTube streaming CDNs
may occasionally re-encode source streams or serve format variations,
and different decoder builds can introduce minor PCM rounding
differences, the reconstruction verification tool provides both strict
bit-exact SHA-256 hashing and an acoustic tolerance fallback (verifying
duration within $`\pm 10`$ ms and waveform Pearson correlation
$`\rho>0.999`$ against reference clips). Users are responsible for
complying with YouTube’s Terms of Service and all applicable copyright
and privacy laws. The metadata and transcriptions are released under
Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial-ShareAlike 4.0 International
(CC BY-NC-SA 4.0).
