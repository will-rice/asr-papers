# Nepali–English Code-Mixed Speech Recognition: Data Preparation and Adaptation of a Speech Foundation Model

Jeevan Neupane    Janardan Bhetwal    Kshitiz Paudel    Anup Aryal   
Bipin Bashyal Affiliation: [AI Lab Pvt. Ltd.](https://ailab.com.np/),
Nepal Affiliation: {jeevanneupane, janardanbhetwal, kshitizpaudel,
anuparyal, bipinbashyal}@ailab.com.np

Abstract

Nepali speakers regularly mix English words into conversation, but
multilingual speech recognisers often struggle with language switches
and the writing script used for English words. We study how to adapt
SraVaani, a 449M-parameter hybrid TDT–CTC model, using a 172.8-hour
corpus covering six Nepali speech domains. Its pretrained tokenizer does
not support digits and several common symbols. Rather than replace it or
discard these recordings, we generate Nepali and English readings for
numbers and use zero-shot ASR output to select a plausible form. Full
fine-tuning reduces test word error rate (WER) from 48.6% to 17.1%; a
12-epoch run reaches 16.3%, compared with 16.4% for fine-tuned Whisper
large-v3-turbo. Freezing the encoder increases WER to 25.5%, while
excluding numeric clips raises WER on numeric test speech from 14.3% to
21.3%. We also evaluate on FLEURS using both its original references and
mixed-script alternatives. The comparison shows that transcription
conventions explain part, but not all, of the out-of-domain performance
loss.

Index Terms: automatic speech recognition, Nepali, code-mixing,
multilingual speech, text normalisation, fine-tuning

## 1.  Introduction

Nepali speech frequently includes English words, particularly in
discussions of education, work and technology. Speakers switch between
languages within a sentence, but the written transcript is expected to
retain Nepali in Devanagari and English in Latin script. A model can
recognise the spoken word yet still produce the wrong script. Under
standard word error rate (WER), that difference is treated as a
recognition error.

Large multilingual recognisers such as Whisper \[1\] and Indic models
trained on broad speech collections \[2, 3, 4\] provide a useful
starting point. They do not, however, make the data preparation problem
disappear. SraVaani was trained mainly on single-script utterances. On
our evaluation set, its zero-shot output places many English words in
Devanagari and sometimes selects another Indic script for short Nepali
speech. The training labels also contain digits and punctuation that its
fixed tokenizer cannot represent.

Numbers are especially difficult. The written form “1947” may correspond
to a Nepali or English reading, while technical recordings contain
equations and programming expressions. Removing these clips loses
relevant speech. Replacing every digit with one language’s number words
can produce labels that do not match the audio. We therefore construct
alternative readings and select among them using the base model’s output
as evidence, followed by quality checks.

Our study addresses three practical questions: how to prepare
mixed-script transcripts without rebuilding the tokenizer, whether full
encoder adaptation and numeric clips improve recognition, and how
writing conventions affect reported error rates. We prepare 172.8 hours
across six domains, compare full and restricted fine-tuning, evaluate
against Whisper and IndicConformer, and test the same out-of-domain
FLEURS speech under two reference conventions. These comparisons are
intended to identify which changes matter, not to establish a new
general-purpose state of the art.

## 2.  Related Work

Multilingual and Indic ASR. Whisper \[1\] and MMS \[5\] cover Nepali,
while IndicVoices \[2\], Vistaar \[6\] and VAANI \[4\] have expanded
Indic speech training and evaluation. SraVaani \[3\] combines a
FastConformer encoder \[7\] with Token-and-Duration Transducer (TDT) and
CTC outputs \[8\]. We retain its pretrained vocabulary and both output
heads.

Code-mixed recognition and scoring. The MUCS challenge \[9\] established
Hindi–English and Bengali–English ASR benchmarks, and earlier work
discusses code-switching and monolingual forgetting \[10, 11\].
Transliteration-aware measures \[12, 13\], language-specific metrics
such as PIER \[14\] and switch-point evaluation \[15\] show why a single
WER value can hide different errors. For Nepali, Devkota \[16\] studied
code-mixed transcription and script conversion, while Paudel \[17\]
examined spontaneous Nepali–English podcast speech. Other Nepali
comparisons include Whisper adaptation \[18\] and multi-model
evaluation \[19\]. We differ in our emphasis on fixed-tokenizer
preparation, bilingual numbers and encoder adaptation.

Normalisation and evaluation corpora. Audio-guided text normalisation
has been explored with NeMo \[20\]. We use a related selection principle
but generate candidates in both languages and compare approximate
pronunciation rather than raw script. OpenSLR Nepali \[21\] supplies
read speech, and FLEURS \[22\] provides a public out-of-domain
reference. Our mixed-script FLEURS version tests the influence of
transcription style without changing its audio.

## 3.  Dataset Collection and Preparation

We combine public Nepali recordings with speech collected from online
material. The preparation pipeline covers audio cleaning, transcript
checks, train/test separation and compatibility with SraVaani’s fixed
tokenizer. Figures 1 and 2 outline the main steps.

### 3.1  Speech sources

The corpus draws on six speech settings (Table 3). OpenSLR supplies
mostly read Nepali sentences. The remaining sources include engineering
lectures, podcasts, news and talk programmes, conversations and
technical interviews. They differ in speaking style, background noise
and the amount of English. Lectures, for instance, contain programming
terms and mathematical expressions that are uncommon in ordinary read
speech.

### 3.2  Collection of in-the-wild speech

For the YouTube material, annotators first searched for suitable Nepali
videos using a small tool built on the YouTube Data API. Audio was
downloaded with yt-dlp. We separated speech from background sounds, used
a 300 Hz–8 kHz band-pass filter and applied denoising. Voice activity
detection then split recordings at pauses of at least 500 ms, targeting
chunks of 3–20 seconds.

Gemini produced the first transcripts. A second LLM pass corrected them,
and human annotators checked the text against the recordings. The
transcription convention was Nepali in Devanagari and English words in
Latin script. The 3–20 second target describes this collection step;
clips from the other sources could be longer.

### 3.3  Audio and transcript quality control

We converted audio to 16 kHz, mono, 16-bit PCM. MMS forced
alignment \[5\] helped trim speech at clip boundaries. We removed clips
with poor transcript coverage and checked for duplicate audio across
subsets. We also removed train-set sentences that were duplicated in
held-out splits, where these could be identified.

A second automatic transcript helped flag possible mistakes for review.
We excluded 604 training clips during the earlier quality filtering
step: 377 with large disagreement between two transcripts, 92 with
repetition artefacts, 98 duplicate held-out sentences and 41 with
implausible speech rates (four clips met two criteria). This left
116,175 source clips or 175.2 hours before tokenizer-specific cleaning.
The existing train, validation and test assignments were kept.

### 3.4  Tokenizer compatibility

SraVaani has a 5,000-piece SentencePiece BPE vocabulary \[23\]. It can
represent Nepali and English letters, but not common digits or symbols
such as the percent sign, punctuation marks and apostrophes. Unsupported
characters are mapped to the unknown token. Table 1 shows that 29.7% of
source clips contain at least one such character; 14.8% contain digits
and 14.9% contain punctuation or symbols without digits.

We retain the original vocabulary to reuse the pretrained prediction
network and output heads. Instead, we changed the transcripts to valid
strings. This preserves those model weights, although English words may
be divided into several short token pieces.

### 3.5  Transcription convention

The training targets follow four rules: Nepali words use Devanagari;
English words use lowercase Latin script; punctuation is removed; and
numbers and unsupported symbols are written as spoken words. Hyphens
become spaces, apostrophes are removed, and we apply Unicode NFKC
normalisation followed by single spacing. We check every final label by
encoding and decoding it with the original tokenizer. A label is
accepted only when no unknown token appears and the round-trip text is
unchanged.

### 3.6  Bilingual number verbalisation

Digits alone do not show which language the speaker used. The string
“1947” may be read in Nepali or English, and engineering recordings
contain forms such as “6 into 10”, “FXK+1” and program identifiers with
dots. We generate possible Nepali and English readings instead of
applying a single rule to each number.

The rule-based verbaliser covers more than 40 patterns, including
integers, years, decimals, ranges, percentages, ordinals, times,
fractions, superscripts, subscripts, alphanumeric forms and code
expressions. It builds candidate utterances from Nepali and English
number words, keeping at most 32 complete-sentence candidates per clip.

Next, we run the base SraVaani model on the audio. Its output often
writes English sounds in Devanagari. To compare outputs fairly, we map
the text to a rough romanised pronunciation key, merging common spelling
differences. We score the candidate strings by Levenshtein distance to
this key. After choosing a sentence-level candidate, we also try
alternative readings for individual numbers. Figure 2 shows one example,
and Table 2 gives more cases.

We reject clips when the two best readings tie (644 clips) or when the
closest candidate is still too far from the model output (315 clips,
using a normalised distance threshold of 0.5). Among 35,157 resolved
numbers, 45.1% use Nepali readings and 53.1% use English readings. The
distribution varies by source: 99.0% of numbers in OpenSLR are read in
Nepali, while 75.6% of numbers in engineering lectures are read in
English.

We manually audited 150 numeric clips sampled across the sources. Five
contained errors (3.3%; 95% confidence interval 1.4–7.6%). Several
errors came from missing reading patterns, such as an extra
\nepalifontएक before hundreds or an operator that was not spoken. Adding
these possibilities changed the chosen readings in 539 of 634 affected
clips according to the same automatic scoring method. A separate sample
of 100 clips had no identified errors in nonnumeric normalisation. The
corrected numeric labels still need independent listening-based
verification.

### 3.7  Length checks and data packaging

SraVaani’s encoder emits one frame every 80 ms. With
$`T=\lfloor d/0.08\rfloor`$ frames for a clip of $`d`$ seconds, a CTC
label of $`U`$ tokens and $`R`$ adjacent repeated tokens must satisfy

```math
U\leq T-2,\qquad U+R\leq T. \tag{1}
```

This check removes 109 clips. We also cap training clips at 20 seconds,
which causes the data loader to skip another 33 clips during training;
validation and test retain their longer clips.

We store the audio and manifests in NeMo’s tarred format \[24\], using
32 training shards and two shards each for validation and test. The
audio bytes and the manifest entries have matching orders. We check file
duration, unique clip IDs, absence of duplicate audio hashes across
splits, tokenizer round trips and full manifest coverage by the loader.

## 4.  SraVaani Adaptation

### 4.1  Model architecture

SraVaani \[3\] is a 449-million-parameter FastConformer model. Its
released checkpoint was pretrained on roughly 31,000 hours of VAANI
speech and then trained on a similar amount of transcribed speech. We
use the released weights, vocabulary and both output heads. Table 5
lists the components, and Figure 3 shows the training flow.

| Component          | Structure                                    | Params |
| ------------------ | -------------------------------------------- | ------ |
| Front end          | log-mel, 128 bins (fixed)                    | –      |
| Subsampling        | 3 strided conv. stages, 256 ch. + linear     | 4.3M   |
| Conformer blocks   | $`17\times`$ ($`d=1024`$, 8 heads, kernel 9) | 428.6M |
| Prediction network | embedding + LSTM, 640 units                  | 6.5M   |
| Joint network      | $`1024+640\rightarrow 640\rightarrow 5006`$  | 4.3M   |
| CTC head           | $`1024\rightarrow 5001`$                     | 5.1M   |
| Total              |                                              | 448.8M |

Table 5: SraVaani components and parameter counts, from the released
checkpoint. All parameters are fine-tuned in our main configuration.

Figure 3: One fine-tuning step of SraVaani. The two decoders share the
encoder and the 5,000-piece vocabulary. All 449M parameters are trained,
and the encoder gets half the learning rate of the decoders.

The front end converts 16 kHz audio to 128-dimensional log-mel features,
using 25 ms windows and a 10 ms hop. Three convolutional stages reduce
the frame rate by a factor of eight. A linear projection and 17
Conformer blocks \[25\] then produce encoder states of width 1024. The
encoder has approximately 433 million parameters.

The main decoder is a Token-and-Duration Transducer \[8\]. A 640-unit
LSTM prediction network represents the tokens emitted so far. A joint
network combines that representation $`g_{u}`$ with the encoder state
$`h_{t}`$:

```math
z_{t,u}=W_{o}\operatorname{ReLU}(W_{h}h_{t}+W_{g}g_{u}),\quad z_{t,u}\in\mathbb{R}^{5006}. \tag{2}
```

The 5,006 output values cover 5,000 vocabulary pieces, one blank symbol
and five duration choices $`D=\{0,1,2,3,4\}`$. Predicting durations lets
the decoder advance by more than one frame. A separate CTC head predicts
the vocabulary and blank at each encoder frame.

### 4.2  Training objective

We keep the checkpoint’s combination of TDT and CTC losses. If
$`\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{U})`$ is the target sequence, the TDT loss
sums the probabilities of all token-and-duration paths that produce
it \[8\]. With $`P(v,d\mid t,u)=P_{T}(v\mid t,u)P_{D}(d\mid t,u)`$, a
forward variable can be written as

```math
\displaystyle\alpha(t,u)={} \displaystyle\sum_{d\in D\setminus\{0\}}\alpha(t-d,u)P(\varnothing,d\mid t-d,u) \tag{3}
```

```math
\displaystyle+\sum_{d\in D}\alpha(t-d,u-1)P(y_{u},d\mid t-d,u-1).
```

The TDT loss is
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{TDT}}=-\log P(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})`$. For
CTC \[26\], $`\mathcal{B}`$ collapses repeated tokens and blanks, giving

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CTC}}=-\log\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{B}^{-1}(\mathbf{y})}\prod_{t=1}^{T}P(\pi_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}). \tag{4}
```

We use the original loss weighting:

```math
\mathcal{L}=0.7\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{TDT}}+0.3\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CTC}}. \tag{5}
```

The original TDT settings for logit under-normalisation
($`\sigma=0.02`$) and occasional RNN-T loss use ($`\omega=0.1`$) are
unchanged. Blanks advance by at least one frame, while token predictions
may use zero duration.

### 4.3  Fine-tuning configuration

We load the released checkpoint in NeMo \[24\] and keep the tokenizer
and decoder dimensions unchanged. Every training label is checked before
use. Table 6 compares our settings with the official recipe.

|                      |                       |                            |
| -------------------- | --------------------- | -------------------------- |
| Setting              | Official script       | Ours                       |
| Encoder              | frozen                | trained, LR $`\times 0.5`$ |
| Peak LR (decoders)   | $`10^{-4}`$           | $`10^{-4}`$                |
| Warm-up              | 10 updates            | 5% (494 updates)           |
| Schedule             | cosine to $`10^{-6}`$ | cosine to $`10^{-6}`$      |
| Clips per update     | $`4\times 8=32`$      | $`8\times 8=64`$           |
| Epochs               | 2                     | 6 (and 12)                 |
| Precision            | fp32                  | bf16 mixed                 |
| Joint sub-batch      | 4                     | 1                          |
| Validation           | once per epoch        | twice per epoch            |
| Checkpoint selection | last weights          | best validation WER        |

Table 6: Our fine-tuning settings compared with the official SraVaani
fine-tuning script.

The main experiment updates all model parameters. We use AdamW with
$`\beta_{1}=0.9`$, $`\beta_{2}=0.98`$ and weight decay $`10^{-3}`$. The
encoder’s peak learning rate is $`5\times 10^{-5}`$; the prediction
network and output heads use $`10^{-4}`$. The schedule warms up for 5%
of updates, then follows cosine decay to $`10^{-6}`$. At update $`s`$,
it is

```math
\eta(s)=\begin{cases}\eta_{\max}s/W,&s<W,\\
\eta_{\min}+\frac{\eta_{\max}-\eta_{\min}}{2}(1+\cos\theta_{s}),&s\geq W,\end{cases} \tag{6}
```

Here $`\theta_{s}=\pi(s-W)/(S-W)`$. For six epochs, $`W=494`$ and
$`S=9888`$; for 12 epochs we use $`W=989`$ and $`S=19776`$. We clip the
global gradient norm to 1 and use bf16 mixed precision.

Each mini-batch contains eight clips. We accumulate gradients over eight
mini-batches, giving 64 clips per update. There are 1,648 updates per
epoch. Eight workers read the tar shards, and we reshuffle shard order
each epoch. Training uses the checkpoint’s SpecAugment settings: two
frequency masks of up to 27 bins and ten time masks of up to 5% of the
clip length \[27\].

The TDT joint tensor can be large, especially for a long clip with many
target tokens. We reduce its computation sub-batch from four clips to
one. Peak reported GPU memory is 19.1 GiB. We evaluate the complete
5,049-clip validation set twice per epoch and choose the checkpoint with
the lowest validation WER. The six-epoch run takes around three hours on
one RTX 5090, including validation. We also train an encoder-frozen
configuration, keeping the other settings the same, to test how much
changing the encoder matters.

### 4.4  Decoding

We use greedy TDT decoding without an external language model. The
decoder chooses a token and duration at each step, stops adding tokens
for blanks and advances according to the predicted duration. We limit
output to ten tokens at a frame. For the CTC comparison, we take the
most likely frame-level symbols and collapse repeats and blanks.

## 5.  Experiments

### 5.1  Baselines and ablations

We test released SraVaani without adaptation, full fine-tuning for six
epochs, a second six-epoch seed, and a 12-epoch run. Three further runs
freeze the encoder, remove numeric clips, or remove OpenSLR from
training. The numeric-data ablation keeps 90,924 training clips (120.1
hours); the no-OpenSLR run keeps 36,315 clips (95.3 hours). We also
measure the CTC output of the main model.

For a model-family comparison, we fine-tune Whisper large-v3-turbo (809M
parameters) on the same training split. Whisper uses AdamW, a
$`10^{-5}`$ peak learning rate, 5% warm-up, linear decay, bf16 precision
and three training epochs. The language token is Nepali, and decoding is
greedy. Its checkpoint is chosen on validation WER.

We also evaluate AI4Bharat’s Nepali IndicConformer. Its released
120M-parameter model has a vocabulary without Latin letters. For
fine-tuning, we keep its encoder but train a new 5,000-piece BPE
tokenizer and reinitialise the decoders, yielding about 128M parameters.
This run uses six epochs, with peak learning rates of $`10^{-4}`$ for
the encoder and $`10^{-3}`$ for the decoders.

### 5.2  Test sets

The in-domain test set contains 4,610 clips (9.08 hours) from the six
sources shown in Table 3. We report results for the full set, for each
source and for code-mixed versus Nepali-only clips. The test subset with
numbers or symbols in the original transcripts has 1,125 clips. Model
selection uses validation data only.

For an out-of-domain check, we use 586 clips (about 1.8 hours) from the
Nepali FLEURS test split \[22\]. We exclude 138 clips containing digits
because the references do not specify which spoken number form was used.
The audio is unchanged, and all systems are scored on the same selected
clips.

### 5.3  Mixed-script FLEURS references

FLEURS writes many English loans and names in Devanagari. Our training
targets instead use Latin letters for English words. To see how much
this difference affects WER, we create a second, mixed-script set of
FLEURS references (examples in Table 7). We convert English loans and
foreign names to lowercase Latin script and separate attached Nepali
suffixes. Common Nepali word forms, names of countries and major cities,
and Nepali terms for languages and nationalities stay in Devanagari.

An LLM made the initial text conversion from the original FLEURS
references without seeing model predictions. We checked phonetic
consistency and conversion rules automatically, but did not complete
human verification. The conversion changes 390 of 586 sentences; 9.3% of
words in the resulting references are in Latin script. This is a test of
writing conventions on read speech, not a new spoken code-switching
benchmark.

### 5.4  Evaluation metrics

All scoring uses lowercased text with punctuation removed. We report
corpus-level word error rate (WER) and character error rate (CER)
overall and for each domain. To separate recognition from writing
convention, we also use four diagnostic metrics. _Language-specific WER_
assigns substitution and deletion errors to the language of the
reference word, and insertions by the predicted script. _English exact
match_ measures English words written correctly in Latin script; _wrong
script_ counts recognisable English words written in Devanagari.
_Romanised WER_ compares approximately phonetic Latin-script forms,
reducing penalties for some script and spelling differences. Finally,
_switch-point error_ measures substitutions and deletions next to a
language boundary, compared with words elsewhere \[12, 13, 14, 15\]. We
use corpus-level error totals, not a mean of clip-level scores. We
estimate 95% confidence intervals with 1,000 paired bootstrap resamples
over clips. These intervals describe uncertainty over clips and do not
account for shared-speaker dependencies.

### 5.5  Implementation details

The experiments reported in this paper used one NVIDIA RTX 5090 with 32
GB of memory, NeMo 2.7.3 and the recorded PyTorch/CUDA environment.
Training was logged with TensorBoard and Weights & Biases.

## 6.  Results and Discussion

### 6.1  Main results

SraVaani without fine-tuning reaches 48.6% WER on the six-domain test
set. After six epochs, its WER is 17.1% (95% CI 16.6–17.6%). A different
seed gives 17.0%. Extending training to 12 epochs lowers WER to 16.3%.
Full results appear in Table 8. Gains are seen in all six domains,
although spontaneous podcasts and news remain harder than read speech.

The two best systems have similar overall accuracy: Whisper
large-v3-turbo reaches 16.4% WER, and 12-epoch SraVaani reaches 16.3%.
SraVaani has 449M parameters compared with Whisper’s 809M. The small
difference between their WER scores is not statistically significant
under our paired bootstrap. IndicConformer reaches 19.9% after
adaptation.

### 6.2  Code-mixed and switch-point performance

Table 9 separates English and Nepali errors. Before fine-tuning,
English-word WER is 80.4%, and only 21.2% of English words match the
reference exactly. After six epochs, English-word WER is 20.1% and exact
recognition is 82.3%. With 12 epochs, these numbers improve to 18.8% and
83.6%. Nepali-word WER also drops, from 36.1% before adaptation to 15.9%
after six epochs.

The gains are not only a change in script. Romanised WER, which largely
ignores script differences, drops from 41.3% to 15.5% after six epochs.
Before adaptation, words next to language switches have a 65.9% error
rate compared with 35.5% elsewhere. After adaptation, switch-point
errors are 14.3%, close to 15.2% on other words. Code-mixed clips still
have slightly higher WER than Nepali-only clips (17.4% versus 15.2% in
the six-epoch run).

### 6.3  Encoder adaptation and training duration

The official fine-tuning recipe freezes the encoder. In our experiments,
that gives 25.5% WER, compared with 17.1% when we update the full
network. The difference is 8.4 WER points (95% CI +8.0 to +8.8). The
frozen-encoder system learns much of the desired output script, but its
romanised WER remains 23.5%, compared with 15.5% for full fine-tuning.
Updating the encoder therefore appears to help recognise the speech
itself, not just change its written form.

The validation curves in Figure 4 show that the frozen-encoder run
levels off earlier. The six-epoch full model ends at 12.94% validation
WER, while the frozen-encoder run ends near 19.9%. Twelve epochs reduce
test WER by another 0.8 points, to 16.3%, with a 95% CI for this change
of $`-1.0`$ to $`-0.6`$. Figure 5 shows the two training schedules. The
12-epoch run still improves at its final checkpoint, so this experiment
does not establish complete convergence.

### 6.4  Effect of numeric training data

Removing training clips whose source transcripts contain digits or
symbols reduces training audio from 152.9 to 120.1 hours. This model has
19.8% overall WER, 2.7 points worse than the main six-epoch model. The
largest difference is on the 1,125 numeric test clips: WER rises from
14.3% to 21.3%. On the remaining test clips, the difference is small
(+0.26 points; 95% CI $`-0.04`$ to +0.56).

Table 11 looks at number words rather than whole utterances. With the
full training data, the error rates are 4.9% for English number words
and 6.4% for Nepali number words. Without numeric training clips they
rise to 16.6% and 29.8%. These results support keeping spoken-number
examples in training, although the way we selected labels can introduce
bias and needs further auditing.

### 6.5  Out-of-domain evaluation

Results on the standard Nepali FLEURS references show a loss after
adaptation. SraVaani’s WER increases from 22.6% zero-shot to 31.1% after
six epochs (Table 12). The two reference conventions explain part of
this difference. The original references often use Devanagari for
English loans, while our fine-tuned model writes those words in Latin
script. With our mixed-script FLEURS references, WER instead falls from
30.0% zero-shot to 23.4% after six epochs.

This change does not mean the out-of-domain problem has disappeared.
When we ignore both script and word boundaries, romanised CER still
increases from 5.1% to 7.6%. Thus, part of the standard FLEURS drop is a
transcription mismatch and part is a real recognition loss. The
frozen-encoder model also loses accuracy, suggesting that decoder output
habits contribute to the change. Training with examples from both
writing conventions may be useful, but we have not tested that here.

### 6.6  Effect of Nepali read speech

OpenSLR supplies many short, mostly Nepali-only clips. Removing it makes
the full-test WER 18.5%, compared with 17.1% for the main run. Most of
the loss comes from OpenSLR test speech, where WER rises from 12.4% to
29.3%. On the five code-mixed domains together, WER changes by only 0.27
points, although Nepali-word errors increase slightly while English-word
errors decrease slightly. Out-of-domain FLEURS WER also rises from 31.1%
to 36.2%. In this setting, monolingual read speech helps preserve Nepali
recognition and out-of-domain performance.

### 6.7  Comparison with other models

The models differ by language and speaking style. Zero-shot Whisper has
a very high WER in our setup (121.5%), partly because repeated phrases
cause many insertions; 388 test outputs show such loops. Fine-tuning
reduces Whisper WER to 16.4%. It recognises English words better than
12-epoch SraVaani (14.8% versus 18.8% English-word WER). SraVaani does
better on Nepali words (15.3% versus 17.0%) and on OpenSLR read speech
(12.1% versus 16.8%).

The Whisper run used only three epochs and its validation score was
still improving. We therefore do not claim that the present comparison
measures the best possible Whisper configuration. Fine-tuned
IndicConformer, with about 128M parameters, reaches 19.9% WER. Much of
the gap is on English words. On OpenSLR read speech, IndicConformer and
SraVaani perform similarly.

### 6.8  Error analysis

Before adaptation, SraVaani often uses the wrong Indic script. It
produces mainly another Indic script for 40.9% of OpenSLR test
recordings and 14.0% of the full test set. After fine-tuning, none of
the test clips is mostly in another script.

After adaptation, errors mainly involve short Nepali particles and
postpositions such as \nepalifontको, \nepalifontले, \nepalifontछ and
\nepalifontर may be missed; spelling choices such as \nepalifontहरु
versus \nepalifontहरू may differ; and uncommon English names, technical
terms or programming tokens are still difficult. Some differences also
come from inconsistent reference spelling. Table 13 shows representative
output examples, including a difficult short code-mixed clip.

## 7.  Conclusion

We adapted SraVaani to Nepali–English code-mixed speech using a
172.8-hour corpus while retaining its tokenizer and output heads. The
main preparation challenge was resolving digits and symbols into labels
that fit the vocabulary and the spoken language. With full fine-tuning,
test WER fell from 48.6% to 17.1% after six epochs and to 16.3% after 12
epochs. The encoder-frozen and data ablations show that updating the
encoder and keeping numeric speech both contribute to the gain. Nepali
read speech helps with Nepali-only and out-of-domain recognition.

The FLEURS experiment shows a limitation of reporting a single WER for
code-mixed systems: changing the reference writing style reverses the
apparent direction of the result. A script-insensitive measure still
indicates some loss, so the issue is not only spelling. The next steps
are to verify numeric labels independently, test speaker-disjoint
splits, and train with more than one accepted transcription convention.

## Limitations and Ethical Considerations

The inherited train, validation and test splits are not guaranteed to be
speaker-disjoint. As a result, in-domain WER may be optimistic. FLEURS
covers read speech with limited English content and does not represent
spontaneous out-of-domain code-mixing. Its mixed-script references were
generated with LLM assistance and automatically checked, but not
independently validated by listening to each recording.

Selecting number readings from the base model’s predictions may favour
its existing errors. An initial audit found five incorrect labels among
150 sampled numeric clips before revising the candidate rules; the final
versions still require independent review. Only the main configuration
was repeated with a second training seed, and Whisper’s validation WER
was still improving after its shorter training schedule. Bootstrap
intervals account for sampled clips, not shared speakers or run-to-run
variation.
