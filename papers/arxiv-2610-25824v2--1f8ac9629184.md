# MS-BENCH: A CONDITION-STRATIFIED MULTI-SPEAKER ASR BENCHMARK

Chunjiang He^(1,∗), Zheng Zi^(1,∗), Bingshen Mu¹, Yurun Li¹, Jingyi
Wang¹, Zhonghua Fu¹, Shuai Wang², Liumeng Xue², Bengu Wu³, Lei Xie^(1,†)
^(†)^(†)thanks: ^(\*)Equal contribution. ^(†)Corresponding author.

###### Abstract

Multi-speaker automatic speech recognition (MSASR) has advanced rapidly
with end-to-end speech large language models. However, existing
benchmarks cover limited ranges of specific multi-speaker conditions and
scenarios, making it difficult to reveal system limitations under
diverse conditions and scenarios. We introduce MS-Bench¹¹ 1 [Demo page:
https://aslp-lab.github.io/MS-Bench/](https://aslp-lab.github.io/MS-Bench/),
a condition-stratified benchmark for fine-grained MSASR evaluation.
MS-Bench integrates both public and internal benchmarks and
characterizes each recording along five dimensions: speaker number,
overlap ratio, speaker similarity, speaker turn interval, and acoustic
quality. We stratify these dimensions into condition ranges and
construct MS-Bench with broad coverage of conditions, scenarios,
languages, and recording devices. Evaluating representative commercial,
open-source, and cascaded systems, we find that overlap affects both
lexical recognition and speaker attribution, while other factors exhibit
more task-specific effects on recognition, attribution, or temporal
prediction. These results demonstrate that aggregate metrics alone
obscure important MSASR failure modes and highlight the value of
condition-stratified evaluation. MS-Bench aims to reveal
condition-specific limitations of current MSASR systems and guide future
improvements.

## 1 Introduction

Figure 1: Overview of the MS-Bench construction pipeline.

Understanding real-world conversations requires accurate recognition of
content and a faithful account of how speakers exchange information.
Reliable multi-speaker transcription therefore underpins conversational
applications, where incorrect speaker attribution can distort the
interpretation of otherwise correctly recognized speech. Recognizing
speech in the presence of competing speakers is a central aspect of the
long-standing cocktail-party problem \[3\]. Beyond recognizing acoustic
content, grounding linguistic descriptions in the corresponding audio
events and temporal locations has become an important capability for
audio understanding models \[25, 23, 5\]. In multi-speaker
conversations, a related requirement is to associate recognized speech
with the correct speakers and time intervals. Multi-speaker automatic
speech recognition (MSASR) aims to transcribe speech while attributing
each segment to its corresponding speaker, thereby addressing the
critical question of “who spoke what and when”. Compared with
single-speaker ASR, MSASR remains challenging due to overlapping speech,
rapid speaker transitions, speaker similarity, and diverse acoustic
conditions in real-world scenarios \[10, 11, 12\]. Traditional cascaded
MSASR pipelines optimize speaker diarization, segmentation, and ASR as
separate modules \[14, 29, 9\], which can lead to error propagation.
Recent advances in audio and speech large language models have
demonstrated strong capabilities in audio understanding and reasoning,
including long-form audio processing and temporal perception \[8, 19,
26\]. End-to-end MSASR systems based on speech large language models
(SLLMs), such as DiaScriber \[15\], VibeVoice-ASR \[16\],
SoulX-Transcriber \[4\], and MOSS-Transcribe-Diarize \[28\], have
achieved competitive performance on widely used multi-speaker
benchmarks, including AISHELL-4 \[7\], AliMeeting \[29\],
MagicData-RAMC \[27\], AMI \[2\], and MLC-SLM \[14\]. These advances
raise an important question: can current audio LLM-based systems
reliably perform MSASR under challenging cocktail-party-like conditions,
correctly recognizing speech and grounding it to the corresponding
speakers and time intervals? However, existing benchmarks remain
insufficient to comprehensively answer this question. First, they
generally lack systematic coverage across different levels of key
multi-speaker conditions, including speaker number, overlap ratio,
speaker similarity, speaker turn interval, and acoustic quality, making
it difficult to evaluate MSASR performance across these factors.
Moreover, most benchmarks focus on specific application scenarios:
AISHELL-4, AliMeeting, and AMI mainly target meeting speech, whereas
MagicData-RAMC and MLC-SLM focus on conversational speech. Consequently,
these limitations make it difficult to reveal condition-specific
weaknesses of current MSASR systems and guide further system
improvement.

In this work, we introduce MS-Bench, a condition-stratified benchmark
for fine-grained evaluation of MSASR systems. MS-Bench assesses a key
aspect of the cocktail-party problem by systematically evaluating how
well MSASR systems determine what was spoken, who spoke it, and when it
occurred across diverse multi-speaker conditions. MS-Bench integrates
multiple public and internal benchmarks and characterizes each recording
along five dimensions: speaker number, overlap ratio, speaker
similarity, speaker turn interval, and acoustic quality. We stratify
these dimensions into different condition ranges and select recordings
to achieve broad condition coverage while balancing the remaining
factors. In this way, MS-Bench provides broad coverage of multi-speaker
conditions, application scenarios, languages, and recording devices,
enabling more systematic analysis of system performance under different
conditions. We evaluate representative commercial, open-source, and
cascaded MSASR systems and analyze their performance at both the system
level and across the five condition dimensions. The results show that
overlapping speech jointly degrades lexical recognition and speaker
attribution; speaker similarity and turn transitions increase
speaker-attribution confusion; acoustic degradation primarily affects
lexical recognition accuracy and is associated with shorter predicted
speech timelines; and higher speaker counts are associated with greater
attribution difficulty in most systems after controlling for dataset and
overlap. Overall, these findings suggest that the evaluated systems do
not yet achieve consistently reliable MSASR under challenging
cocktail-party-like conditions, highlighting the need for further
improvements in overlap-robust recognition, speaker attribution, and
temporal modeling.

## 2 MS-Bench

### 2.1 Sourcing and Characterization

We first collect recordings from publicly available and internal MSASR
benchmarks. The publicly available benchmarks include representative
multi-speaker benchmarks, such as AISHELL-4, AliMeeting, and
AISHELL-5 \[6\]. Each recording is characterized from three
perspectives: speaker interaction, acoustic condition, and recording
metadata. Specifically, we extract the number of speakers, overlap
ratio, speaker similarity, and speaker turn intervals from the speaker
annotations. We measure speaker similarity by extracting CAM++ \[24\]
embeddings from 3–5 non-overlapping segments longer than 2 seconds for
each speaker and computing pairwise cosine similarities. We calculate
speaker turn intervals only between consecutive non-overlapping speaker
turns to avoid the influence of overlapping regions. In addition, we
evaluate acoustic quality using DNSMOS P.835 \[18\] and NISQA \[13\],
and collect metadata information including scenario, language, and
recording device.

### 2.2 Condition Space Construction

Based on the recording-level characteristics obtained above, we
construct a unified condition space by partitioning five dimensions:
speaker number, overlap ratio, speaker similarity, speaker turn
interval, and acoustic quality. We group speaker number using predefined
count ranges, while we manually partition the overlap ratio by severity
to preserve the interpretability of different overlap levels. We divide
other continuous attributes, including speaker similarity, speaker turn
interval, and acoustic quality, according to empirical quantiles to
maintain sufficient sample support within each range. Specifically, we
represent speaker similarity by the maximum pairwise cosine similarity
within each recording to capture the most confusable speaker pair.
Speaker turn interval is represented by its 25th percentile to
characterize relatively rapid speaker transitions while reducing the
influence of long pauses and extreme values. For acoustic quality, we
jointly consider DNSMOS P.835 and NISQA. Because these metrics have
different score distributions, we first convert their scores to
percentile ranks, denoted $`p_{D}`$ and $`p_{N}`$, and combine them as
$`Q=(p_{D}+p_{N})/2`$. We further define $`d_{N}=1-Q`$, where larger
values indicate poorer acoustic quality. Fig. 1 summarizes the resulting
condition ranges.

### 2.3 Candidate Pool Construction

Using the constructed condition space, we build an intermediate
candidate pool to reduce bias from uneven condition distributions across
source datasets. Instead of sampling recordings by dataset identity, we
identify candidates based on their condition profiles, covering
different ranges across the five condition dimensions while avoiding
over-representation of densely populated regions. We also consider
source diversity to prevent any individual dataset from dominating the
candidate pool. The resulting pool provides a structured search space
for constructing the final benchmark subset.

### 2.4 Benchmark Subset Selection

Given the structured candidate pool, we construct the final benchmark
subset by jointly optimizing condition coverage, multi-factor
co-occurrence, condition-profile diversity, and source diversity. To
obtain a compact yet representative subset, each candidate recording
$`i`$ is assigned a selection utility
$`U_{i}=0.40C_{i}+0.25M_{i}+0.20D_{i}+0.15R_{i}`$, where $`C_{i}`$
measures the contribution to condition coverage, $`M_{i}`$ characterizes
the co-occurrence of multiple challenging conditions, $`D_{i}`$ measures
diversity gain in the condition space, and $`R_{i}`$ encourages the
inclusion of under-represented sources. We obtain the final subset by
maximizing total utility under constraints on subset size, minimum
coverage over predefined operating ranges, and source distribution.
Finally, we manually inspect audio integrity, transcripts, speaker
annotations, and timestamps. We replace samples that fail quality
control with eligible candidates with similar condition profiles.

### 2.5 Benchmark Overview

MS-Bench is a condition-stratified benchmark with a total duration of
32.72 hours. Recording durations range from 1.38 to 159.07 minutes, with
a median of 16.50 minutes. The benchmark covers a broad range of speech
scenarios, spanning meetings, spontaneous conversations, films and
television content, podcasts, dinner-party conversations, educational
content, live-streaming content, in-vehicle interactions, and
smart-glasses interactions. The recordings mainly contain Chinese and
English speech, with additional samples in Portuguese, Japanese, Thai,
Italian, and Spanish. They are collected using heterogeneous recording
devices, including microphone arrays, in-cabin recording systems, and
mobile devices. Beyond scenario and metadata diversity, MS-Bench
systematically covers the five condition dimensions defined in Sec. 2.2:
speaker number, overlap ratio, speaker similarity, speaker turn
interval, and acoustic quality. The number of speakers ranges from 2 to
14, and the duration-weighted average overlap ratio is 21.87%. As shown
in Fig. 1, the benchmark covers all predefined operating ranges,
enabling condition-stratified analysis of multi-speaker transcription
systems under diverse interaction and acoustic conditions.

## 3 Evaluation Setup and Metrics

We evaluate the systems using diarization error rate (DER), concatenated
minimum-permutation word error rate (cpWER), time-constrained
minimum-permutation word error rate (tcpWER) \[22\], and word error rate
(WER). DER measures speaker diarization performance with a 0.5-second
collar. cpWER evaluates speaker-attributed transcription by finding the
speaker permutation that minimizes the overall word error rate. tcpWER
further incorporates temporal constraints into speaker–transcript
matching with a 5-second collar. Speaker-agnostic WER evaluates
transcription accuracy independently of speaker assignment by
disregarding speaker labels during scoring.

An inference unit is the audio span submitted in one independent system
request and scored against the corresponding reference span. Recordings
are kept intact whenever supported; those exceeding the system-specific
input limits are split into chunks of at most 90 minutes for the MOSS
systems, 60 minutes for VibeVoice-ASR, and 5 minutes for
SoulX-Transcriber, while the remaining systems process the original
recordings without chunking. We score independently inferred chunks
against their corresponding reference windows because speaker IDs need
not remain consistent across requests. Units are excluded only when
their outputs cannot be reliably scored, including API refusal,
structurally incomplete generation at the token limit, invalid intervals
with $`\mathrm{end}<\mathrm{start}`$, or a language mismatch with the
reference. Parseable repetitive or collapsed outputs remain in scoring
and are reported as soft degradation.

For diagnostic analysis, we decompose the standard WER numerator
$`\mathrm{S}+\mathrm{I}+\mathrm{D}`$ into two mutually exclusive
sources. Substitutions are treated as lexical errors, while every
insertion and deletion is assigned to either lexical recognition or
segmentation according to its cause. Specifically,
$`E_{\mathrm{lex}}=(\mathrm{S}+\mathrm{I}_{\mathrm{lex}}+\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{lex}})/N_{\mathrm{ref}},E_{\mathrm{seg}}=(\mathrm{I}_{\mathrm{seg}}+\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{seg}})/N_{\mathrm{ref}}`$,
where $`\mathrm{I}_{\mathrm{seg}}`$ and $`\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{seg}}`$
denote content introduced or lost due to falsely detected, missed, or
discarded speech regions. Thus,
$`E_{\mathrm{lex}}+E_{\mathrm{seg}}=\mathrm{WER}`$. Speaker attribution
is evaluated separately by $`E_{\mathrm{attr}}`$, the fraction of
reference utterances attributed to an incorrect speaker.

For condition-wise comparisons, $`\Delta_{x}`$ denotes the difference in
error rate between the two conditions being compared, in percentage
points, where $`x\in{\mathrm{lex},\mathrm{seg},\mathrm{attr}}`$. We
define the two conditions separately for each analysis, and restrict
comparisons to inference units containing samples from both conditions,
reducing between-unit confounding. We estimate 95% confidence intervals
by resampling recordings with replacement and recomputing
$`\Delta_{x}`$; this recording-level cluster bootstrap keeps all
utterances and chunks from the same recording together during
resampling.

## 4 RESULTS AND ANALYSIS

We evaluate a cascaded MSASR system, end-to-end MSASR models, commercial
ASR services, and omni-modal LLMs. Specifically, we evaluate
MOSS-Transcribe-Diarize-Pro²² 2 [MOSS-Transcribe-Diarize-Pro
webpage](https://platform.mosi.cn/docs/scenarios/transcribe-diarization) \[28\],
MOSS-Transcribe-Diarize \[28\], VibeVoice-ASR \[16\],
SoulX-Transcriber \[4\], Doubao ASR 2.0³³ 3 [Doubao-ASR 2.0
webpage](https://console.volcengine.com/ark/model/detail?name=doubao-seed-asr-2-0),
Qwen3.8-Omni-Flash⁴⁴ 4 [Qwen3.8-Omni-Flash
webpage](https://modelstudio.console.alibabacloud.com/ap-southeast-1/model/market/detail/qwen3.8-omni-flash) \[21\],
and a cascaded system using pyannote
speaker-diarization-community-1 \[17, 1\] followed by Qwen3-ASR \[20\].

### 4.1 Overall Performance

Table 1 summarizes the overall performance and robustness of the
evaluated systems. MOSS-Transcribe-Diarize-Pro achieves the best overall
performance, with the lowest DER (10.96%), cpWER (21.95%), tcpWER
(22.81%), and WER (22.49%), followed by MOSS-Transcribe-Diarize.
Qwen3.8-Omni-Flash achieves the next-lowest cpWER (29.43%) and WER
(27.86%), while the cascaded pyannote+Qwen3-ASR system obtains a similar
DER (17.35% vs. 17.97%) but higher cpWER (43.25% vs. 29.43%).

The systems also differ in output robustness. MOSS-Transcribe-Diarize
and the cascaded system have no excluded units, whereas
MOSS-Transcribe-Diarize-Pro excludes 15 units: three are rejected by the
API, and 12 contain invalid timestamps. Thus, although
MOSS-Transcribe-Diarize-Pro achieves lower error rates on the valid
outputs, MOSS-Transcribe-Diarize provides better output robustness and
coverage. Qwen3.8-Omni-Flash has five excluded and two soft-degradation
units, further showing that accuracy and robustness do not always vary
consistently across systems.

Figure 2 decomposes WER into $`E_{\mathrm{lex}}`$ and
$`E_{\mathrm{seg}}`$: lexical errors dominate for every system, with
$`E_{\mathrm{lex}}`$ spanning 19.27%–38.17% whereas $`E_{\mathrm{seg}}`$
stays within 1.32%–6.02%. The composition is nevertheless
system-specific—MOSS-Transcribe-Diarize-Pro gains over
MOSS-Transcribe-Diarize mainly by lowering $`E_{\mathrm{lex}}`$
(23.48%$`\rightarrow`$19.27%) at the cost of a higher
$`E_{\mathrm{seg}}`$ (1.54%$`\rightarrow`$3.22%), while
SoulX-Transcriber is dominated by insertions
($`\mathrm{I}_{\mathrm{lex}}`$ 15.5%) rather than by recognition or
segmentation loss. Error mechanisms therefore differ across systems even
when aggregate WER is close, which motivates the condition-wise
decomposition in Sec. 4.2.

### 4.2 Condition-stratified Analysis

Overlap. For each reference utterance $`u`$, $`r_{\mathrm{ov}}(u)`$ is
the fraction of its duration covered by the speech intervals of other
speakers. We compare only $`r_{\mathrm{ov}}=0`$ and
$`r_{\mathrm{ov}}\geq 0.5`$, and retain inference units containing both
conditions. This within-inference-unit comparison keeps the recording
source, acoustic environment, and system input context fixed, thereby
reducing between-recording confounding. Table 2 shows that heavy overlap
significantly increases $`E_{\mathrm{lex}}`$ by 27.2–38.6 pp and
$`E_{\mathrm{attr}}`$ by 4.2–19.1 pp across all seven systems,
indicating that overlap consistently degrades both lexical recognition
and speaker attribution. To further distinguish missed speech from
overlap-localization and speaker-assignment errors, we examine the
system outputs within reference overlap regions. We first evaluate
whether the system temporally captures the onset and offset of an
overlap region: a reference overlap boundary is considered localized if
a hypothesized speech boundary occurs within $`\pm 0.5`$ s of it. Under
this criterion, only 39.9–85.3% of reference overlap boundaries are
successfully localized across systems, indicating substantial variation
in their ability to represent the temporal structure of overlapping
speech. Although 71–93% of overlapped speech is covered by at least one
hypothesized speaker, only 31–63% is covered by the correct speaker.
These results show that overlap-related failures are not simply caused
by missing speech altogether: systems often detect speech activity in
overlap regions, but may fail to represent the overlap boundaries
accurately and, more importantly, assign a substantial portion of the
detected speech to the wrong speaker.

Speaker similarity. For an ordered pair of true speakers
$`(s_{i},s_{j})`$, we define the pairwise confusion
$`C_{i\rightarrow j}`$ as the fraction of aligned tokens from $`s_{i}`$
that the system attributes to $`s_{j}`$, and relate it to the speaker
similarity $`\mathrm{Sim}_{ij}`$ of the two speakers. When the analysis
is restricted to completely non-overlapping utterances, Table 2 shows
significant positive correlations between $`\mathrm{Sim}_{ij}`$ and
$`C_{i\rightarrow j}`$ for all seven systems, with Spearman’s $`\rho`$
ranging from 0.248 to 0.323. Thus, the more acoustically similar two
speakers are, the more likely content from one speaker is to be
attributed to the other, indicating that acoustic speaker similarity is
a consistent source of attribution error even in the absence of overlap.

Acoustic quality. To separate the effect of acoustic quality from
overlap and speaker-transition effects, we restrict this analysis to
utterances without overlap and away from speaker-turn boundaries, and
correlate the recording-level acoustic degradation score $`d_{N}`$ with
each error measure. Table 2 shows significant positive correlations
between $`d_{N}`$ and $`E_{\mathrm{lex}}`$ for all seven systems, with
Spearman’s $`\rho`$ ranging from 0.181 to 0.569. In contrast, the
correlation with $`E_{\mathrm{attr}}`$ is weak and significant for only
one system. These results indicate that acoustic degradation primarily
affects lexical recognition rather than speaker attribution. We further
examine whether acoustic degradation changes the temporal extent of
predicted speech. Let
$`R_{\mathrm{time}}={T_{\mathrm{hyp}}}/{T_{\mathrm{ref}}},`$ where
$`T_{\mathrm{hyp}}`$ and $`T_{\mathrm{ref}}`$ denote the total
hypothesized and reference speech durations, respectively. In Table 2,
$`\rho_{\mathrm{time}}`$ denotes the Spearman correlation
$`\rho(d_{N},R_{\mathrm{time}})`$. The table shows significantly
negative correlations for six of the seven systems, indicating that
poorer acoustic conditions are generally associated with shorter
predicted speech timelines. We interpret this result specifically as
systematic temporal contraction rather than timestamp-boundary error,
since $`R_{\mathrm{time}}`$ measures the overall temporal extent of
hypothesized speech and is insensitive to a uniform temporal shift.

Speaker turn interval. We identify non-overlapping reference speaker
switches, expand each boundary by $`\pm\delta`$, and, within the same
inference unit, compare utterances intersecting a switch neighborhood
with utterances far from all switches. This controls recording factors
while testing the local effect of a turn boundary. Table 2 reports
$`\delta=0.5`$ s, for which $`E_{\mathrm{attr}}`$ increases
significantly by 1.51–4.17 pp in all systems. Neither
$`E_{\mathrm{lex}}`$ nor $`E_{\mathrm{seg}}`$ is significant in any
system, and the same qualitative pattern holds for
$`\delta\in\{0.25,1.0\}`$ . Speaker transitions therefore primarily
disrupt _who spoke_, rather than _what was said_.

Speaker number. Speaker number is strongly coupled with dataset
composition in our benchmark: in particular, all recordings in the
$`P3`$ ($`\geq 9`$ speakers) group come from one dataset. We therefore
avoid interpreting the pooled $`P0`$–$`P3`$ trend as a monotonic effect
of speaker count. For the controlled analysis, we exclude $`P3`$ to
reduce this dataset-specific bias, and exclude SoulX-Transcriber because
its 5-min inference units do not always contain all speakers present in
the original recording. To reduce dataset confounding, we compare
recordings relative to others from the same dataset rather than directly
across datasets. Specifically, we center both speaker count and
$`E_{\mathrm{attr}}`$ by subtracting their respective dataset means, and
then compute the Spearman correlation between the two residualized
variables. Thus, the analysis asks whether a recording with more
speakers than the dataset average also tends to exhibit higher
attribution error than the dataset average. In a stricter analysis, we
additionally remove the effect of overlap ratio from both variables
before computing the correlation. We denote this correlation as
$`\rho_{\mathrm{attr}\mid D,O}`$, where $`D`$ and $`O`$ denote dataset
and overlap ratio, respectively. Table 2 shows positive associations for
all six evaluated systems; five are significant after controlling for
dataset, and four remain significant after additionally controlling for
overlap. These results indicate that higher speaker count is associated
with greater speaker-attribution difficulty beyond dataset composition
and, for most systems, beyond overlap alone, without implying causality.

## 5 CONCLUSION

This work introduces MS-Bench, a condition-stratified benchmark for
systematic evaluation of multi-speaker transcription systems. By
characterizing recordings along five dimensions covering speaker
interaction, speaker similarity, and acoustic conditions, MS-Bench
provides a unified framework for analyzing system performance beyond
dataset-level evaluation. Experiments on representative commercial,
open-source, and cascaded systems reveal that overlapping speech
degrades both lexical recognition and speaker attribution, while speaker
similarity and rapid turn transitions increase attribution confusion.
Acoustic degradation is further associated with increased lexical errors
and shorter predicted speech timelines. These findings indicate that,
despite recent advances in audio and speech LLMs, reliable MSASR under
challenging cocktail-party-like conditions remains an open problem for
the evaluated systems. MS-Bench highlights overlap-robust recognition,
accurate speaker attribution, and faithful temporal modeling as
important directions for future research.
