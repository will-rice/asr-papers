Combining TF-GridNet and Mixture Encoder for\
Continuous Speech Separation for Meeting Transcription
======================================================

###### Abstract

Many real-life applications of automatic speech recognition (ASR) require processing of overlapped speech. A common method involves first separating the speech into overlap-free streams on which ASR is performed. Recently, TF-GridNet has shown impressive performance in speech separation in real reverberant conditions. Furthermore, a mixture encoder was proposed that leverages the mixed speech to mitigate the effect of separation artifacts. In this work, we extended the mixture encoder from a static two-speaker scenario to a natural meeting context featuring an arbitrary number of speakers and varying degrees of overlap. We further demonstrate its limits by the integration with separators of varying strength including TF-GridNet. Our experiments result in a new state-of-the-art performance on LibriCSS using a single microphone. They show that TF-GridNet largely closes the gap between previous methods and oracle separation independent of mixture encoding. We further investigate the remaining potential for improvement.

Index Terms—  speech separation, speech recognition, meeting transcription

## 1 Introduction

The transcription of meetings is an important application scenario for automatic speech recognition (ASR). However, it is inherently difficult for multiple reasons, e.g., the spontaneous nature of the speech, reverberation in the room or background noise \[[1](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib1)\]. Another vital aspect is the appropriate handling of speaker overlap. Past work on speech separation has largely focused on fully overlapping speech of only two speakers \[[2](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib2), [3](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib3), [4](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib4)\]. Recently, there has been a shift in attention towards continuous speech separation (CSS) for realistic meeting scenarios \[[5](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib5), [6](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib6), [7](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib7)\].

The methods for multi-speaker ASR can generally be divided into modular and end-to-end approaches. While end-to-end approaches handle separation and recognition jointly and just take a single, global decision, modular approaches may utilize dedicated separation front-ends. Among those separation front-ends, TF-GridNet \[[8](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib8), [9](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib9)\] has recently shown great performance in separating overlapped and noisy speech in the presence of reverberation. Furthermore, a mixture encoder has been proposed to improve the modular approach by providing information about the original speech mixture in addition to the single separated speaker \[[10](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib10)\]. However, the stronger the separator, the less improvement can be expected by this method. Therefore, we test its limits by combining it with separators of different strengths.

The main contributions of this work are

- •
  the extension of the mixture encoder to a meeting scenario with an arbitrary number of speakers and varying degrees of overlap using the CSS approach,
- •
  state-of-the-art results on LibriCSS using a single microphone achieved by the combination with the powerful TF-GridNet separation front-end,
- •
  studying the effect of exploiting mixture information for acoustic modeling in combination with separators of different strengths as well as
- •
  investigating the remaining difference to oracle performance with a novel detailed analysis of the effect of imperfect separation on the speech recognition search space by computing coincidence rates between the reference speaker and the cross channel, which provides additional insights into the capabilities of TF-GridNet.

## 2 Related work

Transcribing meetings requires a more sophisticated handling of overlapping speech than classical two-speaker speech separation models. In CSS, continuous audio streams are separated into multiple non-overlapped streams \[[6](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib6)\]. Under the assumption that not more than two speakers are active at a time, a separator can output two channels and diarization can be used to track the individual speakers in these channels \[[5](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib5)\].

TF-GridNet \[[8](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib8)\] recently excelled in speech separation under reverberant conditions. Its application to a meeting scenario is discussed in \[[11](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib11)\]. The mixture encoder, which enhances a modular approach for multi-speaker ASR by utilizing additional information about the mixture signal in the acoustic model (AM), has previously been employed on fully overlapped speech \[[10](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib10)\] in combination with a bi-directional long-short term memory (BLSTM) separator. Unlike in end-to-end approaches, the speech separation front-end can easily be replaced due to the model’s modularity. In this work, we further combine TF-GridNet and the mixture encoder for meeting transcription.

## 3 Methods

The baseline architecture of the modular approach is depicted in [Figure 1(a)](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#S2.F1.sf1 "In Figure 1 ‣ 2 Related work ‣ Combining TF-GridNet and Mixture Encoder for Continuous Speech Separation for Meeting Transcription"). During separation, the speech mixture $`\mathbf{x}`$ is separated in two channels $`{\hat{\mathbf{x}}}^{0}`$ and $`{\hat{\mathbf{x}}}^{1}`$ which contain estimates of the non-overlapped speech. After that, a simple energy-based voice activity detection (VAD) mechanism is applied to determine the speech segments in each output channel separately. These segments are input to a single encoder called the separation encoder here. After applying upsampling via transposed convolution, a linear mapping and a softmax, we obtain the frame-wise label posteriors $`p{(\left. \mathbf{y}_{t}^{s} \middle| {\hat{\mathbf{x}}}^{s} \right.)}`$ for channel $`s \in {\{ 0,1\}}`$.

Similar to \[[10](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib10)\], a mixture encoder is added in [Figure 1(b)](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#S2.F1.sf2 "In Figure 1 ‣ 2 Related work ‣ Combining TF-GridNet and Mixture Encoder for Continuous Speech Separation for Meeting Transcription"). It receives the original speech mixture $`\mathbf{x}`$ with possibly overlapping speech as input. The segment boundaries are taken from the single-channel VAD. Note that feature extraction and downsampling are considered to be part of the separation and mixture encoder blocks in [Figure 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#S2.F1 "In 2 Related work ‣ Combining TF-GridNet and Mixture Encoder for Continuous Speech Separation for Meeting Transcription"). The separation and mixture encoders’ outputs are concatenated and further processed by a linear projection and another encoder, i.e., the mixture-aware speaker (MAS) encoder. Unlike \[[10](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib10)\], no combination layer is used here and the channels are handled separately.

## 4 Experimental setup

### 4.1 Data

We use the LibriCSS \[[6](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib6)\] dataset to evaluate our models on a task with realistic room acoustics and partially overlapped speech. LibriCSS is derived from the LibriSpeech \[[12](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib12)\] test-clean dataset and features re-recordings of utterances with different overlap ratios set in a meeting room environment. It is a popular dataset for evaluating multi-speaker ASR systems due to its real noise and room acoustics paired with controlled overlap ratios in different subsets \[[6](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib6), [13](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib13), [14](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib14)\].

As LibriCSS offers only evaluation data, we use the original LibriSpeech corpus to simulate training data. This involves producing spatialized and mixed audios, mirroring the process used for the SMS-WSJ dataset \[[4](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib4)\]. Dynamic mixing \[[15](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib15), [16](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib16)\] further helps to increase the amount of generated training data. The speech separators are trained exclusively on this simulated dataset.

For the AM, we first train a baseline single-speaker model on the LibriSpeech corpus. This initial model serves as the base for initialization as described in [Section 4.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#S4.SS3 "4.3 Acoustic model training ‣ 4 Experimental setup ‣ Combining TF-GridNet and Mixture Encoder for Continuous Speech Separation for Meeting Transcription"). After initialization, the AM is fine-tuned using separated audio tracks which are obtained by applying the previously trained separators to the simulated training data. During this AM fine-tuning phase, we incorporate oracle information for VAD. The simulated AM training data amounts to about $`1000\ h`$ after VAD application. The standard LibriSpeech text corpus is used for language model (LM) training.

Since the focus of this work is on single-microphone techniques, only the first microphone of the LibriCSS recordings is used during evaluation. As suggested in \[[6](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib6)\], we use Session0 as a dev set to tune the recognition hyperparameters and present results on the test set, i.e., the remaining sessions excluding Session0.

### 4.2 Speech separation model

The separator training follows the permutation invariant training (PIT) paradigm \[[17](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib17)\] with a source-aggregated signal-to-distortion ratio (SA-SDR) loss \[[18](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib18)\] in the time-domain on fully overlapping data and single-speaker utterances. At test time, the CSS idea from \[[5](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib5)\] is used: The separator is applied on a sliding window on the observation with a size of 4s and a shift of 3s so that an overlap of 1s is present between adjacent windows. This window size is small enough to allow the assumption that at maximum two speakers are active within a single window. Thus, we obtain two outputs with overlap-free speech. To resolve the permutation problem between adjacent segments, which arises because the separator can order its outputs arbitrarily, we determine the permutation with minimal mean squared error (MSE) on the overlapping parts between the segments. This way, the model can handle an arbitrary number of speakers.

As separators, we train a 3-layer BLSTM \[[17](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib17)\] as well as TF-GridNet \[[8](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib8)\]. The BLSTM architecture is simple, yet competitive with the best models on reverberant data \[[19](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib19)\]. Only recently, TF-GridNet advanced the state-of-the-art performance by a large margin for source separation. Additionally, TF-GridNet was published as part of ESPnet \[[20](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib20)\] and is therefore well reproducible. Note that the increased performance comes at the price of an increase in execution time by a factor of roughly 40 compared to the BLSTM.

### 4.3 Acoustic model training

A hybrid hidden Markov model is used to perform ASR. To generate an alignment between the extracted Gammatone features and the 12001 generalized triphone state labels obtained by a classification and regression tree (CART), we employ a Gaussian mixture hidden Markov model baseline similar to \[[21](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib21)\]. Note that the alignments are taken from LibriSpeech, i.e., they are computed on the clean single-speaker utterances by a model trained on the regular LibriSpeech data. They are then edited and padded with silence to fit the simulated data. The model is trained with a frame-wise cross-entropy (f-CE) loss on single-speaker speech which was produced by the speech separation model.

Unlike \[[21](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib21)\], we use a Conformer-based AM \[[22](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib22)\]. In particular, the neural architecture in the baseline uses a convolutional front-end with a sub-sampling factor of 3 followed by a linear layer and 12 512-dimensional Conformer blocks with 8 attention heads. Before the softmax over the output labels, we apply a transposed convolution to upsample by a factor of 3 again. SpecAugment \[[23](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib23)\], dropout and an additional auxiliary loss with the same targets after the $`\text{6}^{\text{th}}`$ Conformer block are used for regularization.

The architecture with the mixture encoder is based on \[[10](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib10)\]. Unlike the baseline, it does not only take the separated single-speaker speech as input, but in addition also the speech mixture. Here, the separation, mixture and MAS encoders consist of 6 Conformer blocks each, all in the same configuration as the baseline blocks. In total, the AM contains 87M parameters without and 127M with mixture encoder.

A LibriSpeech baseline model is first trained for 15 full epochs on the regular LibriSpeech data. We use it to initialize the AM and fine-tune it using the f-CE loss for up to one epoch on the simulated training data. The checkpoint is selected based on the best error rate on the dev set. Longer fine-tuning did not improve the performance. When using the mixture encoder, the baseline Conformer blocks are split into two parts. Blocks 1-6 are used to initialize both the separation encoder as well as the mixture encoder while the MAS encoder is initialized with blocks 7-12. The concatenated separation and mixture encoder outputs $`\mathbf{h} = {\lbrack\mathbf{h}_{sep},\mathbf{h}_{mix}\rbrack} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{2N}`$ are multiplied with a trainable matrix $`\mathbf{W}`$ (“Linear” in [Figure 1(b)](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#S2.F1.sf2 "In Figure 1 ‣ 2 Related work ‣ Combining TF-GridNet and Mixture Encoder for Continuous Speech Separation for Meeting Transcription")) to form the MAS encoder’s input. For initialization, we set

``` math
\mathbf{W} = {\begin{bmatrix}
{{\alpha \cdot \text{diag}}{(\text{1})}} \\
{{\beta \cdot \text{diag}}{(\text{1})}}
\end{bmatrix} + {\mathbf{η}}} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{{2N} \times N}
```

so that $`{\mathbf{h} \cdot \mathbf{W}} = {{\alpha \cdot \mathbf{h}_{sep}} + {\beta \cdot \mathbf{h}_{mix}} + {\mathbf{h} \cdot {\mathbf{η}}}}`$. We use $`\alpha = 0.9`$,\
$`\beta = 0.1`$ and uniform random noise in the range of $`- 0.01`$ to $`0.01`$ for $`\mathbf{η}`$. $`N = 512`$ is the output dimension of the Conformer block. For some experiments, we additionally apply sequence-discriminative training. The model is initialized with the f-CE-fine-tuned parameters and trained further for half an epoch with the lattice-based state-level minimum Bayes risk (sMBR) loss \[[24](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib24)\]. Again, longer training did not improve the performance.

Unlike \[[10](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib10)\], the separator and recognizer are not jointly fine-tuned in this work. All AMs are trained on a single graphics processing unit (GPU) using the RETURNN toolkit \[[25](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib25)\]. For the baseline encoder, one full epoch of fine-tuning takes around $`17\ h`$ using the f-CE loss and $`120\ h`$ using the sMBR loss on an NVIDIA GeForce GTX 1080 Ti. When adding the mixture encoder, we use an NVIDIA A10 GPU and one full epoch takes around $`15\ h`$ or $`60\ h`$, respectively.

### 4.4 Recognition and scoring

For recognition, the separator from [Section 4.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#S4.SS2 "4.2 Speech separation model ‣ 4 Experimental setup ‣ Combining TF-GridNet and Mixture Encoder for Continuous Speech Separation for Meeting Transcription") is applied to separate the LibriCSS recordings into two overlap-free audio streams. As mentioned in [Section 4.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#S4.SS2 "4.2 Speech separation model ‣ 4 Experimental setup ‣ Combining TF-GridNet and Mixture Encoder for Continuous Speech Separation for Meeting Transcription"), this still allows processing an arbitrary number of speakers following the CSS approach assuming that overlap is limited to two speakers at any time. A simple energy-based VAD is used on the outputs to identify the speech segments. It uses a threshold and additional smoothing to overestimate the activity which avoids information loss for the ASR system \[[14](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib14)\]. Subsequently, we recognize the speech with the model from [Section 4.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#S4.SS3 "4.3 Acoustic model training ‣ 4 Experimental setup ‣ Combining TF-GridNet and Mixture Encoder for Continuous Speech Separation for Meeting Transcription") using the RASR toolkit \[[26](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib26)\]. Either the official LibriSpeech 4gram or a Transformer LM \[[27](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib27)\] is used. The recipes for AM training and recognition are available online¹¹1[https://github.com/rwth-i6/i6_experiments](https://github.com/rwth-i6/i6_experiments).

As the segments’ speaker labels are unknown, it is not possible to compute the regular word error rate (WER). After obtaining the hypotheses for each VAD segment, we concatenate the segments that belong to the same separation output channel. Finally, the concatenated hypotheses are scored with the MeetEval toolkit²²2[https://github.com/fgnt/meeteval](https://github.com/fgnt/meeteval) to compute the optimal reference combination WER (ORC WER) \[[28](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib28)\]. Future work could consider diarization to compute the concatenated minimum-permutation WER (cpWER), but it is out of scope here.

## 5 Results

### 5.1 Baseline Results

The results of the clean single-speaker LibriSpeech baseline model on the standard test sets are shown in [Table 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#S4.T3 "In 4.3 Acoustic model training ‣ 4 Experimental setup ‣ Combining TF-GridNet and Mixture Encoder for Continuous Speech Separation for Meeting Transcription"). Applying this model directly to the LibriCSS data yields the results marked as no fine-tuning in [Table 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#S4.T2 "In 4.3 Acoustic model training ‣ 4 Experimental setup ‣ Combining TF-GridNet and Mixture Encoder for Continuous Speech Separation for Meeting Transcription"). It is striking that TF-GridNet outperforms the BLSTM separator by a large margin, reducing the ORC WER by about a factor of 3. When the system is fine-tuned on the simulated meeting data, improvements can be observed for both separators.

### 5.2 Effect of Mixture Encoder

Adding the mixture encoder further shows a clear improvement for the BLSTM separator. However, for TF-GridNet, the mixture encoder has no advantage over the baseline encoder. In general, the additional mixture information can only help if the separation is imperfect. Although TF-GridNet does not provide a perfect separation, it still reaches a point where the effect of correcting separation artifacts using additional mixture information becomes negligible. This can be seen as a strong supporting argument for the already good performance. To be fair, it should be noted that, unlike TF-GridNet, the BLSTM separator does not use self-attention. It might therefore benefit more from the combination with the Conformer-based mixture encoder, although it is only slightly worse than a SepFormer model on reverberant data \[[19](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib19)\].

### 5.3 State-of-the-Art and Oracle Results

In [Table 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#S4.T3 "In 4.3 Acoustic model training ‣ 4 Experimental setup ‣ Combining TF-GridNet and Mixture Encoder for Continuous Speech Separation for Meeting Transcription"), we compare the best results from [Table 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#S4.T2 "In 4.3 Acoustic model training ‣ 4 Experimental setup ‣ Combining TF-GridNet and Mixture Encoder for Continuous Speech Separation for Meeting Transcription") to the literature and oracle experiments. Our results outperform the previous state-of-the-art using only LibriSpeech data \[[13](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib13)\] by 26% relative. Furthermore, they even outperform \[[14](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib14)\] which uses a WavLM \[[29](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib29)\] model that is 2.5 times larger than our AM and was (pre-)trained on about 100 times more data. Note that \[[13](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib13), [14](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib14)\] do not use an external LM.

In our setup, the TF-GridNet output streams are segmented by a simple VAD model (see [Section 4.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#S4.SS4 "4.4 Recognition and scoring ‣ 4 Experimental setup ‣ Combining TF-GridNet and Mixture Encoder for Continuous Speech Separation for Meeting Transcription")). To assess the limits of this separation, we utilize the boundary times from the LibriCSS annotation to segment the separated channels. The output signal for a segment is determined as the separated channel with the maximum signal-to-distortion ratio (SDR) to the clean LibriSpeech signal of the corresponding segment. [Table 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#S4.T3 "In 4.3 Acoustic model training ‣ 4 Experimental setup ‣ Combining TF-GridNet and Mixture Encoder for Continuous Speech Separation for Meeting Transcription") presents these results as “ours + oracle segmentation”. Note that the separator might still output parts of the segment on the other output stream in that case. As an upper bound, the “oracle signals and segmentation” performance is obtained by recognizing test-clean with the LibriSpeech baseline model and then concatenating and scoring the hypotheses in a way analog to LibriCSS. It is also possible to compute the regular WER in this case and it is identical to the ORC WER. We can observe that despite its strong performance, the TF-GridNet results are still clearly behind the use of the oracle clean data. Around a third of the gap can be explained by the oracle segmentation. The remaining difference is caused by the advantage of oracle separation and enhancement.

### 5.4 Frame-Wise Error Analysis

We aim to get a deeper understanding of the influence of separation errors on the recognition performance. In particular, the goal is to determine how often words from the cross speaker leak to the separated primary channel and end up in the ASR search space. While signal-level metrics struggle to handle silence appropriately and may measure errors that have no effect on the ASR performance \[[30](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib30)\], other metrics that operate purely on the transcription (e.g. using Levenshtein distances) do not guarantee temporal locality of detected errors and thus might e.g. blur leakage effects. In contrast, our proposed analysis based on Hamming distances of frame-wise word-level alignments allows for an accurate attribution of cross channel effects on the ASR search space and 1-best hypothesis.

In particular, we compare forced alignments of the oracle transcription on the primary or cross channel to alignments obtained by computing word lattices or 1-best hypotheses on the primary channel. For the oracle clean audio we compute the forced alignments with the LibriSpeech baseline ASR model and for the separated audio we use our model with TF-GridNet separator and f-CE-fine-tuned baseline encoder from [Table 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#S4.T2 "In 4.3 Acoustic model training ‣ 4 Experimental setup ‣ Combining TF-GridNet and Mixture Encoder for Continuous Speech Separation for Meeting Transcription"). The word boundaries obtained from the alignments can be used to assign a word to each frame \[[31](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib31)\], where the frame shift is $`10\ {ms}`$. The Hamming distance is then computed on a per-frame basis. To obtain the Hamming error rate (HER), the distance is divided by the number of frames in the signal. The coincidence rate (CIR) is the ratio of frames in which both sequences contain the same word. It is defined as $`\text{CIR} = {1 - \text{HER}}`$. Simply speaking, we measure where the hypothesis did not contain the word from the forced alignment with the HER. Meanwhile, the CIR indicates where the primary channel hypothesis contains the same word as the cross channel forced alignment, so potential leakage. For these frame-wise measures, all frames that contain silence in both sequences are excluded. [Figure 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#S5.F2 "In 5.4 Frame-Wise Error Analysis ‣ 5 Results ‣ Combining TF-GridNet and Mixture Encoder for Continuous Speech Separation for Meeting Transcription") depicts an example computation. In contrast, Levenshtein error rates ignore silence entirely and are normalized over the length of the reference transcription.

This suggests that the cross-talker is well suppressed and there is hardly any leakage that plays a role in the search space for the primary channel. Therefore, our results render methods that exploit oracle knowledge about the cross channel transcription during training (e.g. multi-output sequence discriminative training in \[[32](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.08454v2#bib.bib32)\]) in combination with separators of TF-GridNet’s quality as unpromising. ## 6 Conclusion

In this work, we integrate the TF-GridNet separator into a meeting separation and recognition system. Furthermore, the mixture encoder approach is extended to a setup for an arbitrary number of speakers and transferred to a Conformer-based neural architecture. Our best system reaches a new state-of-the-art on LibriCSS and even outperforms a much larger model using additional data. In addition, we study the effect of exploiting mixture speech encoding for acoustic modeling and show that, unlike previously used separators, TF-GridNet advances the separation quality to a level where the effect of additional mixture information vanishes. Finally, our analysis demonstrates that the cross-talker is suppressed sufficiently to have only a negligible effect on the primary channel’s search space. The main contributors to the remaining gap to the oracle performance are thus segmentation errors, reverberation as well as non-leakage-related separation artifacts.
