# Beyond Acoustic Prefixes: Persistent Access to Serialized Acoustic Memory for LLM-Based Multi-Talker Speech Recognition Thanks: Hao Shi, Yuan Gao and Tatsuya Kawahara are with the Graduate School of Informatics, Kyoto University, Kyoto, Japan (email: hshi@ieee.org, yuang@ieee.org, kawahara@i.kyoto-u.ac.jp).Thanks: Xugang Lu is with the National Institute of Information and Communications Technology, Japan (email: xugang.lu@nict.go.jp).Thanks: This work has been submitted to the IEEE for possible publication. ###### Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) provide strong linguistic priors for
serialized output training (SOT), yet LLM-based multi-talker ASR
degrades substantially as the number of overlapping talkers increases.
Conventional systems expose acoustic evidence primarily through an
initial projected mixture prefix, requiring the decoder to preserve and
recover talker-relevant information indirectly throughout autoregressive
generation. We first examine whether this limitation can be resolved by
enriching the static prefix using discrete connectionist temporal
classification (CTC) tokens, hybrid token–acoustic prompts, and
continuous talker-specific representations. The results suggest that
acoustic content alone does not fully address the conditioning
bottleneck. We therefore extend onset-based serialization from the
output target to the acoustic-conditioning pathway and introduce
_persistent decoder-side access to SOT-aligned serialized acoustic
memory_. Talker-specific representations are organized in
utterance-onset order and retained as external acoustic memory, while
the conventional mixture prefix provides a complementary
global-conditioning path. LLM layers query this memory throughout
generation through gated residual cross-attention. We further introduce
a second adaptation stage that jointly applies low-rank updates to the
acoustic-retrieval pathway and selected LLM self-attention projections.
Experiments on LibriMix show consistent improvements over static-prefix
prompting. These results indicate that effective LLM-based multi-talker
ASR depends not only on providing richer acoustic representations, but
also on maintaining persistent access to acoustic evidence structured
according to the serialized output.

## I Introduction

Multi-talker automatic speech recognition (ASR) aims to transcribe
speech when multiple talkers speak simultaneously \[1, 2, 3, 4, 5\].
Compared with single-talker ASR \[6, 7, 8, 9\], it must recover
linguistic content from overlapping voices while resolving which
acoustic evidence belongs to each talker \[10, 11, 1\]. Multi-talker
recognition can be approached with an explicit speech-separation
frontend \[12, 13, 14\], or by jointly modeling separation, diarization,
and recognition \[15, 16, 17\]. Among joint approaches,
permutation-invariant training (PIT) \[18, 19\] and serialized output
training (SOT) \[20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25\] are two representative
paradigms. PIT produces multiple output streams and resolves
label-permutation ambiguity by minimizing over possible assignments, but
requires predefined branches whose assignment and decoding costs grow
with the number of talkers. SOT instead orders talker transcriptions
according to a serialization rule and concatenates them with
speaker-change tokens, avoiding multiple autoregressive output streams
and representing different output segments within a single
sequence \[20\]. However, recognition, talker assignment, and output
ordering must then be solved jointly, making SOT increasingly difficult
as overlap and mixture complexity increase \[20, 26, 27\].

Recent work has explored using large language models (LLMs) as ASR
decoders \[28, 29\]. In multi-talker settings, LLMs have been
incorporated through acoustic-prefix conditioning, task instructions,
contextual modeling, and diarization-conditioned grounding \[30, 31, 32,
33, 34, 35, 36\]. A typical LLM-based ASR system consists of a speech
encoder, a temporal-reduction module, a modality projector, and a
pretrained LLM decoder. The encoded speech sequence is projected into
the LLM embedding space and prepended to the text tokens as an acoustic
prefix for autoregressive generation \[31, 32, 35\]. This formulation is
attractive because the LLM supplies a strong linguistic prior that can
help resolve locally ambiguous acoustic observations.

Nevertheless, the main limitation of LLM-based SOT is not that it is
uniformly ineffective, but that it scales poorly as the number of
talkers increases. As observed in our previous work \[30\], LLM-based
SOT is competitive on two-talker mixtures but degrades substantially on
three-talker mixtures, and increasing the language-decoder capacity does
not consistently remove this degradation. Although three-or-more-way
overlap is less frequent than two-talker overlap, it occurs in real
multi-party meetings and exposes a difficult failure case for systems
designed around two-speaker concurrency assumptions \[37, 38\]. When
several speech streams admit linguistically plausible continuations,
linguistic compatibility alone cannot determine which talker produced a
particular token or token span. The decoder must repeatedly associate
its serialized output with fine-grained evidence from the corresponding
acoustic stream.

We attribute this talker-count scaling problem to two mismatches between
conventional acoustic conditioning and serialized generation. The first
is a _structural mismatch_. SOT organizes the textual target into
onset-ordered talker segments, whereas the conventional acoustic prefix
remains a mixture-level representation that does not explicitly expose
the corresponding talker structure. The decoder must therefore infer the
relationship between a talker-serialized output and structurally
entangled acoustic conditioning. The second is an _access-path
mismatch_. Although talker-specific acoustic evidence may be needed
throughout generation, both acoustic retrieval and textual-context
modeling are mediated by the same self-attention pathway over the prefix
and the growing text history. This does not imply that prefix
representations become mathematically inaccessible after prefill: their
key–value states remain available to later self-attention operations.
Rather, the mixture prefix provides neither an explicitly talker-aligned
organization nor a dedicated pathway for retrieving acoustic evidence
during serialized generation. Both mismatches become more consequential
as the number of possible token-to-talker assignments increases.

Before changing the conditioning interface, we first ask whether the
limitation of prefix-conditioned SOT can be explained simply by
insufficient acoustic content. Building on our previous serialized
output prompting framework \[30\], we revisit three forms of guidance
derived from serialized connectionist temporal classification (CTC)
\[39, 30\]: (1) discrete CTC transcription tokens; (2) hybrid prompts
that combine CTC tokens with projected acoustic representations; and (3)
continuous prompts constructed from talker-specific CTC hidden
representations. These variants vary the form and richness of the
information supplied to the decoder while retaining static-prefix
delivery. Acoustic-enriched prompts substantially outperform discrete
CTC hypotheses, but their gains over conventional SOT remain modest
under three-talker conditions. This result suggests that richer acoustic
content is helpful but may remain insufficient when it is delivered only
through the initial prefix.

Our central hypothesis is therefore that the limitation of
prefix-conditioned SOT is determined not only by how much acoustic
information is provided, but also by how that information is structured
and accessed. We therefore hypothesize that talker-specific
representations organized according to the SOT order provide more
effective acoustic conditioning when they remain directly accessible
throughout autoregressive decoding. We extend onset-based serialization
from the textual output to the acoustic-conditioning pathway. To
instantiate this idea, we retain the continuous talker-specific
representations in utterance-onset order and expose them as _serialized
acoustic memory_. The organization of this memory follows the utterance
order of the SOT target, establishing order-level correspondence between
the acoustic streams and the serialized output. Because the memory
remains outside the autoregressive token sequence, decoder layers can
query it throughout generation. We refer to this combination of
SOT-aligned serialized memory and a dedicated decoder-side access
pathway throughout generation as _SOT-aligned persistent acoustic
access_.

The conventional projected mixture prefix is retained, yielding a
dual-path conditioning interface: the prefix supplies global mixture
context through self-attention, while decoder layers in the LLM retrieve
talker-specific evidence from the serialized acoustic memory through
cross-attention throughout generation. A learnable gate controls the
magnitude of the retrieved residual update. Although external modality
memory, cross-attention retrieval, and gated or zero-initialized
adapters have substantial precedents in multimodal LLMs \[40, 41, 42\],
we use these mechanisms to realize an SOT-specific conditioning
principle: preserving onset-ordered acoustic evidence as an external
memory that remains accessible during serialized generation. The
contribution therefore lies in this serialization-aligned
persistent-memory formulation and its controlled validation, rather than
in cross-attention or gating alone. Prefix conditioning and
external-memory retrieval impose different acoustic-access biases rather
than different theoretical limits on decoder expressiveness.

To validate the proposed formulation and determine which of its
properties are essential, we organize the evaluation around three groups
of controlled comparisons. First, a fully crossed comparison varies the
memory source—mixture-level or onset-ordered talker-specific
representations—and the residual integration mechanism—ungated addition
or learnable gating—under the same initialization and optimization
budget. Second, we compare a dual-static-prefix system with a
persistent-memory system using the same serialized acoustic source,
projector, and temporal resolution. A separate prefill-only control uses
the same cross-attention module as the persistent system but disables
memory access during autoregressive generation, thereby isolating the
contribution of continued decoder-time retrieval from additional module
capacity. Third, we remove the conventional mixture prefix while
retaining access to the serialized talker memory, thereby isolating the
contribution of the complementary dual-path design. Together, these
comparisons examine the respective contributions of talker-aligned
memory structure, persistent access, residual gating, and dual-path
conditioning.

For the complete system, we further propose a two-stage adaptation
strategy. The first stage learns the gated acoustic-memory pathway while
preserving the pretrained LLM weights. The resulting gated model
initializes a second stage in which low-rank adaptation (LoRA) is
applied jointly to the trained cross-attention projections and selected
LLM self-attention projections \[43\]. This second stage is treated as a
downstream optimization of an already established memory interface and
is evaluated separately from the controlled architectural comparisons,
so that its effect is not conflated with memory structure, access
interface, or additional training budget.

## II Preliminaries

### II-A LLM-Based Serialized Multi-Talker ASR

Given an input waveform $`\mathbf{y}`$, a speech encoder,
temporal-reduction module, and modality projector produce a
mixture-level acoustic prefix

```math
\mathbf{H}_{e}=\mathrm{Enc}(\mathbf{y}),\qquad\mathbf{H}_{p}=\mathrm{Projector}_{\mathrm{mix}}\left(\mathrm{Down}(\mathbf{H}_{e})\right), \tag{1}
```

where $`\mathbf{H}_{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{T_{e}\times D_{e}}`$ and
$`\mathbf{H}_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{T_{p}\times D_{\mathrm{model}}}`$. Here,
$`T_{e}`$ and $`D_{e}`$ denote the encoder-sequence length and encoder
hidden dimension, respectively; $`T_{p}`$ is the acoustic-prefix length
after temporal reduction; and $`D_{\mathrm{model}}`$ is the LLM hidden
dimension. $`\mathrm{Down}(\cdot)`$ denotes temporal reduction, and
$`\mathrm{Projector}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\cdot)`$ is a learned projection
into the LLM hidden space. The conventional LLM input is

```math
\mathbf{X}=\operatorname{Concat}\left(\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{inst}},\mathbf{H}_{p},\mathbf{E}_{t}\right), \tag{2}
```

where $`\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{inst}}`$ is an optional instruction
embedding and $`\mathbf{E}_{t}`$ contains the right-shifted text
embeddings. Throughout the paper, $`\operatorname{Concat}(\cdot)`$
denotes concatenation along the sequence (time) axis; all concatenated
representations therefore share the feature dimension
$`D_{\mathrm{model}}`$. For example, for the instruction-tuned LLM, we
use the instruction “TRANSCRIBE THE PROVIDED AUDIO INTO ACCURATE TEXT.”
Its embedding sequence is denoted by $`\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{inst}}`$ and
prepended to the acoustic input. For the base LLM variants,
$`\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{inst}}`$ is omitted. We call $`\mathbf{H}_{p}`$ a
_static prefix_ because it is placed in the autoregressive input rather
than retained behind a separate decoder-side memory interface; its
cached key–value states nevertheless remain accessible to later
self-attention operations. The encoder and LLM can be initialized from
pretrained models \[44, 45, 46\], and the LLM can be adapted using
parameter-efficient methods such as LoRA \[43\].

SOT converts multi-talker recognition into single-sequence
generation \[20\]. For $`K`$ talkers ordered by utterance onset, with
transcription $`\mathbf{t}_{k}`$ for the $`k`$-th talker in that order,
the target is

```math
\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{sot}}=\operatorname{Concat}\left(\mathbf{t}_{1},\langle\mathrm{sc}\rangle,\mathbf{t}_{2},\ldots,\langle\mathrm{sc}\rangle,\mathbf{t}_{K}\right), \tag{3}
```

where $`\langle\mathrm{sc}\rangle`$ is a single speaker-change token
type reused between consecutive talker segments and therefore occurs
$`K-1`$ times in a $`K`$-talker target. The token marks segment
boundaries rather than speaker identities; the permutation ambiguity is
resolved by ordering the segments according to utterance onset. The LLM
is trained autoregressively using

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SOT}}=\mathrm{CE}\left(\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{sot}},\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{sot}}\right), \tag{4}
```

where $`\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{sot}}`$ denotes the LLM output logits and
$`\mathrm{CE}(\cdot,\cdot)`$ denotes token-level cross-entropy between
the serialized target and the corresponding autoregressive output
distribution.

### II-B Serialized CTC and Previous Static Prompting

Our previous EncSep framework \[39\] introduced an encoder-side
separator that produces $`K`$ talker-specific streams from the shared
acoustic representation:

```math
\left\{\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{sep}}^{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{K}=\mathrm{Separator}\left(\mathbf{H}_{e}\right). \tag{5}
```

The separator consisted of a long short-term memory (LSTM) network
followed by layer normalization and talker-dependent projections; its
exact configuration is reported in Section V-C. Each stream is mapped to
CTC logits
$`\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{ctc}}^{k}=\mathrm{CTCHead}(\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{sep}}^{k})`$
and supervised by the transcription with the same onset-order index:

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SerCTC}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathrm{CTC}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{ctc}}^{k},\mathbf{t}_{k}\right). \tag{6}
```

Here, $`\mathrm{CTC}(\cdot,\cdot)`$ denotes the connectionist temporal
classification loss between a talker-specific logit sequence and its
onset-ordered transcription. This supervision aligns the separator
branch index with the SOT output order.

Our SOP framework \[30\] converted the greedy serialized-CTC hypotheses
into token embeddings and concatenated them with the downsampled mixture
prefix before autoregressive decoding. Specifically, if
$`\bar{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathrm{ser}}`$ denotes the onset-ordered
concatenation of the talker-level CTC hypotheses, SOP used

```math
\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{sop}}=\operatorname{Concat}\left(\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{inst}},\mathrm{Embedding}(\bar{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathrm{ser}}),\mathbf{H}_{p},\mathbf{E}_{t}\right). \tag{7}
```

Thus, EncSep learned onset-ordered talker representations, whereas SOP
exposed their decoded hypotheses to the LLM only through the initial
input sequence. The next section retains these essential components but
compares several CTC-derived static representations more directly.

## III Characterizing Static Prefix Conditioning with CTC-Derived Cues

Before introducing decoder-side memory, we examine how discrete and
continuous serialized-CTC cues behave when delivered only through the
initial LLM input. These variants characterize different static
information sources but are not, by themselves, single-factor interface
controls because they differ in whether the conventional mixture prefix
is retained. We therefore also construct a matched dual-prefix baseline
for comparison with persistent access.

### III-A Shared Talker-Specific Representations

Unlike the SOP system, which attached its separator to an intermediate
downsampling representation, the present systems apply the separator in
Eq. (5) directly to $`\mathbf{H}_{e}`$ while retaining the conventional
mixture-prefix pathway in Eq. (1). Greedy CTC decoding is performed
independently for each onset-ordered branch, and the resulting
hypotheses are concatenated as

```math
\bar{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathrm{ser}}=\operatorname{Concat}\left(\mathrm{GreedyCTC}(\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{ctc}}^{1}),\ldots,\mathrm{GreedyCTC}(\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{ctc}}^{K})\right). \tag{8}
```

Repeated symbols and CTC blanks are removed by greedy decoding. No
speaker-change token is inserted between the talker-level hypotheses
before concatenation. The corresponding continuous streams are
concatenated in the same order:

```math
\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{ser}}=\operatorname{Concat}\left(\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{sep}}^{1},\ldots,\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{sep}}^{K}\right). \tag{9}
```

Unless otherwise stated, the static and persistent systems use separator
and serialized-CTC components initialized from the same CTC-stage
checkpoint.

### III-B CTC-Derived Static Prefixes

We consider three static variants derived from Eqs. (8) and (9):

```math
\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{tok}} \displaystyle=\mathrm{Embedding}(\bar{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathrm{ser}}), \tag{10}
```

```math
\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{hyb}} \displaystyle=\operatorname{Concat}(\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{tok}},\mathbf{H}_{p}),
```

```math
\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{aco}} \displaystyle=\mathrm{Projector}_{\mathrm{sep}}(\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{ser}}).
```

Here, $`\mathrm{Embedding}(\cdot)`$ denotes the LLM token-embedding
lookup, and $`\mathrm{Projector}_{\mathrm{sep}}(\cdot)`$ is a learned
mapping from the separator feature dimension to $`D_{\mathrm{model}}`$.
TokenPrompt uses $`\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{tok}}`$ and therefore supplies
talker-ordered lexical hypotheses without frame-level acoustic states.
HybridPrompt uses $`\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{hyb}}`$ and retains the
conventional mixture prefix in addition to those hypotheses.
AcousticPrompt uses $`\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{aco}}`$, preserving continuous
talker-specific information before the CTC output projection, but
replaces rather than augments $`\mathbf{H}_{p}`$. Their inputs can be
written compactly as

```math
\mathbf{X}_{v}=\operatorname{Concat}\left(\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{inst}},\mathbf{E}_{v},\mathbf{E}_{t}\right),\quad\mathbf{E}_{v}\in\left\{\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{tok}},\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{hyb}},\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{aco}}\right\}. \tag{11}
```

All three variants are optimized using the SOT objective in Eq. (4).

### III-C Matched Dual-Prefix Baseline

AcousticPrompt is not an interface-matched control for the proposed
system because it removes $`\mathbf{H}_{p}`$ and uses a separate static
projection pathway. We therefore construct a dual-prefix baseline
containing both the conventional mixture prefix and the same serialized
representation used by its persistent counterpart:

```math
\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{match}}=\mathrm{Projector}_{\mathrm{match}}\left(\mathrm{Reduce}_{\mathrm{match}}(\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{ser}})\right). \tag{12}
```

The matched static and persistent systems share this acoustic source,
temporal reduction, and projector. Here,
$`\mathrm{Reduce}_{\mathrm{match}}(\cdot)`$ denotes the common matched
temporal-reduction operator, and
$`\mathrm{Projector}_{\mathrm{match}}(\cdot)`$ denotes the common
learned projection into the LLM hidden space. The static system
concatenates it into the LLM input:

```math
\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{dual}}=\operatorname{Concat}\left(\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{inst}},\mathbf{H}_{p},\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{match}},\mathbf{E}_{t}\right), \tag{13}
```

whereas the matched persistent system retains
$`\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{match}}`$ outside the token sequence and supplies
it through decoder-side cross-attention.

## IV Persistent Access to Serialized Acoustic Memory

The preceding static variants represent all acoustic and CTC-derived
evidence within the autoregressive input sequence. Their acoustic states
remain available through self-attention, but share the same attention
pathway as the textual context. We introduce persistent access to
serialized acoustic memory to study a different access pattern:
continuous onset-ordered talker representations are retained outside the
autoregressive token stream and supplied to selected decoder layers
through a dedicated cross-attention pathway. This changes where the
acoustic evidence is stored and how decoder states access it during
generation. The main system retains the conventional mixture prefix and
additionally exposes serialized talker representations as external
memory. This dual-path construction is evaluated against matched static,
prefill-only, and memory-only controls so that the effects of acoustic
source, continued access, and mixture-prefix conditioning can be
separated. We further introduce a two-stage acoustic adaptation strategy
tailored to this persistent-memory interface: a decoder-side retrieval
pathway is learned first, after which low-rank updates are introduced
into the trained cross-attention projections and selected LLM
self-attention projections.

### IV-A Serialized Acoustic Memory

The proposed method uses the continuous sequence
$`\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{ser}}`$ in Eq. (9) rather than decoded CTC
hypotheses. A memory projector maps the serialized talker representation
to the LLM hidden dimension:

```math
\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{ser}}=\mathrm{Projector}_{\mathrm{mem}}\left(\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{ser}}\right). \tag{14}
```

Here,
$`\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{ser}}\in\mathbb{R}^{T_{m}\times D_{\mathrm{model}}}`$
is the serialized acoustic memory, where $`T_{m}`$ is its length. The
individual streams are arranged according to the same onset rule used
for the SOT target, so the order of the memory blocks follows the order
of the output segments.

The conventional decoder input of the main system remains
$`\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{main}}=\mathbf{X}`$, as defined in Eq. (2), while
$`\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{ser}}`$ is provided separately to the adapted
decoder layers. Thus, $`\mathbf{H}_{p}`$ and
$`\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{ser}}`$ provide two complementary conditioning
paths: a mixture-level input prefix and a decoder-side talker-structured
memory.

For the memory-source control, we also construct mixture-level memory
from the encoder sequence:

```math
\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{mix}}=\mathrm{Projector}_{\mathrm{mem}}^{\mathrm{mix}}\left(\mathbf{H}_{e}\right). \tag{15}
```

The mixture and serialized memory projectors use the same output
dimension and matched projector architecture. This permits the memory
source to be varied without changing the decoder-side cross-attention
formulation.

### IV-B Decoder-Side Acoustic Retrieval

We insert a lightweight cross-attention sublayer after the
self-attention sublayer of each LLM layer. Let
$`\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{L_{t}\times D_{\mathrm{model}}}`$
denote the hidden states after self-attention in layer $`\ell`$, where
$`L_{t}`$ is the current decoder-sequence length. For an acoustic memory
$`\mathbf{M}_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{T_{s}\times D_{\mathrm{model}}}`$, with
$`s\in\{\mathrm{mix},\mathrm{ser}\}`$ and memory length $`T_{s}`$, the
query, key, and value representations are computed as

```math
\begin{split}\mathbf{Q}^{(\ell)}=\operatorname{LayerNorm}_{\mathrm{in}}^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)})\mathbf{W}_{q}^{(\ell)},\\
[\mathbf{K}^{(\ell)}\;\mathbf{V}^{(\ell)}]=\mathbf{M}_{s}[\mathbf{W}_{k}^{(\ell)}\;\mathbf{W}_{v}^{(\ell)}].\end{split} \tag{16}
```

Here, $`\operatorname{LayerNorm}_{\mathrm{in}}^{(\ell)}(\cdot)`$ is the
input LayerNorm of the cross-attention sublayer. Let $`D_{a}`$ denote
the total cross-attention projection dimension. The learned matrices
$`\mathbf{W}_{q}^{(\ell)},\mathbf{W}_{k}^{(\ell)},\mathbf{W}_{v}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{\mathrm{model}}\times D_{a}}`$
project decoder or memory states to queries, keys, and values,
respectively, and
$`\mathbf{W}_{o}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{a}\times D_{\mathrm{model}}}`$
projects the retrieved context back to the LLM hidden dimension.

For multi-head attention, let $`d_{h}`$ denote the dimensionality of
each attention head. The complete retrieved acoustic update is

```math
\mathbf{U}^{(\ell)}=\mathrm{Softmax}\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}^{(\ell)}\mathbf{K}^{(\ell)\top}}{\sqrt{d_{h}}}+\mathbf{S}\right)\mathbf{V}^{(\ell)}\mathbf{W}_{o}^{(\ell)}, \tag{17}
```

where $`\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{L_{t}\times T_{s}}`$ is a masking bias
that excludes padded memory positions and is broadcast across attention
heads. The “Delta Compute” block in Fig. 1 denotes the computation of
this retrieved acoustic update $`\mathbf{U}^{(\ell)}`$.

We consider stacked and gated residual interfaces under the unified
formulation

```math
\mathbf{H}_{r}^{(\ell)}=\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)}+\eta_{\ell,r}\mathbf{U}^{(\ell)},\eta_{\ell,\mathrm{stack}}=1,\eta_{\ell,\mathrm{gate}}=\sigma(\gamma_{\ell}), \tag{18}
```

where $`r\in\{\mathrm{stack},\mathrm{gate}\}`$, $`\gamma_{\ell}`$ is a
trainable scalar gate logit, and $`\sigma(\cdot)`$ denotes the sigmoid
function. Thus, the stacked interface directly adds the retrieved
update, whereas the gated interface explicitly controls its magnitude.
The resulting states are then passed through the original feed-forward
sublayer of the LLM.

### IV-C Two-Stage Acoustic Adaptation

We introduce a two-stage acoustic adaptation strategy tailored to the
persistent-memory interface. The two stages refer specifically to
decoder-side adaptation after the separator and serialized CTC branches
have been initialized.

#### Stage 1: acoustic-memory interface

In the first stage, the dense parameters of the pretrained LLM are kept
fixed while the memory projector and decoder-side cross-attention
parameters are optimized. For a gated configuration, the gate logits are
optimized in the same stage. This produces a trained cross-attention
pathway without modifying the dense pretrained LLM weights.

#### Stage 2: joint low-rank refinement

The trained Stage 1 cross-attention model initializes the second stage.
All dense projection matrices remain fixed, and LoRA modules are
introduced into selected LLM self-attention projections and the query,
key, value, and output projections of cross-attention. Let
$`\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{sa}}^{(\ell)}`$ denote the selected
self-attention matrices and let

```math
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{ca}}^{(\ell)}=\left\{\mathbf{W}_{q}^{(\ell)},\mathbf{W}_{k}^{(\ell)},\mathbf{W}_{v}^{(\ell)},\mathbf{W}_{o}^{(\ell)}\right\} \tag{19}
```

denote the cross-attention matrices. For each
$`\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)}\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{sa}}^{(\ell)}\cup\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{ca}}^{(\ell)}`$,
we introduce

```math
\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}^{(\ell)}=\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)}+\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{LoRA}}}{r}\mathbf{B}^{(\ell)}\mathbf{A}^{(\ell)}. \tag{20}
```

For a projection
$`\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}`$,
the trainable factors satisfy
$`\mathbf{A}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}`$ and
$`\mathbf{B}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r}`$. The
rank $`r`$ controls the update capacity, and $`\alpha_{\mathrm{LoRA}}`$
controls its scale. This stage introduces trainable low-rank
perturbations on both sides of the decoder-side retrieval interface
without introducing another source of acoustic information. Its
incremental contribution is evaluated against the corresponding pre-LoRA
Stage 1 model and is not used to infer the effects of memory structure
or access pattern.

After training, the low-rank updates are merged into their corresponding
projection matrices:

```math
\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)}\leftarrow\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}^{(\ell)}. \tag{21}
```

Consequently, LoRA introduces no additional inference-time branches. The
cross-attention sublayers remain part of the decoder because they
implement access to the external acoustic memory.

### IV-D Training Objectives

Before the two-stage acoustic adaptation, the encoder and SOT decoder
were first trained using $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SOT}}`$. The SOT pathway
was then frozen while the separator and talker-specific CTC branches
were initialized using $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SerCTC}}`$ in Eq. (6).
After this initialization, the trained encoder, separator, and
CTC-related components were frozen and used to construct the serialized
acoustic memory. Both Stage 1 and Stage 2 are optimized exclusively with
the SOT objective:

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Stage\text{-}1}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Stage\text{-}2}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SOT}}. \tag{22}
```

Under this common objective, Stage 1 updates the memory projector and
cross-attention parameters while keeping the dense pretrained LLM fixed.
Stage 2 starts from the trained Stage 1 model, keeps its dense
parameters fixed, and optimizes only the newly introduced low-rank
updates in the selected self-attention and cross-attention projections.

## V Experimental Setup

### V-A Datasets and Evaluation Protocol

We conducted experiments on LibriMix \[47\], a widely used
overlapped-speech benchmark constructed from LibriSpeech \[48\]. We
evaluated two- and three-talker recognition using Libri2Mix and
Libri3Mix, respectively. Source onset times were controlled using offset
files. For Libri2Mix, we adopted the offsets from the ESPnet SOT
recipe¹¹ 1
https://github.com/espnet/espnet/tree/master/egs2/librimix/sot_asr1; for
Libri3Mix, we created additional configurations to obtain diverse
three-talker onset patterns. The Libri3Mix offset configurations are
publicly available in our released source code²² 2
https://github.com/hshi-speech/LibriMix-repo/tree/main/Libri3Mix_offset.
These offsets introduced delays between source utterances rather than
prescribing a fixed overlap ratio. Using speaker-activity labels at a
20-ms frame resolution, we computed overlap as the number of frames with
at least two active speakers divided by the number of frames with at
least one active speaker. Frame counts were aggregated across each test
set before division. The resulting overlap ratios were approximately
40.2% and 55.2% for the offset-controlled Libri2Mix and Libri3Mix test
sets, respectively. For Libri3Mix, three speakers were simultaneously
active in approximately 17.6% of speech-active frames. Models in Exp. 1
to Exp. 47 were trained separately on Libri2Mix Clean, Libri2Mix Noisy,
Libri3Mix Clean, and Libri3Mix Noisy, respectively, and each was
evaluated on the development and test sets matching its training
condition.

For cross-domain evaluation, we additionally used LibriCSS \[49\], a
far-field multi-talker corpus recorded in a real meeting room. Our
models assumed a known talker count and used separate encoder-side
configurations for two- and three-talker recognition. We therefore
derived two fixed-count evaluation sets from the official LibriCSS
release; the segmentation script and complete clip and reference lists
are publicly available³³ 3
https://github.com/hshi-speech/grounded-multitalker-asr/tree/main/libricss.
For each session, the utterances in the official meeting transcription
were sorted by start time and independently partitioned into
consecutive, non-overlapping blocks of two or three utterances. Audio
was extracted from channel 0 of the seven-channel array over this
interval without additional padding. This procedure produced 1,754
two-talker clips containing 62,579 reference words and 508 three-talker
clips containing 23,719 reference words. The two- and three-talker sets
contained 5.7 and 2.1 hours of audio, respectively, with mean
(median/90th-percentile) clip durations of 11.8 (11.5/18.0) s and 14.7
(15.2/19.2) s. Both sets covered the six official LibriCSS conditions:
non-overlapping speech with short or long inter-utterance silence (0S
and 0L) and nominal overlap ratios of 10%, 20%, 30%, and 40%. Each
reference was formed by lowercasing the official utterance
transcriptions and concatenating them with the speaker-change token in
utterance-onset order. We applied the corresponding Libri2Mix- or
Libri3Mix-trained model without adaptation to LibriCSS and computed WER
directly against this onset-serialized SOT reference, without
permutation search or oracle speaker reassignment.

We reported word error rate (WER), where lower values indicated better
recognition performance. For each mixture, the reference transcriptions
were concatenated according to utterance onset order using the same
serialized target construction as in SOT training. The generated
serialized transcription was scored directly against this onset-ordered
reference without permutation search or oracle speaker reassignment.
Consequently, the reported WER reflected both recognition accuracy and
consistency with the prescribed serialization order. Speaker-change
tokens were used only as serialization delimiters and were excluded from
word-level scoring.

### V-B Potential Text Contamination

LibriMix and LibriCSS were derived from LibriSpeech utterances whose
transcripts originated from public-domain audiobooks. We therefore could
not completely rule out overlap between their textual content and the
pretraining corpora of LLaMA-family models, and the absolute WERs of
LLM-based systems on these benchmarks should be interpreted with this
caveat. Our principal comparisons nevertheless used the same pretrained
LLM backbone and the same evaluation data, and differed only in the
acoustic source, conditioning interface, or adaptation strategy under
study. Moreover, the most challenging noisy and three-talker conditions
required acoustic stream assignment and serialized ordering in addition
to predicting linguistically plausible text.

### V-C Model Configurations

#### Speech encoder and LLM backbones

All systems were implemented using Hugging Face Transformers. The input
consisted of 16-kHz waveforms normalized using the Wav2Vec2 feature
extractor. We used the 24-layer WavLM-Large model as the speech encoder.
Its output representation had a dimension of 1,024 and a frame rate of
50 Hz, corresponding to one frame every 20 ms. For the conventional
mixture-prefix pathway, three stride-2 convolutional adapter layers
reduced the temporal resolution by a factor of eight to approximately
6.25 Hz. The resulting sequence was mapped to the LLM hidden dimension
by the modality projector. We evaluated LLaMA decoders at three scales:
LLaMA-3.2-1B, LLaMA-3.2-3B, and LLaMA-3.1-8B, together with their
instruction-tuned counterparts. For the instruction-tuned models, we
prepended the fixed instruction “TRANSCRIBE THE PROVIDED AUDIO INTO
ACCURATE TEXT,” whose embedding sequence was denoted by
$`\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{inst}}`$.

#### Encoder-side separator and serialized CTC

Following EncSep \[39\], the separator first applied a linear projection
from 1,024 to 796 dimensions, followed by a rectified linear unit and
layer normalization. The projected sequence was then processed by a
two-layer LSTM with a hidden size of 796 and dropout of 0.2. A
subsequent layer normalization and $`K`$ talker-specific branches mapped
the LSTM output back to 1,024 dimensions. Each branch consisted of two
linear transformations, $`796\rightarrow 796\rightarrow 1{,}024`$, with
a rectified linear unit and layer normalization. Here, $`K`$ was fixed
to two for Libri2Mix and three for Libri3Mix. Each separated stream was
connected to an independent CTC head. The CTC head was a linear
transformation from 1,024 dimensions to 128,265 output classes,
including the additional CTC blank symbol. The remaining classes
followed the non-blank vocabulary of the corresponding LLM tokenizer.

#### Serialized acoustic memory and gated decoder-side access

The talker-specific representations were concatenated in utterance-onset
order without temporal downsampling. Consequently, the serialized
acoustic memory retained the native 50-Hz WavLM resolution and had a
length of $`T_{m}=KT_{e}`$, where $`T_{e}`$ denotes the number of WavLM
frames. Each decoder layer contained one independent single-head
cross-attention adapter placed after the self-attention residual and
before the feed-forward network. Each adapter used an attention
bottleneck dimension of $`D_{a}=512`$. The decoder hidden states were
mapped to queries, while the 1,024-dimensional memory states were mapped
to keys and values. The retrieved context was then projected back to the
LLM hidden dimension. The adapter additionally contained input and
output layer-normalization modules and used an attention dropout of 0.0.
Padded memory positions were excluded using an attention mask. For gated
access, the residual contribution was scaled by
$`g_{\ell}=\sigma(\gamma_{\ell})`$, with one scalar gate for each
decoder layer.

### V-D Initialization and Training Pipeline

#### SOT backbone training

We first trained an LLM-based SOT model using only
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SOT}}`$. The WavLM encoder, temporal-reduction
module, and modality projector were trainable, whereas the dense LLM
parameters remained frozen. LoRA was applied to the query, key, value,
and output projections of self-attention in every LLM layer, with rank
$`r=16`$, scaling factor $`\alpha_{\mathrm{LoRA}}=32`$, and dropout 0.1.
The input embeddings and LM output head were also updated. The LoRA
updates were merged into the corresponding self-attention projection
matrices after this phase. We extended the tokenizer with task-specific
special tokens and updated the corresponding embedding rows. For the
base LLaMA models, we added the speaker-change token
$`\langle\mathrm{sc}\rangle`$. The instruction-tuned models additionally
used $`\langle\mathrm{pad}\rangle`$,
$`\langle\mathrm{bos\_prompt}\rangle`$,
$`\langle\mathrm{eos\_prompt}\rangle`$,
$`\langle\mathrm{bos\_speech}\rangle`$,
$`\langle\mathrm{eos\_speech}\rangle`$,
$`\langle\mathrm{bos\_response}\rangle`$, and
$`\langle\mathrm{eos\_response}\rangle`$ to delimit the instruction,
speech, and response segments.

#### Serialized-CTC initialization

We initialized the WavLM encoder and LLM decoder from the SOT-backbone
checkpoint trained in phase (a) (Section V-D–a), rather than directly
from the public pretrained checkpoints, and attached the separator and
$`K`$ talker-specific CTC heads. The existing SOT pathway, including
WavLM and the LLM decoder, was frozen, and only the separator and CTC
heads were updated. This phase was optimized exclusively using
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SerCTC}}`$ in Eq. (6); no SOT loss was applied.

#### Stage 1: residual acoustic access

Starting from the serialized-CTC checkpoint, we first trained the
cross-attention adapters using the direct stacked residual interface.
The speech encoder, mixture-prefix pathway, separator, CTC heads, and
dense LLM parameters were frozen. Only the decoder-side cross-attention
adapters were updated using $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SOT}}`$; the
serialized-CTC loss was not used. For the sequential gated system, the
trained stacked-access checkpoint was then used to initialize the gated
interface. The cross-attention parameters and layer-wise gate logits
were updated using only $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SOT}}`$, while the
encoder-side components and dense LLM parameters remained frozen. Each
gate logit was initialized as $`\gamma_{\ell}=-2`$, corresponding to
$`g_{\ell}\approx 0.12`$.

#### Stage 2: joint low-rank refinement

Stage 2 was initialized from the corresponding trained Stage 1
checkpoint. All pre-existing parameters were frozen, and new LoRA
modules were applied to the query, key, value, and output projections of
the cross-attention adapters. The joint-refinement variants additionally
applied LoRA to the query, key, value, and output projections of LLM
self-attention. The selected configuration used a maximum rank of
$`r=8`$, scaling factor $`\alpha_{\mathrm{LoRA}}=4`$, LoRA dropout of
0.05, and an initial rank logit of 2.0. Only the newly introduced
low-rank parameters were updated, using $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SOT}}`$
without a serialized-CTC loss.

### V-E Optimization Details

Training used distributed data parallelism on eight GPUs and
full-precision floating-point computation. Unless otherwise specified,
we used AdamW with $`\beta_{1}=0.9`$, $`\beta_{2}=0.999`$,
$`\epsilon=10^{-8}`$, and zero weight decay. The learning rate was
linearly warmed up to $`3\times 10^{-5}`$ over the first 400
optimization steps. It was then linearly decayed according to the
configured scheduler horizon. Gradients were clipped to a maximum norm
of 1.0, no gradient accumulation was used, and gradient checkpointing
and length-based sample grouping were enabled.

For the 1B and 3B systems, the per-device batch size was 16 on Libri2Mix
and 12 on Libri3Mix, corresponding to global batch sizes of 128 and 96,
respectively. For the 8B systems, the per-device batch size was 8,
corresponding to a global batch size of 64. All experiments ran for 50
epochs. We used a random seed of 42. Validation loss and greedy-decoding
WER were computed every 1,600 steps. Checkpoints were saved at the same
interval, and at most five checkpoints were retained. The checkpoint
with the lowest recorded validation loss was used for evaluation.

## VI Experimental Results and Analysis

### VI-A Overall Performance and Talker-Count Scaling

Table I presents the detailed comparison between static prefix
conditioning, encoder-only serialized CTC, and persistent decoder-side
memory access. The conventional LLM-SOT systems performed well on
Libri2Mix but degraded substantially as the talker count increased. For
the Instruct model, the WER increased from 9.1% to 32.3% under the noisy
condition and from 3.7% to 19.1% under the clean condition when moving
from Libri2Mix to Libri3Mix. The Base model exhibited the same pattern,
increasing from 11.0% to 38.9% and from 4.6% to 20.4%, respectively.
Instruction tuning improved the LLM-SOT baseline in all four conditions,
but did not resolve its sensitivity to the number of talkers.

The serialized-CTC references showed substantially less degradation from
two to three talkers. For example, the 1B-Instruct serialized-CTC system
obtained 10.1% and 20.5% WER on noisy Libri2Mix and Libri3Mix,
respectively, compared with 9.1% and 32.3% for LLM-SOT. Thus, although
serialized CTC was not consistently better on Libri2Mix, its
talker-specific encoder representations remained considerably more
robust as the number of speakers increased.

Although derived from the same serialized-CTC representations, static
prompts yielded only modest improvements. For example, HybridPrompt
reduced noisy Libri3Mix WER from 38.9% to 37.4% for the Base model and
from 32.3% to 31.3% for the Instruct model. Static prompting was more
competitive on Libri2Mix, where HybridPrompt obtained the best
clean-condition LLM-decoding result in both decoder panels. By contrast,
TokenPrompt performed poorly, indicating that a greedily decoded CTC
token sequence did not provide an effective acoustic conditioning signal
in this configuration.

Providing repeated decoder-side access to these representations produced
the largest improvements on Libri3Mix. For the Base model, gated access
reduced WER from 38.9% to 20.5% on noisy Libri3Mix and from 20.4% to
9.2% on clean Libri3Mix. For the Instruct model, gated access reduced
noisy Libri3Mix WER from 32.3% to 17.4%, while Stage 2 refinement
reduced clean Libri3Mix WER from 19.1% to 10.8%. These gains were
substantially larger than those obtained by the static CTC-derived
prompts, supporting the benefit of retaining direct memory access during
autoregressive generation. However, Stage 2 refinement did not improve
every condition relative to Stage 1. The main gain was therefore
attributed to gated persistent memory access, with low-rank refinement
providing additional improvements only in selected conditions.

Tables II and III show that the benefit over the corresponding LLM-SOT
baseline extended to larger decoders. For the 3B-Instruct model, Stage 2
reduced noisy and clean Libri3Mix WER from 27.9% and 15.2% to 17.1% and
8.7%, respectively. For the 8B models, the noisy Libri3Mix WER decreased
from 43.8% to 24.3% with the Base backbone and from 34.9% to 21.7% with
the Instruct backbone. The corresponding clean Libri3Mix results
decreased from 31.0% to 11.1% and from 21.6% to 10.1%.

Absolute performance was nevertheless not monotonic with decoder size.
On noisy Libri3Mix, the LLM-SOT results for the 1B, 3B, and 8B models
were 38.9%, 31.1%, and 43.8% for the Base variants and 32.3%, 27.9%, and
34.9% for the Instruct variants. Moreover, the 8B Stage 2 systems
remained slightly worse than their serialized-CTC references on this
condition. The scaling experiments therefore established improvements
over the matched same-scale LLM-SOT baselines, rather than showing that
increasing the LLM size necessarily improved multi-talker recognition or
that LLM decoding uniformly outperformed encoder-only serialized CTC.

### VI-B Controlled Memory and Interface Comparisons

Table IV organizes the controlled experiments according to the
hypothesis examined by each comparison. Panel A showed that CTC-derived
talker information could improve recognition when supplied as a static
prefix. However, the static configurations remained relatively weak on
noisy Libri3Mix, suggesting that providing the acoustic cues only during
prefill was insufficient for the more difficult three-talker condition.

Panel B examined the memory source and residual interface. IDs 42–44
shared the same per-corpus serialized-CTC initialization and 50-epoch
optimization budget. With mixture memory fixed, replacing direct
residual addition (ID 42) with gated integration (ID 43) substantially
reduced WER in all four conditions, indicating that the gate stabilized
the introduction of decoder-side acoustic information. With the gated
interface fixed, onset-ordered talker memory (ID 44) outperformed
mixture memory (ID 43) in both noisy conditions and on clean Libri3Mix,
while their clean Libri2Mix results were comparable. The advantage of
talker-structured memory was therefore more evident for three-talker
recognition. ID 18 was retained as the previously published
direct-access reference, but it was not used as a strictly
budget-matched control for IDs 42–44. Similarly, ID 19 was reported
separately as the sequentially initialized Stage 1 reference.

differing only in whether it was concatenated to the static prefix or

(ID 46) and persistent access (ID 19), which shared the same
stacked-memory initialization and optimization budget, showed the same
trend. In particular, retaining memory access during autoregressive
generation substantially improved noisy three-talker recognition. These
results demonstrated the benefit of making the memory available during

### VI-C Intermediate-Layer WavLM Front Ends

The original system constructed its talker-specific representations from
the final output of the 24-layer WavLM-Large encoder. This design
required all WavLM layers to remain active before the separator
constructed the serialized acoustic memory. Motivated by
intermediate-layer sidecar architectures \[54\], we investigated whether
the upper WavLM layers were necessary for this purpose. Intermediate
representations might preserve sufficient local acoustic and
talker-discriminative information before the stronger contextualization
introduced by the final layers. If such representations were sufficient,
the remaining upper encoder blocks could be removed, reducing the active
encoder parameters and acoustic-front-end computation.

We therefore constructed two WavLM-Large variants truncated after
Transformer layer 12 or 18. For a truncation depth $`\ell\in\{12,18\}`$,
the output of layer $`\ell`$ was supplied directly to the existing
separator and subsequent acoustic-conditioning modules. Layers
$`\ell+1`$ through 24 were removed from both training and inference.
This differed from merely tapping an intermediate representation while
retaining the full encoder: truncation produced an actual reduction in
deployment-time encoder depth and computation. The separator
architecture was held fixed across the two variants so that the
experiment primarily examined the effect of the WavLM feature depth and
the removal of the upper encoder blocks. Both variants used
LLaMA-3.2-1B-Instruct as the language decoder.

The separator and serialized-CTC heads were initialized from the same
serialized-CTC checkpoint used to initialize Exp. 44 and were further
fine-tuned in these experiments. The retained WavLM layers, temporal
adapter, enc_to_dec_proj, separator, and serialized-CTC heads were
trainable. For the LLM, LoRA was applied to the query, key, value, and
output projections of self-attention. The input embeddings,
language-model output head and the remaining dense LLM parameters were
frozen. The models were optimized using the training objective in
Eq. (22).

The results in Table V show that the performance improved monotonically
as more WavLM layers were retained. Retaining all 24 WavLM layers
produced the best result in every condition, with larger gains in the
three-talker conditions. One possible explanation was that deeper WavLM
representations provided contextual or linguistic information useful for
recognizing overlapping speech. In this sense, the term “acoustic
memory” described the speech-derived source of the representations and
did not imply that they contain only low-level acoustic information.
However, all retained WavLM layers were trainable, so increasing encoder
depth also increased the number of trainable parameters and the capacity
for task-specific adaptation. The present comparison therefore could not
isolate the contribution of richer contextual representations from that
of increased trainable capacity. We interpreted these results as an
empirical accuracy–cost trade-off for encoder truncation under the
evaluated training protocol.

### VI-D Stage 2 Joint Low-Rank Refinement

Stage 2 was a downstream refinement of the trained gated cross-attention
system rather than evidence for the architectural effect of gating. The
complete sweep in Table VI separated the effect of adding self-attention
LoRA from changes in cross-attention LoRA rank and scaling. For the
matched $`r=8`$, $`\alpha_{\mathrm{LoRA}}=4`$ pairs, joint refinement
lowered or matched test WER in every displayed cell. Thus, the evidence
supported joint low-rank refinement as a useful second-stage
optimization, but not a uniform claim that its benefit grows with model
size. Across that sweep, changing cross-attention-only LoRA capacity
produced relatively small and non-monotonic differences; therefore,
larger rank alone did not explain the gains of the joint configuration.

gap indicated that the current systems were sensitive to the onset
configuration and that performance on the offset mixtures did not
directly predict performance on the original LibriMix mixtures. In
particular, the absence of additional offsets produced more simultaneous
or weakly separated onsets, making the onset-based serialization order
more ambiguous.

To further examine ordering ambiguity on the original LibriMix mixtures,
we analyzed the decoding outputs of five systems under both clean and
noisy conditions. All source waveforms started at sample zero, while the
reference transcripts followed metadata source-index order. After
removing prompt wrappers, we split each hypothesis at speaker-change
tokens and, for hypotheses with exactly $`K`$ nonempty segments, found
the one-to-one assignment to reference transcripts that minimized summed
word-level edit distance. Among hypotheses with a unique minimum-cost
assignment, agreement with source-index order ranged from 49.3% to 50.5%
on Libri2Mix and from 16.0% to 17.8% on Libri3Mix. The observed
permutations were approximately evenly distributed, indicating no strong
preference for the metadata source-index order. These statistics covered
67.1–99.9% of mixtures; hypotheses with missing or extra segments or
tied assignments were excluded. These results were consistent with
increased ordering ambiguity on the original LibriMix mixtures. However,
waveform start did not necessarily coincide with actual speech onset,
and reliable onset annotations were unavailable. The analysis therefore
did not establish whether the generated order followed true speech onset
or other acoustic cues, and transcript-based assignments could also have
been affected by recognition errors.

### VI-F Cross-Domain Evaluation on LibriCSS

Table VIII evaluates transfer to far-field room recordings without
retraining on LibriCSS \[49\]. Across both the two- and three-talker
subsets, the overall WER improvement was driven primarily by fewer
insertion errors. For the two-talker subset, the overall substitution,
deletion, and insertion rates changed from 6.02%, 2.39%, and 14.15% for
LLM-SOT to 6.62%, 2.51%, and 10.96% for the proposed system,
respectively. Thus, the WER reduction from 22.56% to 20.09% resulted
from a 3.19-point reduction in insertions, despite small increases in
substitutions and deletions. The same pattern was more pronounced for
the three-talker subset. The overall substitution rate decreased
slightly from 11.11% to 10.77%, while the insertion rate decreased
substantially from 25.04% to 15.71%. The deletion rate, however,
increased from 6.02% to 7.73%. Together, these changes reduced the
overall WER from 42.17% to 34.22%.

The three-talker 0L condition provided the clearest example of this
insertion-dominated behavior. For LLM-SOT, the substitution, deletion,
and insertion rates were 8.12%, 0.74%, and 41.21%, respectively, showing
that most of its 50.06% WER originated from insertions. The proposed
system reduced the insertion rate to 5.29% and the substitution rate to
6.03%, although its deletion rate increased to 4.18%, resulting in a
substantially lower WER of 15.50%. This error pattern was consistent
with reduced over-generation and more stable serialization in the
presence of long pauses, rather than an improvement attributable solely
to acoustic modeling. Because the three-talker 0L subset contained only
24 clips from seven sessions, this observation should nevertheless be
interpreted cautiously.

The advantage became limited under the most severe overlap condition.
For two-talker OV40, insertions decreased only slightly from 22.92% to
22.34%, while substitutions and deletions increased from 9.95% and 3.36%
to 10.59% and 4.49%, respectively. Consequently, the proposed system was
slightly worse than LLM-SOT (37.42% versus 36.23% WER). For three-talker
OV40, the insertion rate decreased from 27.73% to 24.04%, but the
deletion rate increased from 7.67% to 9.70%; the resulting WER
improvement was therefore modest (49.08% versus 51.18%). We interpreted
these cross-domain results as evidence that serialized acoustic-memory
access primarily mitigates insertion-dominated failures, particularly
under long pauses, while robustness to severe overlap remains limited.

### VI-G Inference Efficiency and Deployment Cost

Table IX reports the inference efficiency and active deployment
parameters of the 1B, 3B, and 8B systems. The LLM-SOT baselines achieved
RTFs ranging from 0.083 to 0.194, whereas the gated memory-access
systems ranged from 0.130 to 0.280. The corresponding audio throughputs
remained between 3.57 and 7.71 seconds of input audio per second of
processing. Relative to the same-scale LLM-SOT baseline, the end-to-end
latency of the stacked and gated systems increased by approximately
$`1.45\times`$–$`1.62\times`$. For the 1B models, gated access increased
weighted decoding latency from 15.6/15.7 to 21.7/21.8 ms per token for
Libri2Mix/Libri3Mix. The corresponding changes were from 26.3/26.6 to
38.1/38.2 ms per token for 3B and from 30.0/30.7 to 42.0/41.5 ms per
token for 8B. Thus, most of the remaining overhead arose during repeated
cross-attention-based decoding rather than from the one-time
construction of the memory cache.

The higher end-to-end latency on Libri3Mix was also partly explained by
the input and output distributions. The evaluated Libri3Mix utterances
were longer on average and contained approximately 1,641 memory
positions and 67 generated tokens, compared with 898 memory positions
and 45 generated tokens for Libri2Mix. In contrast, the weighted
per-token latency changed only modestly between the two corpora for a
fixed system. The Libri2Mix–Libri3Mix difference in end-to-end latency
therefore reflected both the longer serialized memory and the larger
number of generated tokens. The additional memory required by the
cross-attention K/V cache was moderate: approximately 28/51 MB for 1B,
49/90 MB for 3B, and 56/103 MB for 8B on the two-/three-talker subsets.

In parameter terms, gated access added 64–66M active parameters to the
1B baseline, 132–133M to the 3B baseline, and 182–184M to the 8B
baseline. These additions corresponded to approximately 4.4%, 3.8%, and
2.2% of the respective LLM-SOT deployment models. Consequently, the
proposed interface was parameter-efficient relative to the size of the
LLM backbone, but it was not computationally lightweight. Repeated
attention over a long serialized acoustic memory introduced
non-negligible latency and memory overhead.

### VI-H Streaming Limitation

The proposed method is currently limited to offline recognition.
Serialized acoustic memory is constructed from the complete input
segment and remains fixed during autoregressive decoding. Accordingly,
“persistent access” refers only to the decoder’s repeated access to this
complete segment-level memory. It does not imply that memory extracted
from an initial chunk remains sufficient when subsequent audio arrives.

For strict streaming recognition, a fixed memory constructed from the
initial chunk cannot contain acoustic evidence from future chunks and is
therefore insufficient. The memory would need to be updated as new
chunks arrive. Such an extension would also require a causal or
chunkwise encoder, consistent tracking of talker branches across chunks,
and a streaming-compatible SOT emission policy. These components are not
included or evaluated in the present system, and we therefore do not
claim streaming capability.

A more direct extension is buffered long-form transcription, in which a
complete memory is constructed and held fixed within each fully observed
window and then reconstructed for the next window. Adjacent windows can
include an overlapping region, which can be used to maintain
cross-window speaker consistency through PIT-style permutation matching
between their talker-specific representations or predicted token
sequences. After determining the speaker correspondence, the hypotheses
can be stitched by removing duplicated tokens in the overlap. A simple
strategy is to discard tokens from the later window whose timestamps
fall within the overlapping region. Although the autoregressive decoder
does not directly produce token timestamps, approximate timing
information can be obtained from the corresponding CTC alignment. We
leave the implementation and evaluation of this buffered long-form
extension for future work.

### VI-I Speech-Prefix Attention Across Decoder Depth

To examine how the two systems access the conventional acoustic prefix,
we analyzed decoder self-attention in LLM-SOT (ID–26) and the proposed
model (ID–30.D) in Figure 2. The speech prefix $`\mathbf{H}_{p}`$
denotes the projected acoustic representations embedded in the decoder
input sequence, distinct from the external serialized memory accessed
through cross-attention. We inspected five pre-specified layers, L0, L7,
L14, L21, and L27 (zero-based indexing), covering different depths of
the 28-layer decoder (3B-Instruct Model). For each of the 24 query heads
in a selected layer, we summed the self-attention probabilities assigned
to valid speech-prefix positions at each lexical-token prediction step.
We averaged these values over lexical tokens within each sample and then
equally across samples, excluding control tokens. The proposed model
assigned more attention to the speech prefix at all five inspected
layers. This difference was not fully explained by attention to the
first speech position: after excluding that position, the proposed model
still exhibited higher attention mass throughout the inspected depths.
The first-position contribution was nevertheless substantial at L14 and
L21, highlighting the importance of distinguishing total prefix
attention from attention to its remaining positions. The difference also
extended across heads. At L7 and L21, all 24 heads in the proposed model
exceeded the total-mass threshold, compared with zero and six heads in
LLM-SOT, respectively. These observations indicated that, in the
inspected layers, the proposed system distributed attention to the
conventional speech prefix across more heads, despite retaining an
additional external-memory pathway.

## VII Conclusion

In this work, we investigated why conventional LLM-based serialized
output training performs well for two-talker mixtures but degrades
substantially as the number of competing talkers increases. Continuous
CTC-derived prompts were more effective than discrete CTC hypotheses,
but their limited improvements on Libri3Mix showed that enriching the
initial prefix alone is insufficient. We therefore proposed SOT-aligned
serialized acoustic memory with persistent decoder-side access.
Talker-specific representations are organized in onset order and
retained as external memory, while gated residual cross-attention makes
them directly available during autoregressive generation. Here,
“persistent” refers to the continued availability of the memory, rather
than guaranteed reliance on it at every decoding step. The controlled
comparisons clarify the contributions of this design. Gated integration
consistently improved over direct stacked cross-attention in the
reported configurations. The matched static–persistent and prefill-only
comparisons further showed that retaining access during generation is
more effective than presenting the same acoustic information only in the
initial context. Removing the conventional mixture prefix caused
substantial degradation, demonstrating that the global mixture
representation and talker-structured memory provide complementary
information. Across the 1B, 3B, and 8B backbones, persistent memory
access substantially improved the corresponding same-scale LLM-SOT
baselines, with the largest gains generally observed on three-talker
mixtures. Joint low-rank refinement provided additional improvements in
selected conditions, with its most consistent gains observed for the 3B
models, but did not uniformly improve every Stage 1 result. Performance
was also not monotonic with decoder size. In particular, the 8B systems
did not surpass encoder-only serialized CTC on noisy Libri3Mix despite
their greater inference cost. The results therefore support persistent
acoustic access rather than a general claim that larger LLM decoders
necessarily improve multi-talker ASR. The optimized implementation
caches both the LLM self-attention states and the projected keys and
values of the fixed acoustic memory. Although this substantially reduces
the cost of the original uncached implementation, repeated
cross-attention still introduces non-negligible latency. The proposed
interface is therefore parameter-efficient relative to the LLM backbone,
but should not be characterized as computationally lightweight.
Serialized CTC remains the preferable accuracy–efficiency trade-off in
some conditions. Evaluation on the derived fixed-talker LibriCSS subsets
showed transfer to far-field recordings without retraining, with the
overall gains arising primarily from fewer insertion errors. However,
improvements became limited under severe overlap. Improving robustness
to ambiguous onsets, unknown talker counts, severe overlap, and
streaming or long-form input remains important future work.
