###### Abstract

Speech language models (SLMs) exhibit strong capabilities in automatic
speech recognition (ASR) and general-purpose speech understanding, yet
ASR-specific training often degrades spoken question answering (QA)
performance. We revisit this conflict from the perspective of
task-specific input–output mappings and one-hot supervision. Our
findings reveal pronounced task-asymmetric effects: ASR-only training
severely limits QA capability, whereas QA-only training retains
reasonable ASR performance. We hypothesize that the relatively
deterministic mapping of ASR, combined with single-reference
supervision, biases models toward transcription-specific behavior.
Motivated by this perspective, we advocate broadening ASR learning
objectives while preserving recognition accuracy. We propose Learning
from Distribution (LFD), which aligns speech-conditioned predictions
with the conditional output distributions of a pretrained text LLM.
Extensive experiments demonstrate that LFD improves both ASR and QA
performance, exhibits superior data efficiency, and remains effective
under full-parameter fine-tuning. CoT-ASR further supports the broader
applicability of our proposed supervision principle.

## 1 Introduction

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have accelerated the
development of speech language models (SLMs), improving both automatic
speech recognition (ASR) and general-purpose speech understanding Chu et
al. (2024); Xu et al. (2025); Lin et al. (2026); Yang et al. (2026); Lu
et al. (2025). Unlike conventional ASR systems, SLMs enable
instruction-conditioned recognition, incorporating contextual
information and domain-specific hotwords into transcription Zheng et al.
(2026); Wang et al. (2025). By leveraging pretrained LLM capabilities,
SLMs offer a framework for unifying speech recognition and
general-purpose language intelligence Wang et al. (2024a); Wang et al.
(2024b).

Despite recent advances, general-purpose SLMs still lag behind
specialized ASR systems in recognition accuracy and robustness,
motivating ASR-specific fine-tuning Shi et al. (2026); Ni et al. (2025).
However, ASR specialization may compromise general-purpose capabilities,
particularly spoken question answering (QA), by biasing models toward
transcription Held et al. (2025); Hsiao et al. (2025); Cuervo et al.
(2026); Guo et al. (2026). This raises a key challenge: improving ASR
without sacrificing broader language capabilities.

Existing studies have primarily attributed the degradation of
general-purpose capabilities in SLMs to catastrophic forgetting and
cross-modal misalignment Hsiao et al. (2025); Held et al. (2025); Cuervo
et al. (2026); Lu et al. (2025); Tan et al. (2025). To mitigate these
issues, various strategies have been explored, including experience
replay, parameter-efficient adaptation, model merging, and cross-modal
knowledge distillation Hsiao et al. (2025); Wang et al. (2024a); Fan et
al. (2025); Wang et al. (2026); Guo et al. (2026); Cao et al. (2026).
Nonetheless, how task-specific data characteristics and supervision
objectives contribute to capability degradation remains insufficiently
understood.

Addressing this question requires examining the relationship between
task-specific mappings and training supervision. Although ASR and QA
share the same generative formulation, ASR follows an approximately
one-to-one mapping, whereas QA admits multiple valid responses. Despite
these differences, both tasks commonly rely on one-hot cross-entropy
(CE) supervision, which reinforces a single reference output per
training example. While CE naturally aligns with the deterministic
mapping of ASR, diverse QA responses can collectively approximate the
underlying output distribution. We argue that these distinct mappings
may lead to asymmetric learning behaviors under the same One-hot
supervision objective.

We hypothesize that the interaction between task-specific input–output
mappings and one-hot supervision contributes to the ASR–QA conflict. To
address this, we advocate broadening ASR learning objectives beyond
single-reference targets. We propose Learning from Distribution (LFD),
which realizes this principle by aligning speech-conditioned predictions
with the conditional output distributions of a pretrained text LLM. We
further explore an alternative training strategy to demonstrate the
broader applicability of this principle. Our main contributions are
summarized as follows:

- •
  We investigate the ASR–QA conflict from a new perspective by examining
  the interaction between task-specific input–output mappings and
  one-hot supervision. Through systematic comparisons of ASR-only and
  QA-only training, we reveal pronounced task-asymmetric effects and
  provide a mapping-based explanation for this phenomenon.
- •
  We propose broadening ASR learning objectives based on the distinct
  input–output mappings of ASR and QA. We introduce Learning from
  Distribution (LFD) as one realization of this principle and further
  demonstrate its generality through Chain-of-Thought ASR.
- •
  Extensive experiments demonstrate that LFD improves both ASR and QA
  performance with superior data efficiency. Tail-aware top-$`k`$
  supervision maintains competitive performance while enabling compact
  storage of teacher distributions and efficient offline supervision.

## 2 A Mapping Perspective on ASR–QA Conflict

ASR and QA can both be formulated as speech-conditioned text generation:

```math
p_{\phi}(Y\mid X,I)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{\phi}(y_{t}\mid X,I,y_{<t}), \tag{1}
```

where $`X`$ is the speech input, $`I`$ is the task instruction, $`Y`$ is
the output sequence, and $`\phi`$ denotes the trainable adapter
parameters.

QA’s one-to-many mapping with multiple valid responses.

Figure 2: Overview of LFD. The text LLM teacher generates responses and
token distributions, which the Speech LLM student learns via forward KL
divergence with a tail-aware top-$`k`$ approximation.

Despite sharing the same formulation, ASR and QA exhibit distinct
input–output mappings, as illustrated in Fig. 1. ASR typically involves
an approximately one-to-one mapping from speech to transcription,
whereas QA admits a one-to-many mapping with multiple semantically valid
responses. These mapping differences are reflected in the entropy
characteristics of their conditional output distributions. ASR generally
exhibits lower conditional output entropy due to its relatively
deterministic transcription targets, whereas QA tends to exhibit higher
entropy owing to the diversity of valid responses. We investigate these
task-dependent entropy characteristics in SLMs and text LLMs, with the
main results presented in Section 5.3 and additional analyses provided
in Appendix A.

Conventional cross-entropy (CE) applies the same one-hot supervision to
both tasks:

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}=-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log p_{\phi}(y_{t}^{*}\mid X,I,y_{<t}^{*}), \tag{2}
```

where $`y_{t}^{*}`$ denotes the reference token. For each training
example, CE reinforces the conditional probability of a single reference
output. For ASR, this supervision naturally aligns with its relatively
deterministic input–output mapping, consistently reinforcing
speech-to-transcription alignment. For QA, although each training
example provides only one reference response, the inherent diversity of
valid responses can be captured across sufficiently large training
datasets, allowing CE to approximate the underlying conditional output
distribution.

Nevertheless, the distinct mapping characteristics of ASR and QA may
lead to different learning behaviors under the same supervision
objective. In particular, repeated optimization toward deterministic ASR
targets may bias speech–language alignment toward transcription-specific
behavior, limiting access to the pretrained LLM’s broader generation
capabilities. In contrast, diverse QA supervision may expose the model
to richer generation patterns, resulting in different cross-task
effects. We therefore hypothesize that the ASR–QA conflict is partly
shaped by the interaction between task-specific input–output mappings
and one-hot supervision, rather than being an inherent consequence of
ASR data alone.

## 3 Method

### 3.1 Rethinking ASR Supervision

As discussed in Section 2, the approximately one-to-one mapping of ASR
naturally aligns with one-hot CE supervision. However, repeatedly
optimizing deterministic transcription targets may bias speech–language
alignment toward transcription-specific behavior, limiting the SLM’s
ability to leverage the pretrained LLM’s broader generation
capabilities. This suggests that the ASR–QA conflict may be mitigated by
reconsidering how ASR data is used for supervision.

Motivated by this observation, we advocate broadening ASR learning
objectives beyond single-reference supervision. Rather than relying
solely on deterministic transcription targets, ASR training should
incorporate richer supervision that preserves the pretrained LLM’s
broader generation behavior. This principle aims to maintain accurate
speech recognition while reducing the risk of compromising
general-purpose capabilities.

### 3.2 Learning from Distribution

Following this principle, we propose Learning from Distribution (LFD),
which broadens the learning objective by aligning speech-conditioned
predictions with the conditional output distributions of a pretrained
text LLM. Instead of relying solely on one-hot reference tokens, LFD
preserves the LLM’s probability allocation across possible predictions,
providing richer supervision for ASR while aiming to retain its broader
generation capabilities. As illustrated in Fig. 2, a frozen text LLM
teacher and an SLM student are conditioned on task-specific text and
speech inputs, respectively.

Given the target response $`Y^{*}`$, teacher forcing is applied to both
models along the same response trajectory. Their token distributions are
defined as

```math
\displaystyle q_{t} \displaystyle=p_{T}(\cdot\mid C_{\mathrm{text}},y_{<t}^{*}), \tag{3}
```

```math
\displaystyle p_{\phi,t} \displaystyle=p_{\phi}(\cdot\mid X,I,y_{<t}^{*}),
```

where $`C_{\mathrm{text}}`$ denotes the task-conditioned textual context
and $`p_{T}`$ the teacher distribution. LFD minimizes the forward KL
divergence between them:

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{full}}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{t}\|p_{\phi,t}). \tag{4}
```

This objective transfers the LLM’s conditional generation behavior to
speech, rather than supervising individual reference tokens alone.

### 3.3 Tail-Aware Top-k Distribution Learning

Directly storing full-vocabulary teacher distributions incurs
substantial storage overhead Peng et al. (2025); Zaidi et al. (2025);
Ryskulov et al. (2026). To enable efficient distribution supervision, we
retain only the top-$`k`$ tokens with the highest teacher
probabilities Li et al. (2025a). Let $`\mathcal{S}_{t}^{k}`$ denote the
selected token set at position $`t`$.

However, naively truncating the teacher distribution yields

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{trunc}}=-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{S}_{t}^{k}}q_{t}(v)\log p_{\phi,t}(v), \tag{5}
```

which encourages the student to concentrate its probability mass on the
selected tokens. In particular, when $`k=1`$, this objective reduces to
a scaled cross-entropy loss on the teacher’s most probable token, losing
the intended benefits of distributional supervision.

Motivated by the importance of preserving tail probability
information Zaidi et al. (2025); Huang and Wei (2026); Meng et al.
(2026), we adopt a tail-aware KL objective that retains the aggregate
probability mass outside the top-$`k`$ set. The teacher and student tail
probabilities are defined as

```math
\displaystyle q_{t}^{\mathrm{tail}} \displaystyle=1-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{S}_{t}^{k}}q_{t}(v), \tag{6}
```

```math
\displaystyle p_{\phi,t}^{\mathrm{tail}} \displaystyle=1-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{S}_{t}^{k}}p_{\phi,t}(v).
```

The resulting objective is

```math
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LFD}}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Bigg[ \displaystyle\sum_{v\in\mathcal{S}_{t}^{k}}q_{t}(v)\log\frac{q_{t}(v)}{p_{\phi,t}(v)} \tag{7}
```

```math
\displaystyle+q_{t}^{\mathrm{tail}}\log\frac{q_{t}^{\mathrm{tail}}}{p_{\phi,t}^{\mathrm{tail}}}\Bigg].
```

By preserving residual probability mass, this objective constrains the
student’s predictions within and beyond the top-$`k`$ set, enabling
efficient distribution matching without storing full-vocabulary teacher
distributions.

## 4 Experimental Setup

### 4.1 Model Architecture and Training Setup

Our SLM consists of a pretrained Whisper-large-v3 speech encoder Radford
et al. (2023), a modality adapter, and a Qwen3-4B LLM¹¹ 1
https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-4B Yang et al. (2025). We employ
Qwen3-4B as the frozen text teacher for distribution supervision.

We use 8,000 hours of speech from Emilia He et al. (2024) to construct
ASR and QA training data. ASR uses the original transcripts, while QA
data is generated from transcripts using Qwen3-4B. We evaluate four
settings: CE-ASR, CE-QA, LFD-ASR, and LFD-QA. Within each task, CE and
LFD share identical data, architectures, and initializations, differing
only in supervision objectives. LFD uses $`k=256`$ by default.

In the main experiments, we focus on speech–language alignment, where
the pretrained speech encoder and LLM remain frozen, and only the
modality adapter is optimized. All models are trained for one epoch with
a learning rate of $`1\times 10^{-4}`$ and a cosine learning rate
scheduler.

### 4.2 Evaluation Benchmarks and Metrics

We evaluate ASR performance on the LibriSpeech test-clean and test-other
sets Panayotov et al. (2015) using word error rate (WER). For spoken QA,
we adopt the TELEVAL benchmark Li et al. (2025b), including LlamaQA-en,
TriviaQA-en, and WebQ-en, and report answer accuracy (ACC). Detailed
descriptions of the benchmarks and evaluation metrics are provided in
Appendix B.

## 5 Results and Analysis

### 5.1 Task-Asymmetric Effects of One-Hot Supervision

Table 1 compares CE and LFD under ASR-only and QA-only training
settings.

One-hot supervision exhibits task-asymmetric effects consistent with
distinct input–output mappings. Comparing A1 and B1, ASR-only CE
achieves strong recognition performance but severely limited QA
capability. In contrast, QA-only CE maintains strong QA performance
while retaining reasonable ASR capability. This pronounced asymmetry is
consistent with our mapping perspective: the approximately one-to-one
mapping of ASR, combined with one-hot supervision, repeatedly reinforces
deterministic transcription targets, potentially biasing the adapter
toward transcription-specific alignment. In contrast, the inherent
diversity of QA responses may expose the model to richer generation
patterns, resulting in different cross-task behaviors.

Broadening ASR supervision mitigates the degradation of general-purpose
capabilities. Comparing A1 and A2, replacing CE with LFD under the same
ASR-only training setting substantially improves QA capability while
further enhancing ASR performance. Notably, these improvements are
achieved without introducing any QA training data. By broadening the
learning objective beyond individual reference tokens, LFD enables the
model to better leverage the pretrained LLM’s broader generation
capabilities. This finding suggests that the degradation associated with
ASR specialization depends not only on the training data itself, but
also on how the model learns from it.

Distributional supervision mitigates task-asymmetric learning effects.
Comparing B1 and B2, LFD improves both ASR and QA under QA-only
training. Comparing A2 and B2, LFD substantially narrows the cross-task
gap between ASR-only and QA-only training, while each retains an
advantage on its primary task. These findings suggest that learning
richer conditional output distributions mitigates task-asymmetric
effects, supporting our principle of broadening supervision beyond
single-reference targets.

### 5.2 Scaling Behavior of CE and LFD

To investigate the scaling behavior of CE and LFD, we conduct ASR-only
and QA-only training with 2k, 4k, 6k, and 8k hours of data, using a
top-$`k`$ value of 256 for LFD. All experiments follow the same model
architecture and training configurations described in Section 4.1.
Table 2 summarizes the overall ASR and QA performance, with detailed
results provided in Appendix C.

LFD exhibits superior data efficiency. With limited data, LFD achieves
substantially better ASR and QA performance than CE under both ASR-only
and QA-only settings. While CE requires more data for effective
speech–language alignment, LFD achieves strong cross-task performance at
smaller scales. These results suggest that learning richer conditional
output distributions provides more informative supervision, facilitating
effective alignment with limited data.

Increasing data does not eliminate the task asymmetry of one-hot
supervision. As training data increases, ASR-only CE substantially
improves recognition performance but consistently fails to develop
meaningful QA capability. In contrast, QA-only CE progressively improves
both tasks, achieving strong QA performance while retaining reasonable
ASR capability. This persistent asymmetry is consistent with our mapping
perspective: the approximately one-to-one mapping of ASR, combined with
one-hot supervision, repeatedly reinforces deterministic transcription
targets, potentially favoring transcription-specific alignment.
Importantly, increasing ASR data alone does not alleviate this
limitation, suggesting that the observed capability degradation cannot
be attributed solely to insufficient training data.

Broadening supervision changes cross-task scaling behavior. Unlike
ASR-only CE, ASR-only LFD develops strong QA capability even with
limited training data, while continuously improving recognition
performance as data increases. QA-only LFD also maintains strong
performance on both tasks, with QA accuracy gradually approaching
saturation. Although QA-only LFD retains a modest advantage in QA
performance, the gap between the two training settings remains
substantially smaller than under CE. These findings further support our
proposed principle that broadening the learning objective beyond
single-reference targets can mitigate task-asymmetric alignment effects,
rather than relying solely on increasing the amount of task-specific
data.

### 5.3 Distributional Analysis of ASR and QA

To investigate the task-dependent distributional characteristics of ASR
and QA, we analyze the token-level predictive entropy of SLMs trained
with CE and LFD, as reported in Table 3.

Predictive entropy reflects task-specific input–output mappings. Except
for A1, all systems exhibit lower predictive entropy on ASR than on QA,
consistent with the distinct mapping characteristics discussed in
Section 2. This difference reflects the relatively constrained output
space of ASR and the diversity of valid responses in QA. In contrast, A1
exhibits similarly low entropy on both tasks and predominantly generates
transcriptions even for QA inputs, suggesting a transcription-oriented
bias induced by ASR-only CE supervision. Furthermore, as shown in
Appendix A, LFD-trained SLMs exhibit task-dependent entropy patterns
similar to those of the pretrained text LLM, suggesting that
distributional supervision helps preserve its task-conditioned
generation characteristics.

### 5.4 Effect of Distribution Granularity

To investigate the effect of distribution granularity, we vary top-$`k`$
over $`\{1,16,64,256\}`$ using 8,000 hours of training data under both
ASR-only and QA-only settings. Fig. 3 shows the overall performance,
with details in Appendix D.

LFD is robust to distribution granularity. Across both training
settings, varying top-$`k`$ has a relatively limited impact on ASR and
QA performance, with even $`k=1`$ achieving competitive results. For
ASR-only training, this may be attributed to the concentrated teacher
distributions induced by the repetition instruction. For QA-only
training, diverse generation trajectories in the training data may
provide rich supervision across different contexts, reducing the need to
explicitly preserve large numbers of token probabilities at each
position.

Sparse distribution supervision offers practical advantages. The
competitive performance of $`k=1`$ suggests that distributional
supervision need not require fine-grained token probabilities. Notably,
our tail-aware objective retains the teacher’s top-1 confidence and the
remaining probability mass, rather than reducing supervision to a hard
label. This finding suggests that sparse distribution supervision can
substantially reduce the storage and data transfer overhead of teacher
distributions, particularly in offline distillation.

### 5.5 Behavior under Full-Parameter Fine-Tuning

To investigate whether the task-asymmetric effects of CE persist beyond
adapter-only alignment, we further conduct full-parameter fine-tuning
experiments. For ASR-only training, both CE and LFD are initialized from
the same ASR-only LFD model trained with top-$`k=1`$ during the
alignment stage. Similarly, QA-only experiments are initialized from the
corresponding QA-only LFD model. All training configurations are kept
identical except for the supervision objectives and their associated
settings, with top-$`k=1`$ used for LFD. Table 4 presents the results,
including the performance of the initialization models.

Task-asymmetric effects of one-hot supervision persist under
full-parameter fine-tuning. From C0, ASR-only CE (C1) improves
recognition performance but severely degrades QA capability. In
contrast, QA-only CE (D1) improves both ASR and QA performance over its
initialization (D0). This asymmetry is consistent with our mapping
perspective: repeated optimization toward deterministic ASR targets
under one-hot supervision may favor transcription-specific behavior,
compromising previously acquired general-purpose capabilities.
Importantly, this phenomenon persists even when the model starts with
strong QA capabilities, suggesting that the conflict is not limited to
the initial speech–language alignment stage.

Broadening ASR supervision mitigates capability degradation during
full-parameter fine-tuning. Compared with C0, ASR-only LFD (C2) improves
ASR and overall QA performance. Notably, C2 achieves nearly identical
ASR performance to C1 while substantially outperforming it on all QA
benchmarks. Similarly, QA-only LFD (D2) improves both ASR and overall QA
performance over D0 and outperforms CE (D1) on both overall metrics.
These findings support our principle that broadening supervision beyond
single-reference targets can mitigate task-asymmetric effects while
maintaining strong task performance, even during full-parameter
fine-tuning.

## 6 Beyond LFD: Rethinking ASR Supervision with CoT

The full-parameter fine-tuning results in Section 5.5 show that ASR-only
CE training can severely degrade QA capabilities. To further examine our
hypothesis regarding the interaction between ASR’s deterministic mapping
and single-reference supervision, we investigate Chain-of-Thought ASR
(CoT-ASR) as an alternative strategy. Unlike direct transcription
supervision, CoT-ASR broadens the generation space through intermediate
reasoning while preserving transcription as the primary objective. We
first obtain a base SLM through adapter-only alignment on mixed CoT-ASR
and CoT-QA data, then separately perform full-parameter fine-tuning with
CoT-ASR and CoT-QA data from this shared initialization.

As shown in Table 5, CoT-ASR improves recognition while largely
preserving QA capability despite using only ASR data for full-parameter
fine-tuning. CoT-QA similarly improves ASR while maintaining strong QA
performance. These findings suggest that capability degradation is not
inevitable under ASR-only training but may depend on the interaction
between task mappings and supervision objectives. Thus, CoT-ASR provides
an alternative realization of our principle of broadening ASR
supervision beyond LFD.

## 7 Discussion

### 7.1 Task Mappings and Supervision as Joint Factors

Our findings suggest that the ASR–QA conflict arises partly from the
interaction between task-specific input–output mappings and supervision
objectives. Although CE can learn conditional output distributions for
both tasks, single-reference supervision may induce substantially
different learning behaviors. In particular, ASR’s relatively
deterministic mapping, combined with repeated transcription supervision,
may bias speech–language alignment toward ASR-specific behavior. This
interpretation is consistent with persistent QA degradation under
ASR-only CE despite increasing training data, as well as severe
capability loss during full-parameter fine-tuning. The distinct entropy
characteristics of ASR and QA further support our proposed mapping
perspective.

Our results also highlight broadening ASR supervision as a promising
solution without changing the recognition objective. LFD realizes this
principle by learning the pretrained LLM’s conditional output
distributions beyond individual reference tokens, while CoT-ASR broadens
the generation space through intermediate reasoning. Both strategies
preserve ASR as the primary objective, suggesting that general-purpose
capability degradation is not inevitable under ASR-only training but
depends partly on how task-specific data is supervised during training.

### 7.2 Relation to Prior Distribution-based Alignment Methods

Prior studies have explored different perspectives to address the
degradation of general-purpose capabilities in SLMs. DiVA Held et al.
(2025) and BLSP-KD Wang et al. (2024a) leverage cross-modal distillation
to transfer pretrained LLM capabilities to speech, while SALAD Cuervo et
al. (2026) investigates capability degradation from the perspectives of
catastrophic forgetting, cross-modal misalignment, and domain mismatch.
Although these studies propose effective approaches to preserving
general-purpose capabilities, they do not systematically examine why
ASR-oriented training can lead to particularly severe capability
degradation.

In contrast, our work introduces a mapping-based perspective to
investigate the interaction between task-specific input–output mappings
and one-hot CE supervision. We argue that the relatively deterministic
mapping of ASR, combined with single-reference supervision, may bias
models toward transcription-specific behavior, leading to asymmetric
effects on ASR and QA capabilities. Based on this perspective, we
advocate broadening the learning space of ASR while preserving its
recognition objective. LFD realizes this principle by learning the
conditional output distributions of pretrained LLMs, while CoT-ASR
provides an alternative realization through richer generation
trajectories. Our contribution lies not only in the method but also in a
new perspective for understanding and mitigating the ASR–QA conflict.

## 8 Conclusion

In this work, we revisit the ASR–QA conflict in SLMs through the
interaction between task-specific input–output mappings and supervision
objectives. We reveal task-asymmetric effects of one-hot supervision and
advocate broadening ASR learning objectives while preserving recognition
accuracy. We realize this principle through Learning from Distribution
(LFD), which leverages the conditional output distributions of
pretrained LLMs to provide richer supervision. Experiments demonstrate
that LFD improves both ASR and QA, mitigates task asymmetry, and
preserves cross-task capabilities during full-parameter fine-tuning.
CoT-ASR further supports the broader applicability of this principle,
highlighting the importance of mapping-aware supervision for
general-purpose SLMs.

## 9 Limitations

Our experiments are conducted on a single SLM architecture based on
Whisper-large-v3 and Qwen3-4B. Although our mapping-based perspective
focuses on task characteristics and supervision objectives rather than
specific model architectures, its generalizability across different
model families and scales remains to be explored.

Furthermore, our analysis focuses on ASR and spoken QA, leaving its
applicability to other speech-language tasks for future investigation.
Extending this perspective to tasks with diverse input–output mappings
may provide further insights into the interaction between task
characteristics and supervision objectives.

To further investigate the distributional characteristics of ASR and QA,
we analyze the token-level predictive entropy of both text LLMs and
SLMs. Given a model’s predictive distribution $`p_{t}`$ over vocabulary
$`\mathcal{V}`$ at generation step $`t`$, the entropy is defined as

```math
H(p_{t})=-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}p_{t}(v)\log p_{t}(v). \tag{8}
```

We report the average predictive entropy across response tokens for each
evaluation benchmark. Text LLMs are conditioned on speech transcripts,
whereas SLMs receive the corresponding speech inputs.

As shown in Table 6, both text LLMs and SLMs consistently exhibit
substantially lower predictive entropy on ASR than on QA. This pattern
is consistent with their distinct input–output mappings: ASR typically
involves relatively deterministic transcription targets, whereas QA
allows multiple semantically valid responses.

Notably, the pretrained Qwen3-4B exhibits the same task-dependent
entropy pattern, suggesting that these distributional differences are
not specific to speech–language alignment. Furthermore, the SLMs retain
this distinction, exhibiting concentrated predictions on ASR and more
diverse predictive distributions on QA. These observations provide
additional evidence that the entropy characteristics of ASR and QA
reflect their underlying task-specific mapping structures.

## Appendix B Evaluation Benchmarks and Metrics

ASR Evaluation. We evaluate ASR performance on LibriSpeech Panayotov et
al. (2015), a widely used English speech recognition benchmark derived
from public-domain audiobooks. We use the test-clean and test-other
subsets, which represent different recognition difficulty levels.
Performance is measured by word error rate (WER), defined as
$`(S+D+I)/N`$, where $`S`$, $`D`$, and $`I`$ denote substitution,
deletion, and insertion errors, respectively, and $`N`$ is the number of
reference words. Overall WER is averaged across the two subsets.

Spoken QA Evaluation. We evaluate spoken QA using three English subsets
of TELEVAL Li et al. (2025b): LlamaQA-en (300 samples), TriviaQA-en (837
samples), and WebQ-en (1,938 samples). These subsets assess spoken
question answering across general knowledge and factoid QA tasks.
Following TELEVAL’s evaluation protocol, responses are evaluated against
multiple acceptable reference answers using string matching. We report
answer accuracy (ACC) for each subset, with overall accuracy computed
across all 3,075 samples.

## Appendix C Detailed Scaling Results

Table 8 presents detailed scaling results of CE and LFD under ASR-only
and QA-only training settings. We evaluate both supervision objectives
with 2,000, 4,000, 6,000, and 8,000 hours of training data to
investigate their effectiveness across different data scales. ASR
performance is reported as WER on LibriSpeech test-clean and test-other,
while QA performance is measured by accuracy on three English TELEVAL
subsets. Individual benchmark results and overall performance are
provided for comprehensive comparison.

The results show that LFD provides substantial advantages over CE at
smaller data scales. As training data increases, ASR-only CE improves
recognition performance but consistently exhibits limited QA capability.
In contrast, LFD achieves stronger cross-task performance under both
training settings. These observations further support the data
efficiency and task-asymmetric learning effects discussed in
Section 5.2.

## Appendix D Detailed Distribution Granularity Results

Table 8 presents detailed results of LFD with different top-$`k`$ values
under ASR-only and QA-only training settings. We investigate
distribution granularity using $`k\in\{1,16,64,256\}`$, while keeping
all other training configurations unchanged. For each setting, we report

Across different top-$`k`$ values, LFD maintains relatively stable

distribution granularity. Notably, even $`k=1`$ achieves competitive
results when the residual probability mass is preserved through
tail-aware supervision. This finding highlights the practicality of
compact teacher distribution storage for efficient offline training.
