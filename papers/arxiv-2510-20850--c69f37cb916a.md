# Can Large Audio Language Models Understand Child Stuttering Speech? Speech Summarization, and Source separation

###### Abstract

Child speech differs from adult speech in acoustics, prosody, and language development, and disfluencies (repetitions, prolongations, blocks) further challenge Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) and downstream Natural Language Processing (NLP). Recent large audio-language models (LALMs) demonstrate strong cross-modal audio understanding; however, their behavior in disfluent child speech remains underexplored. We evaluate several state-of-the-art LALMs in two settings: an interview (mixed speakers) and a reading task (single child). The tasks are (i) single-channel source separation to isolate the child and (ii) child-only summarization that preserves clinically relevant disfluencies and avoids adult-speech leakage.

Evaluation combines Large Language Model (LLM) as a judge, human expert ratings, and BERTScore (F1), and we report agreement between models and between models and humans to assess reliability. Our findings delineate the conditions under which LALMs produce faithful child-only summaries from mixed audio and where they fail, offering practical guidance for clinical and educational deployments. We provide prompts and evaluation scripts to support replication.

Index Terms—  Large Audio-Language Model, LLM, Source Separation, Summarization, Child Speech, Stutter

## 1 Introduction

Recent advances in ASR and LALMs have opened up new opportunities for tools that can enhance pediatric health and education services \[[1](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib1), [2](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib2), [3](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib3)\]. However, robust modeling of children’s speech remains challenging. Relative to adults, children show higher and more variable fundamental frequency \[[4](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib4)\], evolving articulation and smaller or shifted vowel spaces \[[5](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib5)\], as well as slower and less stable speaking rates \[[6](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib6)\]. These developmental differences, combined with marked inter-child variability by age, context, and clinical presentation \[[7](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib7), [4](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib4)\], create persistent domain shifts that degrade adult-optimized ASR and downstream applications.

At the same time, LALMs have begun to demonstrate unified audio understanding, instruction following over long audio contexts, and speech-centric reasoning. Models such as Qwen2-Audio \[[8](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib8)\], SALMONN \[[9](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib9)\], and Audio Flamingo 3 \[[10](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib10)\] accept raw audio and natural-language instructions, exhibiting emergent abilities for multi-speaker comprehension and long-context summarization \[[11](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib11), [12](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib12)\]. Building on these developments, we consider a real and consequential use case: producing _child-only_ summaries from multi-speaker child–adult interviews, where faithfulness to the child’s content and avoidance of adult-speech leakage are critical.

Within clinical and educational settings, stuttering and other fluency disorders introduce additional phenomena repetitions, prolongations, blocks, and secondary behaviors—that stress both recognition and summarization \[[13](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib13)\]. Community resources, including UCLASS and FluencyBank (with recent timestamped updates), make possible both descriptive and predictive work on disfluency while underscoring the need for speaker-aware, content-faithful outputs that neither erase disfluency nor hallucinate information not present in the child’s speech \[[14](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib14), [15](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib15)\]. Because our downstream objective is a _child-only_ summary from mixed audio, the modeling challenge is not only “what was said” but also “_who_ said it.” Imperfect diarization or source separation can cause _adult-speech leakage_ \[[16](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib16)\], in which interviewer prompts are paraphrased or attributed to the child.

We propose and evaluate _speaker-purity-aware_ child-only summarization from mixed child–adult interviews. To our knowledge, this is the _first_ study to explore LALMs for child speech analysis in the presence of stuttering/fluency disorders. Concretely, we:

- •
  Formalize child-only summarization with explicit speaker purity constraints and instantiate two reproducible pipelines: text-first , audio-first (LALMs with child-focused prompting).
- •
  Introduce an evaluation framework that complements “LLM-as-judge” with expert reference summaries and task-grounded metrics: content-unit coverage, speaker leakage/purity, and faithfulness checks, alongside human ratings with inter-rater reliability.
- •
  Benchmark multiple audio LLMs and text baselines; and provide targeted error analyses (leakage, omission, hallucination) that identify conditions where LALMs produce the most faithful child-only summaries \[[17](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib17), [16](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib16)\].

Our results highlight practical choices for deploying LALM-based tools in pediatric contexts, including when to prefer text-first versus audio-first approaches, the importance of diarization/separation quality for leakage control, and which prompt designs best preserve disfluency while remaining concise, accurate, and clinically useful.

## 2 Background and Related Work

Traditional ASR systems are designed to convert speech into text \[[18](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib18)\]. Once converted, the text can then be used for other NLP tasks, such as summarization, question answering, and sentiment analysis \[[19](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib19)\]. Over time, ASR systems have become increasingly advanced. Modern models, such as Transformer-Transducer \[[20](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib20)\], Whisper \[[21](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib21)\], and Conformer \[[22](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib22)\], achieve near-human accuracy on adult speech but show substantial performance degradation on spontaneous or pediatric speech due to domain mismatch \[[23](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib23)\].

Recently, LALMs have emerged, combining powerful speech encoders with large language model decoders to understand and generate language directly from raw audio \[[24](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib24)\]. These models extend far beyond simple transcription: they learn to interpret nuanced acoustic cues—such as tone, pitch, and rhythm—and use these signals to reason, summarize, and generate coherent, context-aware responses \[[25](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib25), [26](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib26)\]. As highlighted in surveys by Peng et al. \[[25](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib25)\] and Yang et al. \[[26](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib26)\], this development marks a shift from traditional “speech-to-text” systems toward “speech-to-meaning” models that can interpret not only the words spoken but also the surrounding context, emotion, and intent.

Despite these advances, both ASR systems and LALMs continue to underperform on child speech, largely due to domain mismatch in adult-trained data \[[27](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib27), [28](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib28)\]. Recent efforts such as CHSER \[[29](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib29)\] and DRAF \[[30](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib30)\] have improved transcription through data augmentation and self-supervised adaptation, yet they remain focused on recognition accuracy rather than comprehensive multimodal understanding \[[25](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib25), [26](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib26)\].

Comprehensive understanding extends beyond recognition, encompassing source separation, summarization, and audio captioning \[[25](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib25), [26](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib26)\]. Source separation distinguishes a child’s voice from overlapping adults or background noise \[[31](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib31)\], while summarization condenses spoken content into concise, faithful representations. Audio captioning further describes the surrounding acoustic scene and speaker context \[[32](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib32)\]. By integrating these abilities through shared acoustic–semantic embeddings, LALMs achieve a deeper form of multimodal understanding \[[25](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib25), [26](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib26)\].

While Large Audio–Language Models (LALMs) have shown strong generalization across a range of domains \[[25](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib25), [32](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib32)\], their behavior on natural, disfluent child speech remains largely unexplored \[[28](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib28)\]. Existing benchmarks rarely test whether these models can effectively (a) separate a child’s voice from overlapping speakers or (b) generate accurate, child-focused summaries \[[25](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib25), [26](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib26)\]. Addressing this gap is essential for developing reliable and inclusive systems that can understand children’s speech in real-world clinical and educational contexts.

## 3 Methods

### 3.1 Tasks and Settings

We study two child–speech settings: (a) Reading, where a single child speaker reads aloud, and (b) Interview, a dialog between a child and an adult. We evaluate two capabilities of Large Audio–Language Models (LALMs): (1) _single-channel source separation_ in Interview audio, to isolate the child’s speech from mixed speakers; and (2) _child-only summarization_, to produce concise, clinically useful summaries without adult content and without over-normalizing disfluencies (repetitions, prolongations, blocks).

### 3.2 Dataset: FluencyBank English Voices

We use the FluencyBank English Voices–CWS corpus , a curated collection of video recordings and aligned transcripts of children who stutter (CWS) collected in the United States \[[15](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#bib.bib15)\]. The corpus was assembled by Nan Bernstein Ratner, with recruitment and logistical support from FRIENDS (a national organization for children and families who stutter) and the National Stuttering Association, and recordings were obtained at their annual meetings.

#### Participants and design.

The dataset comprises 22 child participants (CWS). Each participant completed two tasks: (i) a semi-structured interview (int) and (ii) a reading task (rdg). The interview follows a fixed six-question protocol eliciting experiences and perspectives about stuttering:¹¹1Prompts summarized from the corpus page: activities at the FRIENDS meeting; family reasons for attending; peer interactions; talking about one’s speech; what was learned about stuttering; speech therapy experiences and advice for future clinicians. The reading task uses grade-appropriate passages from the Stuttering Severity Instrument–4 (SSI–4) under license from the publisher.²²2Per corpus policy and publisher license, we do not reproduce SSI–4 passage text in this paper.

### 3.3 Models

We evaluate two families of systems: (i) _audio-first_ LALMs that operate directly on waveform inputs (Qwen2-Audio, SALMONN, GAMA, Audio Flamingo–3, Kimi Audio), and (ii) a _text-first_ baseline that transcribes and separates before summarizing (Whisper Large $`\rightarrow`$ pyannote.audio $`\rightarrow`$ Llama-3.2). For evaluation, we additionally use _LLM-as-Judge_ models (Qwen2-7B, Mistral-7B, Llama-3.2) under a shared rubric. ### 3.4 Prompts

We standardize prompts across systems and report them verbatim. Decoding is fixed unless otherwise noted: temperature$`=0.2`$, top_p$`=0.95`$, max tokens$`=512`$. The prompting techniques used were zeroshot for the interview and few shot for the LLM as a judge.

#### Interview and Reading summarization.

> System role: You are a clinical Speech–Language Pathologist.\
> Task: Produce a faithful, objective 3–sentence summary of the child’s _reading_ performance (content and difficulties) from the transcript/audio.\
> Schema: {”summary”: string}.\
> Guidelines: Keep the summary concise, faithful, and specific to the child’s speech. (Adult-filtering is typically unnecessary for reading items.)

#### LLM-as-Judge (evaluation).

> System role: You are an expert Speech–Language Pathologist and evaluation judge for child-speech summarization.\
> Instruction: Evaluate the Model Summary against the Reference Summary created by a baseline text LLM. Rate each criterion from 1 (very poor) to 5 (excellent): (1) Overall quality; (2) Fluency/Coherence; (3) Faithfulness/Factuality; (4) Coverage of child’s main points; (5) Speaker purity (child-only); (6) Usefulness (for SLPs/parents/researchers).\
> Inputs:\
> Reference Summary: {reference}\
> Model Summary: {summary}\
> Output (strict JSON):\
> {”score_overall”: int, ”score_fluency”: int, ”score_faithfulness”: int, ”score_coverage”: int, ”score_purity”: int, ”score_usefulness”: int, ”rationale”: string}

### 3.5 Evaluation

#### Automatic metrics.

We assess summary quality against baseline summary using BERTScore (F1) and ROUGE.¹⁰¹⁰10We report ROUGE–1/2/Lsum as F1, and include precision/recall for transparency. Concretely, for each system and item we compute: (i) ROUGE–1/2/Lsum (rouge1_f1, rouge2_f1, rougeLsum_f1) alongside precision, recall, and f1; and (ii) BERTScore–F1 as a semantic adequacy proxy. Uncertainty is estimated via a nonparametric bootstrap over items ($`1{,}000`$ resamples); we report f1_ci_low and f1_ci_high as 95% confidence intervals.

#### Human ratings and LLM-as-Judge.

Two human experts (speech/fluency) rate each summary on a shared rubric (faithfulness, coverage, coherence, usefulness, and speaker purity). In parallel, Qwen2–7B, Mistral–7B, and Llama–3.2 serve as LLM judges with the same rubric and anonymized, randomized system outputs.

Reliability and agreement. We quantify within-group reliability and cross-group agreement as follows:

- •
  LLM vs LLM: Pairwise correlations between judge models using Pearson_r (with $`p`$-values) and Spearman_r, computed per score dimension (overall, fluency, faithfulness, coverage, purity, usefulness) and per task (task $`\in`$ {Interview, Reading}). We also report Cohen_kappa after discretization when used.
- •
  LLM vs Human: Concordance between each judge model and the human mean via Pearson_r/Spearman_r and (when preferences are collected) accuracy and $`\kappa`$ on pairwise comparisons.

For all agreement statistics we include the sample size (n_samples) and 95% CIs via bootstrap. Task-level results are reported separately for Interview and Reading and then macro-averaged across tasks in the main Table [2](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#S3.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 3.6 Reproducibility ‣ 3 Methods ‣ Can Large Audio Language Models Understand Child Stuttering Speech? ## 4 Results

#### Overview.

We evaluate child–only summarization for Reading (single speaker) and Interview (mixed speakers). We report automatic metrics (ROUGE-1/2/Lsum, BERTScore), LLM-as-Judge ratings, human ratings, and agreement statistics. Task-level results are reported separately and then macro-averaged.

LLM-as-Judge Evaluation (Interview Task). As shown in Table [4](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#S3.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 3.6 Reproducibility ‣ 3 Methods ‣ Can Large Audio Language Models Understand Child Stuttering Speech? Speech Summarization, and Source separation") and Table [5](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#S3.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 3.6 Reproducibility ‣ 3 Methods ‣ Can Large Audio Language Models Understand Child Stuttering Speech? Speech Summarization, and Source separation"), AF3 achieved the highest ratings across all evaluation dimensions, demonstrating consistent superiority in overall quality ($`3.43\pm 0.39`$), fluency ($`4.28\pm 0.30`$), faithfulness ($`3.07\pm 0.30`$), purity ($`4.73\pm 0.10`$), and usefulness ($`3.43\pm 0.15`$). These results suggest that AF3’s interview summaries were consistently perceived as clearer, more accurate, and more relevant across all three LLM judges (Llama-3.2, Mistral, and Qwen). Kimi ranked second overall, showing strong fluency and usefulness but slightly lower faithfulness. SALMONN and Qwen exhibited moderate performance with greater variability, while GAMA received the lowest average scores, reflecting weaker content quality and stylistic coherence.

Automatic metrics. Table [8](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#S3.T8 "Table 8 ‣ 3.6 Reproducibility ‣ 3 Methods ‣ Can Large Audio Language Models Understand Child Stuttering Speech? Speech Summarization, and Source separation") reports semantic similarity (F1) between model-generated summaries and reference texts. Across both tasks, BERTScore values indicate relatively low absolute overlap, consistent with open-ended summarization settings where lexical diversity is high. For the Interview task, Kimi achieved the strongest alignment ($`F1=0.28`$), followed by AF3 ($`F1=0.23`$), indicating greater preservation of reference meaning. In contrast, GAMA showed minimal overlap ($`F1=0.05`$), reflecting weak semantic fidelity. For the Reading task, SALMONN led with the highest BERTScore ($`F1=0.30`$), suggesting accurate content retention, while Kimi underperformed ($`F1<0`$), likely due to short or lexically mismatched summaries. Overall, these findings suggest that Kimi generalizes better in conversational summarization (Interview), whereas SALMONN performs best for more structured text (Reading). AF3 demonstrate stable but mid-range performance across tasks, while GAMA consistently lags behind in semantic similarity.

LLM-as-Judge and human ratings. Table [6](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#S3.T6 "Table 6 ‣ 3.6 Reproducibility ‣ 3 Methods ‣ Can Large Audio Language Models Understand Child Stuttering Speech? Speech Summarization, and Source separation") summarizes the correspondence between human judgments and LLM-as-Judge scores for the Interview task. Across models, Llama 3.2 exhibited the highest alignment with human evaluations, achieving a moderate–strong Pearson correlation ($`r=0.56`$) and fair categorical agreement ($`\kappa=0.36`$), with 84.3% of items rated within $`\pm 1`$ point on the 5-point scale. In contrast, Mistral and Qwen showed weaker correlations ($`r\approx 0.34`$) and minimal categorical agreement ($`\kappa<0.10`$), though both maintained 73–78% within-$`\pm 1`$ consistency. Error analyses reinforced these trends, with Llama 3.2 yielding the lowest mean absolute error (0.58) and RMSE (0.93). Overall, these results indicate that Llama 3.2 provides the most human-aligned evaluations among the tested models, with agreement levels comparable to those reported in prior evaluation studies for inter-human reliability.

Agreement and reliability. Across both Reading and Interview tasks, the LLM-as-Judge evaluations showed strong rank consistency and moderate to substantial agreement across model pairs. For the Reading task (Table [7](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#S3.T7 "Table 7 ‣ 3.6 Reproducibility ‣ 3 Methods ‣ Can Large Audio Language Models Understand Child Stuttering Speech? Speech Summarization, and Source separation")), inter-judge Pearson correlations ranged from $`r=0.71`$ to $`0.79`$ and Kendall’s $`\tau`$ (not shown) was typically between $`0.65`$–$`0.77`$, indicating robust monotonic alignment of scores. Absolute agreement, captured by Cohen’s $`\kappa`$ and within-one-point agreement, was highest between Llama-3.2 and Mistral ($`\kappa\approx 0.39`$–$`0.46`$, within-1 $`\geq 0.92`$). In contrast, pairings involving Qwen achieved strong correlation but lower $`\kappa`$, suggesting consistent relative judgments but differences in calibration.

For the Interview task (Table [7](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20850v1#S3.T7 "Table 7 ‣ 3.6 Reproducibility ‣ 3 Methods ‣ Can Large Audio Language Models Understand Child Stuttering Speech? Speech Summarization, and Source separation")), all judge pairs again showed strong consistency ($`r=0.70`$–$`0.80`$). Llama-3.2 and Mistral achieved the strongest overall reliability ($`\kappa\approx 0.42`$–$`0.43`$, within-1 $`>0.98`$), while Qwen pairings demonstrated high within-1 agreement ($`\geq 0.74`$) but smaller $`\kappa`$, again reflecting systematic scoring offsets rather than disagreement in rank ordering. Overall, these results indicate that the LLM-as-Judge evaluations are highly consistent in ranking quality, though minor differences in absolute calibration persist across model pairs.

#### Significance.

Pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (per item, per task) indicated that AF3 significantly outperformed all other models on overall LLM-as-Judge and human evaluation scores ($`p_{\text{adj}}<.05`$). However, this superiority did not consistently extend to automatic metrics: ROUGE and BERTScore values did not rank AF3 highest, reflecting a partial divergence between lexical/semantic overlap and holistic human-judged quality. This contrast highlights that surface- and embedding-based metrics may underestimate aspects of coherence, clarity, and task relevance that are better captured by human or LLM-based evaluations.

## 5 Conclusion

This study presents one of the first comprehensive evalua- tions of Large Audio–Language Models (LALMs) on stut- tering and other disfluent forms of child speech. The models were examined across two key challenges: producing child- centered summaries free from adult interference and perform- ing single-channel speaker separation. To obtain a balanced and informative assessment, we combined automatic metrics with expert human evaluations and LLM-based judgments, allowing both content accuracy and speaker distinctiveness to be measured effectively.

Among the tested systems, Audio Flamingo 3 delivered the most natural and faithful summaries, clearly emphasizing the child’s speech. Kimi Audio and SALMONN performed closely behind in conversational and reading sessions, while GAMA and Qwen2-Audio showed more fluctuation and weaker semantic alignment. These differences reveal how current LALMs can still be affected by subtle changes in prompt wording and context. Although the ROUGE and BERTScore outcomes were moderate, the close match between human and LLM-judge ratings suggests that standard surface metrics often fail to capture the perceptual qualities that humans notice.

Overall, our findings show that modern audio–language models are capable of producing coherent, clinically meaningful summaries even from mixed and noisy recordings. Yet they continue to struggle with maintaining natural disfluencies and avoiding small factual drifts. Future work will aim to broaden the benchmark to include more accents and languages, and to refine prompting and feedback strategies for greater robustness and reliability.
