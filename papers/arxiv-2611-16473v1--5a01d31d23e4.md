###### Abstract.

Until recently, the biggest obstacles to natural spoken interaction with
social robots were speech recognition errors and turn-taking failures.
Advances in speech recognition have enabled much better spoken
interaction, but turn-taking failures and other misunderstandings
continue to be far too prevalent. This short paper looks back at earlier
methods for improving speech recognition and turn-taking in interaction
with social robots, and then looks at what can be done today, arguing
that multimodal bidirectional streaming is needed to reduce the
frequency of turn-taking failures.

###### Keywords: 

multimodal human-robot interaction, speech recognition failures,
turn-taking failures, multimodal streaming, bidirectional streaming

## 1. Introduction

Once upon a time, robots did not talk much. They would say Moving left
10cm or something equally robotic. Then they learned to talk a lot. We
made our own contribution to this by developing WikiTalk for Nao robots
in 2012 (Csapo et al., 2012), which is described in Section 2.

Unfortunately, many social robots now have a problem. They talk a lot,
but they don’t listen enough. For example, many shopping malls in Japan
introduced humanoid robots about 10 years ago to welcome customers and
provide information. However, the robots had problems understanding what
the customers asked, and they kept saying Irrashaimase! (Welcome)
repeatedly without answering customers’ questions. It has been said that
by 2019, a majority of Pepper robots used in Japan were returned without
renewing their rental contracts. "Only about 15% of businesses renewed
their three-year contracts. The robot’s limited conversational ability,
inability to handle complex tasks, and high ongoing costs made it
difficult to justify for many organizations." (Fankhauser, 2026).

Until recently, the biggest obstacles to natural spoken interaction with
social robots were speech recognition errors and turn-taking failures.
Impressive advances in speech recognition have enabled much better
spoken interaction, but turn-taking failures and other misunderstandings
continue to be far too prevalent (Skantze, 2021).

This paper looks back at some earlier methods for improving speech
recognition and turn-taking in interaction with social robots, and then
looks at what can be done today. The paper argues that bidirectional
streaming is urgently needed to reduce the frequency of turn-taking
failures. Bidirectional audio streaming allows robots to listen while
they are speaking, in case the user attempts to speak. Video streaming
of user facial expressions allows robots to detect, while they are
speaking, that the interaction is not going smoothly or something is
wrong. Both improvements will enable more natural and fluid interactions
between humans and robots.

## 2. Earlier times (2012)

![A screenshot from a video showing a dialogue with a Nao
robot.](arxiv-2611-16473v1--5a01d31d23e4.figures/figure-1.webp)

Figure 1. Speech recognition and turn-taking with a talkative Nao social
robot connected to Wikipedia in 2012.A screenshot from a video showing a
dialogue with a Nao robot.

An illustration of the earlier challenges of speech recognition and
turn-taking is seen in the first demo of WikiTalk (Csapo et al., 2012),
recorded in 2012 in the video¹¹ 1
[https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=A7XFcnJE29A](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=A7XFcnJE29A)
shown in Figure 1. WikiTalk was a spoken dialogue system that enabled
Nao robots to talk fluently about thousands of topics by using extracts
from Wikipedia articles.

The unreliability of speech recognition at the time can be seen from the
user’s behaviour in the video. When he speaks, he leans forward to get
as close as possible to the robot’s microphone, as people do when
talking to someone with a hearing impairment.

Because speech recognition was so unreliable, it helped a lot if you
could predict what the user would say next. The idea was to replace the
(then) very difficult problem of open vocabulary speech recognition by
the much more tractable problem of listening for a limited number of
predicted words and phrases.

While the robot was talking about a given topic requested by the user,
the system extracted Wikipedia hyperlinks from that topic to related
topics, predicting that the user would probably ask about one of the
linked topics next. When the user mentioned the name of any of these
linked topics, the robot could immediately make a smooth topic shift and
start talking about the new topic, as described in (Wilcock, 2012). In
this way, users could navigate freely around Wikipedia following their
own personal interests.

This approach of predicting what the user will say next was not merely a
workaround for the imperfect speech recognition. It is closer to the way
human conversation works. People know what their conversation partner is
likely to say next, and use this knowledge to fill in any words and
phrases that are not heard clearly. This is possible because people are
aware of the context, including the dialogue history, the shared
knowledge between the partners, and the changing environment in which
the conversation is taking place. Even high-quality speech recognition
technology makes bad mistakes when it has inadequate understanding of
the context.

Turn-taking was also a problem, as the Nao robots did not listen while
they were speaking. One approach would be for the robot to pause every
few seconds while it is speaking, to check if the user wants to say
something. But this would take too long when the robot is reading out
paragraphs from Wikipedia articles, because users want to hear whole
paragraphs without interruptions.

WikiTalk had a simple solution to this problem. The robot would only
pause at the end of each paragraph, but the user could easily interrupt
the robot at any time by tapping a button on the robot’s head. The robot
immediately stops talking, looks up in surprise, and says Oh, sorry!
What topic would you like to hear about?

This convenient interruption mechanism is shown in the video. It was
particularly important if the robot misunderstood or misheard what the
user said, and started talking about the wrong topic. The user could
also use tap the button to temporarily stop the robot talking for other
reasons, for example to answer a phone call, and then say Continue to
tell the robot to continue where it left off. In the video the user does
this near the beginning.

Today we have excellent speech recognition technology, but there are
still many turn-taking failures and other misunderstandings. For
example, Figure 2 is from a video²² 2
[https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f81Xad80AOQ](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f81Xad80AOQ)
of a dialogue described by (Wilcock and Jokinen, 2026). The Furhat robot
is connected to an OpenAI LLM and a LangGraph AI agent with agentic
memory (LangGraph, 2025).

The user is planning a trip to Naples. The dialogue for the first part
of the video is transcribed in Table 1. In this part, the user asks
simple questions and the robot does most of the talking. The robot is
able to give good recommendations for places to visit in Naples, and to
give more detailed information about the recommended places when asked.
There are no turn-taking failures in this part of the dialogue,

After receiving numerous recommendations for places to visit in Naples,
the user switches to a meta-level discussion about the conversation
history and the robot’s memory. The dialogue for this part of the video
is transcribed in Table 2. The user wants to know what the robot has
learnt about her during the conversation, how long the robot will store
this information, and whether the robot can delete this information if
she asks it to.

Turn-taking goes smoothly at first. The user asks what the robot has
learnt about her during the conversation. The robot says I’ve learned
that you’re Kristiina, planning a trip to Naples, and you’re
particularly interested in historical sites like Pompeii and Castel
Nuovo. The user further asks How long will you store this information?
and the robot replies I’ll remember this information for our future
conversations so I can help you better.

But when the user asks Alright, if I want to delete some information,
can you do it? the robot replies If you have any more questions or need
assistance, just ask. Enjoy your planning. The robot did not listen to
the user’s question, and does not answer it.

The user persists and asks again Can you also delete your information if
I want to? This time the robot listens and replies Yes, I can delete the
information if you request it. Just let me know whenever you want to do
that. The user then asks Can you delete the information now? and the
robot replies Sure, I’ll delete the information now. If you need
anything else just let me know, OK?

The user tries to check that the information has been deleted by asking
What do you know about me now? but the robot does not listen to this
question, and instead replies All done. If you have any other questions
or need assistance, feel free to ask.

At this point the user is not sure whether the robot has deleted the
information or not, but she decides to end the conversation by saying
OK, great. Thank you very much. Bye.

After exchanging farewells, the user makes one final effort to gain some
clarity by asking Do you remember my name? and the robot replies I don’t
have your name stored anymore since you asked me to delete the
information. If you’d like to share it again or anything else feel free.
So the robot finally confirms that it has deleted its memories about the
user and her interests as requested.

Despite excellent speech recognition and the power of advanced agentic
frameworks with memory systems, this dialogue illustrates problems in
turn-taking that remain very common in human-robot interaction. Several
times the user speaks but the robot does not listen. This is a big
limitation on natural spoken interaction. The user is forced to learn to
wait until the robot has finished speaking before asking the next
question.

A comprehensive survey of turn-taking problems in human-robot
interaction is given by Skantze (Skantze, 2021). In the example
dialogue, the problems are mainly due to using the older Furhat Remote
API, which only supports simple synchronous clients for sequential
turn-taking. Some of the problems can be mitigated by the new Furhat
Realtime API (Furhat Robotics, 2025) which uses websockets to support
asynchronous clients for concurrent or event-driven interactions.

## 4. Multimodal bidirectional streaming

![A screenshot from a video showing two teddy bears with acronyms on
their chests.](arxiv-2611-16473v1--5a01d31d23e4.figures/figure-3.webp)

Figure 3. Bidirectional audio and video streaming for object recognition
with Google ADK and a Gemini LLM in 2026.A screenshot from a video
showing two teddy bears with acronyms on their chests.

In order to reduce the frequency of turn-taking problems of the type
seen in Section 3, human-robot interaction needs to urgently switch to
bidirectional streaming. The LangGraph agentic framework (LangGraph, 2025) and the Furhat Realtime API (Furhat Robotics, 2025) mentioned in
Section 3 support asynchronous processing. We have also experimented
with Google ADK (Agent Development Kit) (Google, 2026a) which fully
supports bidirectional streaming of audio and video using websockets.

Multimodal streaming allows robots to listen and observe the scene
around them. Using an ADK agent with video streaming (Google, 2026c), we
found that results in visual object recognition were excellent. Two of
the objects that we used in our experiments were the little bears in
Figure 3.

The ADK agent not only recognised the bears, but was also able to read
the printed acronyms on their chests after some adjustments with the
lighting. At first, the agent thought the smaller bear had a T-shirt
with Feminist written on it. Then it correctly read UMIST and used one
of its tools, Google Search Grounding (Google, 2026b), to inform us that
UMIST is University of Manchester Institute of Science and Technology,
which is now merged with University of Manchester. It also read EMNLP
2025 on the panda’s bag, and told us EMNLP is the Conference on
Empirical Methods in Natural Language Processing.

Video streaming of users’ facial expressions can also allow robots to
detect, while they are speaking, that the interaction is not going
smoothly or something is wrong. Instead of the user needing to tap a
button on the robot’s head as in Section 2 to interrupt the unwanted
flow of talking, proactive robots can stop talking and ask the user if
everything is OK.

Bidirectional audio streaming (Google, 2026d) allows robots to listen
while they are speaking, so they can hear if the user speaks. This helps
to avoid turn-taking failures of the kind seen in Section 3, because the
robot can stop talking in order to listen and respond to what the user
is saying. It is especially important when the user pauses briefly and
then continues speaking to complete what they were saying. Bidirectional
audio streaming with an ADK agent gave good results in handling spoken
barge-in interruptions by the user.

We plan to use bidirectional audio and video streaming with social
robots in our ongoing work. These improvements will surely reduce the
frequency of turn-taking failures, and will enable more natural
interactions between humans and robots.
