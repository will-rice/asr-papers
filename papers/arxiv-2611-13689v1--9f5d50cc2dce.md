# Walrus Technical Report

Alkis Koudounas    Osamu Take    Hayato Futami Affiliation: Quentin
Jodelet    Emiru Tsunoo Affiliation: Speech & Language AI Lab
Affiliation: Sony Group Corporation Affiliation: Tokyo, Japan

###### Abstract

We present Walrus, a unified 8B speech-and-audio foundation model
handling speech recognition (including code-switching and multi-talker
speech), speech-to-text and speech-to-speech translation,
text-to-speech, emotion recognition, audio captioning, and general audio
understanding. Our main contribution is not a new architecture but a
data-centric training recipe; we use a standard decoder-only backbone
initialized from Gemma 4, so that gains can be traced to the data. For
speech generation, Walrus predicts low-bitrate discrete speech tokens,
and a lightweight token-to-waveform decoder converts them into
high-fidelity audio. The recipe is a three-stage data curriculum that
moves from broad multilingual data to multi-talker, code-switching, and
domain-specific data. At each stage, task-aware and dataset-aware
weighting balances tasks with very different token budgets and protects
core skills from forgetting. Trained on over 600M samples covering more
than 20 tasks and 100 languages, Walrus leads on most multilingual,
multi-talker, and code-switching ASR, speech translation, and audio
understanding benchmarks, outperforming both open speech language models
of comparable size and dedicated task-specific models. On most
benchmarks, it even matches or surpasses the $`4\times`$ larger
Qwen3-Omni’s performance. Results suggest that data engineering is a
primary driver of quality in multi-task speech and audio modeling.

## 1 Introduction

Speech is a natural interface between people and machines, and real
applications rarely need only one skill. A meeting assistant must
transcribe overlapping speakers and keep track of who said what; a
dubbing system must translate speech into another language and speak it
back in a natural voice; a media platform must transcribe noisy
entertainment audio and describe its music and sound effects. Across
these settings, systems also need to recognize emotion, answer questions
about audio, and handle speakers who switch languages mid-sentence Ji et
al. (2024); La Quatra et al. (2024); Peng et al. (2026a); Wang et al.
(2025). Training and serving a separate model for each task is costly,
and the resulting pipelines pass errors from one stage to the next; a
single model that covers all of these tasks is cheaper to deploy and
simpler to maintain.

Speech Large Language Models (SpeechLLMs) are a promising path to such a
model. Recent work improves them by scaling the model or redesigning the
architecture Abouelenin et al. (2025); Ding et al. (2025); Ghosh et al.
(2026); Intelligence (2025); Xu et al. (2025a). Far less is reported
about the data: how it is cleaned, mixed, and scheduled across tasks.
This gap matters because the hardest cases are data problems:
code-switched speech, overlapping speakers, entertainment audio, and
reasoning-focused audio questions are rare in public corpora. Tasks also
differ greatly in token budget, so a naive mixture lets long generative
outputs dominate the loss and weakens core recognition. We take a
different position: we argue that careful data engineering is a primary
driver of quality, and that it can deliver state-of-the-art results
without any new architecture. We demonstrate this by holding the
architecture fixed and putting all design effort into the data.

We present Walrus, a unified speech-and-audio foundation model with
about 8B parameters. Walrus is initialized from the Gemma 4 E4B model¹¹
1
[huggingface.co/google/gemma-4-E4B](https://huggingface.co/google/gemma-4-E4B) Team
et al. (2026) and keeps the backbone architecture unchanged. It supports
automatic speech recognition (ASR), code-switching ASR (CS-ASR),
multi-talker ASR (MT-ASR) with speaker diarization, machine translation
(MT), automatic speech translation (AST), speech-to-speech translation
(S2ST), text-to-speech (TTS), speech emotion recognition (SER), audio
captioning, closed captioning, and general audio understanding. For
speech generation, Walrus predicts low-bitrate discrete speech tokens at
only 325 bits per second; a lightweight token-to-waveform decoder
converts these tokens into high-fidelity audio.

The core of our work is a three-stage data curriculum, where each stage
adds new data and new tasks on top of a stable foundation. We pair the
curriculum with task-aware and dataset-aware weighting to balance tasks
with very different token budgets and keep core skills strong as the
model learns harder tasks. From this curriculum, we build two variants:
a Commercial-Ready (CR) model trained only on commercially usable data,
and a Research (R) model trained on the full curriculum for the best
overall quality. The recipe leads to large and consistent gains over
strong open SpeechLLMs of comparable size. Figure 2 summarizes
performance across recognition, translation, and understanding. Both
Walrus variants push the frontier beyond the best previous system on
most axes.

Our main contributions are as follows. First, we present a data-centric
recipe for training a multi-task speech and audio model on a fixed
backbone. Second, we design a three-stage curriculum with task-aware and
dataset-aware weighting, which grows from broad multilingual coverage to
complex and domain-specific data. Third, we describe the data pipelines
behind the hardest tasks: synthetic code-switching, multi-talker
mixtures, entertainment speech, conversational context, closed
captioning, and audio reasoning. Finally, we show through extensive
evaluation that this recipe reaches state-of-the-art results on most
benchmarks, and we provide a commercial-ready variant trained only on
commercially usable data.

## 2 Model

The Walrus architecture is deliberately simple; our contribution is the
data strategy, not the network design. This section describes the
backbone (Sec. §2.1, including the audio representation used for input
and output and the token-to-waveform decoder, and the supported tasks
(Sec. §2.2). Figure 1 shows how the components connect.

### 2.1 Architecture

Backbone. Walrus is initialized from Gemma 4 E4B,1 a decoder-only
Transformer with about 4B effective parameters ($`\approx`$ 8B in total,
including embeddings). We keep the architecture unchanged and fine-tune
all parameters on our data curriculum, using scaled dot-product
attention (SDPA) for efficient training and inference. A single set of
weights serves every task, and tasks are selected through
natural-language prompts.

Speech input. Walrus keeps the speech input design of Gemma 4 E4B; the
Universal Speech Model (USM) encoder Zhang et al. (2023) converts the
input speech into continuous representations, which are then fed to the
decoder together with the text prompt. We unfreeze the encoder during
fine-tuning, so that it adapts to our audio domains.

Speech output. For TTS and S2ST, Walrus generates discrete speech tokens
autoregressively; we extend the backbone vocabulary with 8,192 dedicated
speech tokens. Such tokens are then converted back into a waveform via a
separate decoder, which we adopt from Futami et al. (2026). It consists
of a single-layer vector quantization (VQ) tokenizer and a GAN-based
vocoder. Decoupling token prediction from waveform synthesis keeps the
backbone lightweight and lets the decoder specialize in high-fidelity
generation and speaker transfer.

Speech VQ tokenizer. The tokenizer is a single-layer VQ module trained
to reconstruct self-supervised speech features. The target features come
from the 20th layer of a pre-trained W2v-BERT 2.0 encoder Barrault et
al. (2023), which empirically best captures semantic content. A strided
convolution reduces the feature rate from 50 Hz to 25 Hz; a three-layer
Conformer Gulati et al. (2020) encodes the features, and a single
codebook with 8,192 entries in a 512-dimensional space quantizes them. A
symmetric Conformer decoder reconstructs the features. We train the
module end-to-end with a VQ-VAE objective Van Den Oord et al. (2017),
which combines a cosine reconstruction loss and a commitment loss. The
resulting stream has 25 tokens per second at 13 bits each
($`\log_{2}8192=13`$), which gives a bit-rate of 325 bits per second.

Vocoder. The GAN-based vocoder converts the VQ speech tokens into a
waveform in a single non-autoregressive step. It builds on UniCATS
(CTX-vec2wav) Du et al. (2024) and replaces the generator with
Vocos Siuzdak (2023) for higher synthesis quality. The VQ tokens are
first mapped back to continuous features by the VQ decoder, and then
converted onto waveform through the Vocos decoder. The vocoder is
additionally conditioned on the source speech, using features from the
8th layer of W2v-BERT 2.0. Lower layers of the encoder encode speaker
identity more strongly, so this conditioning transfers the source
speaker identity and prosody to the output. It also relaxes the data
requirement for expressive generation, because the model no longer needs
source and target recordings with matched speaker and prosody. We train
the vocoder as in Futami et al. (2026), with multi-period and multi-band
discriminators, a mel-spectrogram loss, a feature-matching loss Kong et
al. (2020); Kumar et al. (2023), and a multi-resolution spectral
loss Yamamoto et al. (2020).

### 2.2 Supported tasks

Walrus handles a broad range of speech and audio tasks; Table 1 groups
them into four families. Each task is specified through a
natural-language prompt at both training and inference time. During
training, we draw the prompt for each sample from a pool of
task-specific templates, which improves robustness to prompt wording;
Appendix §A lists the most frequently used prompt for each task.

The task families span the full pipeline from recognition to generation.

Recognition. This family covers standard ASR together with all its
conditioned variants. Context-conditioned ASR receives preceding text as
discourse context, while keyword-conditioned ASR receives a list of
candidate keywords to bias transcription toward salient entities,
including names and domain terms. Timestamp-aware ASR predicts word or
segment times, and a timestamp QA variant answers what is said within a
given time span. The family also includes code-switching ASR (CS-ASR),
which handles utterances that mix two languages within a single sentence
and transcribes each word in the phonetics of its own language. Finally,
it covers multi-talker ASR (MT-ASR) for audio with up to 4 speakers:
overlapping-speech ASR that produces a speaker-attributed transcription,
target-speaker ASR that transcribes only a chosen speaker, and speaker
diarization. We also support chain-of-thought variants, which first
estimate the number of speakers and then transcribe each one, together
with timestamped multi-talker transcription.

Translation. This family covers text-to-text machine translation (MT),
speech-to-text translation (AST), and speech-to-speech translation
(S2ST). Both AST and S2ST are supported in direct and cascaded forms:
the direct form maps source speech to the target in one step, while the
cascaded form first transcribes and then translates (and eventually
synthesizes). Context- and keyword-conditioned AST and S2ST variants
carry the same discourse and entity hints as their recognition
counterparts.

Generation. This family covers text-to-speech (TTS). Together with S2ST,
generative tasks emit the low-bitrate discrete speech tokens described
in Sec. §2.1, which the decoder then renders into audio.

Understanding. This family covers audio captioning, closed captioning
(CC), audio question answering, audio reasoning, and speech emotion
recognition (SER). Closed captioning spans several granularities, from a
simple list of speech/non-speech events up to a full structured scene
summary with speakers, sound effects, music, and timing (similar
to Kumar et al. (2026b)). Audio reasoning covers five skill families,
spanning temporal, speech-in-sound, speaker, spatial, and
instruction-following questions (Sec. §3.8.5).

## 3 Data curriculum

The data curriculum is the core of Walrus; we hold the architecture
fixed, so that gains can be traced to the data. This section describes
how we compose, weight, and stage the training data across all four task
families. We first state our design principles and the resulting task
distribution (Sec. §3.1-§3.2). We then describe the shared training
setup (Sec. §3.3), the three stages (Sec. §3.4 to Sec. §3.6), and the
two trained variants (Sec. §3.7). Finally, we describe the
data-preparation pipelines (Sec. §3.8).

### 3.1 Design principles

Our data strategy follows three principles that apply to every task
family.

Grow from broad and clean to complex and specialized. Early training
uses well-known, high-quality corpora that teach stable core skills
while retaining Gemma 4’s foundation capabilities. Later training adds
harder data, such as overlapping speech, code-switching, and in-domain
entertainment synthetic audio. This order lets the model learn easy
skills before it faces difficult ones.

Balance the mix with explicit weights. Tasks differ greatly in the
number of tokens they produce: generative tasks such as TTS and S2ST
emit long token sequences, so they can dominate the training loss;
recognition tasks emit far fewer tokens, yet they form the backbone of
the model. We therefore give generative tasks low weights in the early
stages, and raise them only after the recognition core is stable.

Protect core skills at every stage. We keep foundation data in all three
stages, and ASR keeps the highest task weight in every stage. As a
result, adding new tasks in later stages does not erode the recognition
skills built in the first stage.

### 3.3 Common setup

All three stages share the same optimization setup. We use the AdamW
optimizer with $`\beta_{1}=0.9`$, $`\beta_{2}=0.95`$,
$`\epsilon=10^{-8}`$, and a weight decay of 0.1. We clip the gradient
norm to 1.0 and use scaled dot-product attention (SDPA) for efficient
training and inference. We stream the data from WebDataset shards. We
use a per-device batch size of 8 with 2 gradient accumulation steps, and
a maximum sequence length of 1,024 tokens; on 128 GPUs, this gives an
effective batch size of 2,048 samples. Table 2 lists the learning rate
and schedule of each stage.

We shape the data mix with two independent controls: task weights scale
the contribution of each task to the loss, whereas dataset weights scale
how often each dataset is sampled, so a weight of 0.5 halves the
effective size of a corpus in the mixture. Together, the two controls
let us set the effective data distribution independently of the raw
corpus sizes. Table 3 summarizes the task weights of the three stages.

### 3.4 Stage 1: Foundation

The first stage builds a broad multilingual foundation across all four
task families. It covers ASR, MT, AST, S2ST, TTS, SER, audio captioning,
and audio QA, in many languages. It does not yet include timestamp ASR,
code-switching ASR, multi-talker ASR, diarization, or closed captioning.

We draw on well-established public corpora and internal data. For speech
recognition and translation, these comprehend CommonVoice (CV14) Ardila
et al. (2020), VoxPopuli Wang et al. (2021), LibriSpeech Panayotov et
al. (2015) and Multilingual LibriSpeech Pratap et al. (2020),
FLEURS Conneau et al. (2023), CoVoST-2 Wang et al. (2020), AISHELL-1 Bu
et al. (2017), CSJ Maekawa (2003), YODAS-Granary Rao Koluguri et al.
(2025), and IndicVoices-ST Javed et al. (2024). For TTS, we use
LibriTTS Zen et al. (2019), VCTK Veaux et al. (2013), and AISHELL-3 Shi
et al. (2020). We further leverage internal datasets for audio
captioning, and a small subset of SIFT-50M Pandey et al. (2025) for
instruction following. We clean the transcripts as described in
Sec. §3.8.

The task weights make recognition the priority (Table 3). ASR has the
highest weight (5.0), followed by context and keyword ASR (3.0). AST has
a weight of 2.0, and MT and S2ST have a weight of 0.5 each. TTS has a
weight of only 0.25, so that its long token sequences do not dominate
the loss. Audio QA, audio captioning, and SER have a weight of 1.0 each.
All datasets are sampled at their natural size, and we do not yet apply
dataset weights. This stage teaches stable core skills before we
introduce harder tasks.

### 3.5 Stage 2: Expansion

The second stage starts from the Stage-1 checkpoint and adds new tasks
to every family. The foundation data stays in the mix with reduced
dataset weights, so that the new data has room in the mixture.

In recognition, we add multi-talker and target-speaker ASR. The data
comes from real meetings (AMI Kraaij et al. (2005), AliMeeting Yu et al.
(2022), and AISHELL-4 Fu et al. (2021)) and from simulated mixtures
(LibriMix Cosentino et al. (2020)). We also add timestamp prediction,
diarization, and synthetic code-switching ASR. For understanding, we add
synthetic closed captioning and richer audio QA, including additional
SIFT-50M samples Pandey et al. (2025) and MECAT Niu et al. (2025). We
also add the UltraVoice Tu et al. (2025) and MSP-Podcast Busso et al.
(2026) corpora, which provide instruction-style speech and emotion data.
Across recognition and translation, we add dialogue-level context tasks
built from synthetic dialogues (Sec. §3.8.3). This stage teaches the
model to handle overlapping speakers, mixed languages, timing, and
conversational context.

We also introduce dataset weights in this phase. They let us lower the
influence of large or redundant corpora, so that these corpora do not
crowd out smaller, high-value data. For example, we halve the weight of
very large ASR corpora such as Common Voice and YODAS-Granary, while
giving the newly introduced tasks full weight. The task weights of the
shared tasks stay the same as in Stage 1, and the new tasks get a task
weight of 1.0; ASR therefore keeps the highest task weight.

### 3.6 Stage 3: Specialization

The third stage starts from the Stage-2 checkpoint; it keeps the data of
the first two stages and adds new data that closes specific gaps and
covers target domains. This also includes corpora that are not
commercially usable.

In recognition, we add synthetic entertainment speech, low-resource
languages, and more code-switching data. We also add a large number of
synthetic multi-talker mixtures with two to four speakers, covering
seven languages: Chinese, English, French, German, Italian, Japanese,
and Spanish. In translation, we add more S2ST data:
SpeechMatrix Duquenne et al. (2023), Europarl-ST Iranzo-Sánchez et al.
(2020), and internal dubbing-aligned data built with SpeechVecAlign Meng
and Koehn (2025). For closed captioning, we add the remaining segment
libraries (Sec. §3.8.5).

We halve the learning rate to $`1\times 10^{-6}`$, because a model with
strong core skills needs refinement rather than large updates. We also
rebalance the task weights to strengthen generation and understanding:
we lower the ASR weight from 5.0 to 4.0, while raising the weights of
TTS (0.25 to 2.0), S2ST (0.5 to 2.0), audio captioning, closed
captioning, and SER (each 1.0 to 2.0). The ASR weight stays the highest,
so recognition remains the core of the model. Sec. §3.8 describes the
data-preparation and filtering pipelines for these corpora.

### 3.7 Training scale and variants

Walrus is trained on over 600M samples that cover more than 20 task
variants and 100 languages. The run has three consecutive stages; each
one is initialized from the previous checkpoint and uses a different
random seed, which changes the data shuffling. Training takes about 30
days on 128 NVIDIA H100 GPUs, which is about 92,000 GPU hours.

We derive two variants from the curriculum. The Commercial-Ready (CR)
variant is the Stage-2 checkpoint, trained only on commercially usable
data, making it suitable for deployment. The Research (R) variant is the
Stage-3 final checkpoint; it uses all data, including non-commercial
corpora, and delivers the best overall quality, achieving
state-of-the-art performance.

The two variants share the same backbone, but differ in training stage,
data license, and task weights. The research variant improves on the
commercial-ready variant on every benchmark in Table 4. The largest
gains are in CS-ASR (about 19 points lower CER on CS-FLEURS read
speech), internal entertainment ASR (about 9 points lower average WER
across 6 languages), and MMAU-Pro (more than 20 points higher accuracy).
These gains come from Stage 3, which adds high-quality data while
rebalancing the task weights.

### 3.8 Data preparation

Raw corpora are rarely usable as they are, and public data is missing
for several of our tasks. We therefore clean the source data and build
task-specific supervision for every task family; where suitable data
does not exist at scale, we generate it with dedicated pipelines. This
applies to code-switching, multi-talker conversation, context- and
keyword-conditioned recognition and translation, closed captioning, and
reasoning-focused audio QA.

The following subsections describe these pipelines. Sec. §3.8.1
describes transcript cleaning, which applies to all speech corpora.
Sec. §3.8.2 covers recognition, Sec. §3.8.3 summarizes synthetic
dialogues, Sec. §3.8.4 covers translation, and Sec. §3.8.5 highlights
understanding. Appendix §A lists example task prompts, and Appendix §B
summarizes all pipelines in one table.

#### 3.8.1 Transcript cleaning

We apply two steps to the transcripts of our speech corpora: restoration
of casing and punctuation, and text normalization. Clean and readable
text benefits every task that uses these transcripts.

##### Casing and punctuation restoration.

Many ASR corpora provide only lowercase transcripts without punctuation.
A model trained on such text learns to drop casing and punctuation,
which hurts readability and downstream tasks. We restore both with a
large language model, Qwen3.5-9B,⁴⁴ 4
[huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.5-9B](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.5-9B)
used as a text normalizer. For each transcript, we build a few-shot
prompt with language-specific examples. The prompt has one strict rule:
add casing and punctuation only, and never add, remove, or change a
word. We run the restoration separately for each language, and we skip
transcripts that already contain mixed case.

A language model can silently rewrite words, so we verify every output:
we remove casing and punctuation from the original and the restored
transcript, and we compute the character error rate between them. We
keep a restored transcript only if this CER is at most 0.05: this limits
the edits to the surface form and filters out outputs where the model
changed the content. We also discard outputs whose length differs too
much from the original, as a second guard against hallucination. The
filter retains the large majority of samples (90-95% for most datasets)
while removing the few cases where the normalizer altered content.

##### Text normalization.

After restoration, we apply a normalization pipeline with separate rules
for three language groups: European languages, CJK languages (Chinese,
Japanese, and Korean), and others. These scripts differ in spacing,
punctuation, and digit conventions, so each group needs its own rules;
we tuned the rules by manually inspecting samples from each group. The
pipeline gives consistent and readable references across the
multilingual mix.

#### 3.8.2 Recognition supervision

This subsection describes the data for the recognition tasks that need
dedicated pipelines. Context ASR and keyword ASR are built from cleaned
ASR corpora. Code-switching ASR is fully synthetic, and multi-talker ASR
combines real and simulated recordings. Entertainment speech combines
curated available data with synthetic augmentation. Sec. §3.8.3 adds
more context- and keyword-conditioned data from synthetic dialogues.

##### Context ASR.

Standard ASR treats each utterance on its own, but real speech depends
on what was said before. We build context ASR data from the document
structure of read-speech corpora. We group utterances by chapter (for
MLS Pratap et al. (2020)) or by domain (for internal datasets), and sort
them in reading order. For each target utterance, we randomly attach
between one and five preceding utterances as text context. The model is
then asked to transcribe the target utterance given this context. This
teaches the model to use prior discourse, which helps with proper nouns,
topic-specific vocabulary, and consistency across a sequence of
utterances.

##### Keyword ASR.

In keyword-biased ASR, the model receives a list of candidate keywords
together with the audio. We extract candidate keywords from transcripts
using named-entity recognition, keeping people, places, organizations,
and other proper nouns. We normalize and de-duplicate the entities, and
keep only those that appear in at least two utterances, which removes
rare noise. For each utterance, the keyword list contains the entities
that the utterance actually includes (positives) and randomly sampled
entities that it does not contain (distractors). We draw the list size
from $`\{0,10,20,50,100\}`$, so that the model sees short and long
keyword lists, as well as no list at all. We further shuffle each list,
so that the position of a keyword carries no information. This teaches
the model to use helpful keywords and to ignore irrelevant ones, which
matters for domain-specific speech.

##### Code-switching ASR.

Code-switched speech, where a speaker mixes two languages in one
utterance, is rare in public corpora. We therefore generate both the
text and the audio in three steps: code-switched text generation,
segment-based speech synthesis, and quality filtering. We cover 13
language pairs, with a focus on English and Japanese mixed with European
and Asian languages.

Text generation. We generate the sentences with a large language model,
Gemma 4 26B.⁵⁵ 5
[huggingface.co/google/gemma-4-26B-A4B-it](https://huggingface.co/google/gemma-4-26B-A4B-it)
For each language pair, we prompt the model to write one natural
sentence that is mostly in a matrix language with short embedded phrases
from the other language. The prompt enforces realistic switching: it
allows switches only where a bilingual speaker would use them, such as
technical terms, loanwords, or emotional expressions, and limits each
switch to a few words. We guide the model with hand-written few-shot
examples for each language pair and each switching direction. To
increase diversity, we vary the topic across 25 everyday domains, such
as work, travel, food, and technology. After generation, we remove
duplicate sentences so that each text is unique.

Speech synthesis. A single-language TTS model cannot pronounce a mixed
sentence reliably, because each word must follow the phonetics of its
own language; we therefore synthesize each sentence in monolingual
segments.⁶⁶ 6 We employ Qwen3-TTS
([huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-TTS-12Hz-1.7B-Base](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-TTS-12Hz-1.7B-Base))
and a fast in-house variant of Chatterbox-TTS
([github.com/resemble-ai/chatterbox](https://github.com/resemble-ai/chatterbox)).
We decide which to use depending on the language pair, following
preliminary results from a pilot study. First, we detect which words
belong to the embedded language, using a per-language word list built
from the generated data. Next, we group consecutive words of the same
language into segments, and we merge very short segments into their
neighbors to avoid irregular, discontinuous audio. Then, we synthesize
each segment with the correct language tag, and we join the segments
with a random short silence gap. Every word in the resulting audio
carries the phonetics of its own language.

Speaker control. For each utterance, we sample one reference speaker
from a pool of high-quality FLEURS speakers of the matrix language. We
select the pool with the NISQA quality score Mittag et al. (2021), and
we keep only clean, high-fidelity recordings. We clone the reference
voice for all segments of the utterance, so that the whole utterance has
one consistent speaker. Sampling a new speaker for each utterance gives
broad speaker variety across the dataset.

Quality filtering. We apply two filters to the synthesized speech.
First, we score the naturalness of each utterance with NISQA, and we
discard utterances below a 3.50 threshold. Second, we check the
intelligibility; we transcribe each utterance with Whisper Large-v3
Turbo⁷⁷ 7
[huggingface.co/openai/whisper-large-v3-turbo](https://huggingface.co/openai/whisper-large-v3-turbo) Radford
et al. (2023) and compute the WER against the intended text (or CER,
depending on the language). We discard utterances whose error rate is
above a 5% threshold. The remaining audio and its known code-switched
text form the training pairs.

##### Multi-talker ASR.

Real-world conversations and media content involve multiple speakers
with overlapping speech. We build data for four related tasks:
overlapping-speech ASR with speaker-attributed output, target-speaker
ASR, speaker diarization, and timestamped variants of these tasks. With
this data, the model learns to transcribe the content, to separate the
speakers, and to predict when each speaker talks. All clips are at most
30 seconds long, which is the input limit of Walrus.

We combine real and simulated conversations. The real recordings come
from AMI Kraaij et al. (2005), AliMeeting Yu et al. (2022), and
AISHELL-4 Fu et al. (2021). We split the full recordings into 30-second
clips with the DiCoW recipe⁸⁸ 8
[github.com/BUTSpeechFIT/mt-asr-data-prep.git](https://github.com/BUTSpeechFIT/mt-asr-data-prep.git) Polok
et al. (2026a). For simulated conversations with overlapped speech, we
rely on public recipes and further scale the data with FastMSS Polok et
al. (2026b). We first adopt the public LibriMix Cosentino et al. (2020)
and LibriSpeechMix Kanda et al. (2020) corpora, then use FastMSS to cut
single-speaker utterances at the word level and to rearrange them into
multilingual conversations with two, three, or four speakers, including
overlap and turn-taking. The single-speaker sources are LibriSpeech for
English, an internal corpus for Japanese, and MLS for French, Spanish,
German, and Italian. Because MLS and the internal Japanese corpus lack
the word- and character-level alignments that FastMSS requires, we
generate them with Qwen3-ForcedAligner-0.6B⁹⁹ 9
[huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-ForcedAligner-0.6B](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-ForcedAligner-0.6B) Shi
et al. (2026).

##### Entertainment speech.

Entertainment audio, such as anime, games, movies, and TV, is a key
target domain, yet clean labeled data for it is scarce. We build this
data in two ways. First, we curate in-domain speech from large,
permissively licensed collections such as YODAS-Granary Rao Koluguri et
al. (2025), and we pass it through the cleaning and filtering pipeline
of Sec. §3.8.1. Second, we synthesize entertainment-style speech by
augmenting existing corpora with domain-characteristic effects, such as
high-pitched or stylized voices, expressive prosody, and non-speech
vocal and background sounds. This exposes the model to the acoustic
conditions of entertainment content that read-speech corpora rarely
contain. We use the resulting data for ASR and, because the synthetic
effects and background events are known by construction, also for the
closed-captioning pipeline of Sec. §3.8.5.

#### 3.8.3 Conversational supervision via synthetic dialogues

Real conversational data with reliable context, translations, and
keyword annotations is scarce. We therefore build synthetic multi-turn
dialogues, following the framework of Koudounas et al. (2025). The
dialogues provide context- and keyword-conditioned supervision for ASR,
AST, and S2ST. The pipeline has five steps: dialogue generation,
dialogue-level filtering, speech synthesis, quality gating, and task
construction.

Dialogue generation. We generate dialogues between two instruction-tuned
LLMs across 40 everyday domains, such as travel, food, technology, and
health. An emotion-chain manager drives each dialogue, so that emotions
evolve naturally from turn to turn and stay appropriate to the domain.
We use three model pairings to increase diversity: Gemma 4 26B5–Gemma 4
26B, Qwen3.5 9B4–Gemma 4 26B, and Qwen3.5 9B–Qwen3.5 9B. Each dialogue
has two to five turns, because smaller models tend to hallucinate in
longer dialogues Koudounas et al. (2025); Laban et al. (2026). Each
textual dialogue is then translated from English to 11 different
languages using Qwen3.5 27B.¹⁰¹⁰ 10
[huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.5-27B](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.5-27B)

Dialogue-level filtering. We filter the generated dialogues with an
ensemble of LLM judges, as in Koudounas et al. (2025). Each judge gives
a binary coherence decision and a five-point quality rating. We keep a
dialogue only if all judges find it coherent and give it the highest
rating.

Speech synthesis. We synthesize each turn with the in-house Chatterbox
variant described in Sec. §3.8.2. Speaker voices are cloned from Common
Voice (CV14) reference clips Ardila et al. (2020). Each dialogue has two
distinct speakers, balanced by age and gender; we use fallback
strategies when the pool lacks a matching speaker, which keeps the
speaker pool diverse.

Quality gating. We apply strict quality gates to every synthesized turn.
We transcribe the turn with Whisper Large-v3 Turbo7 and compute the WER
or CER, depending on the language. For translation tasks, we also score
the target text with a reference-free quality estimator, which differs
depending on the training stage. In Stage 2, we use an LLM judge (Gemma
4 26B),5 which we prompt to output a score on the same 0-1 scale as
COMET-Kiwi Rei et al. (2022). In Stage 3, we use COMET-Kiwi
directly.¹¹¹¹ 11
[huggingface.co/Unbabel/wmt22-cometkiwi-da](https://huggingface.co/Unbabel/wmt22-cometkiwi-da)
We keep a turn only if its error rate is at most 0.1 and its translation
quality score (COMET-Kiwi or its LLM equivalent) is at least 0.80. These
gates keep only clean and faithful turns.

Task construction. We build context- and keyword-conditioned tasks from
the filtered turns. For keyword tasks, we extract salient entities
(people, locations, and organizations) with a multilingual named-entity
recognizer, then present them together with distractor keywords in a
list of ten to fifty items. For context tasks, we instead prepend the
previous turns of the dialogue. This gives data for six tasks: context
ASR/AST/S2ST, keyword ASR/AST/S2ST. Translation tasks are generated in
both direct and cascaded forms. This gives the model rich supervision
for handling conversational context and salient keywords, which standard
corpora rarely provide.

#### 3.8.4 Translation supervision

Beyond the synthetic dialogues, we build a large pool of parallel data
for MT, AST, and S2ST. We first mine and filter parallel text, then
synthesize speech for the retained text pairs, which gives the
supervision for AST and S2ST.

##### Parallel text mining.

We mine parallel text from the OPUS collection Tiedemann and Nygaard
(2004). Because our target includes a commercial-ready model, we first
restrict OPUS to datasets with clear, permissive licenses.¹²¹² 12 We
focus on English- and Japanese-centric pairs, covering mainly Arabic,
Chinese (including Traditional Chinese), French, German, Hindi, Italian,
Korean, Portuguese, Russian, and Spanish. We use only the main variety
of each language, which avoids tiny dialect-specific splits. We extract
the source-target sentence pairs and shuffle them, which removes any
corpus- or document-level ordering. Mined parallel data is noisy, so we
score every pair for cross-lingual semantic similarity. We embed both
sides with a multilingual e5 sentence encoder¹³¹³ 13
[huggingface.co/intfloat/multilingual-e5-large](https://huggingface.co/intfloat/multilingual-e5-large)
and compute the cosine similarity of the two embeddings. For each
language pair, we sort the pairs by this score and keep the top decile:
this removes most misaligned, partial, and low-quality translations. We
also drop pairs whose source sentence starts with a non-letter
character, because these are usually markup, list bullets, or formatting
artifacts.

##### Voice selection.

We synthesize the speech with the in-house Chatterbox variant described
above. For each side of a pair, we pick a reference voice in the correct
language from the FLEURS speakers. We pair a source-side voice with the
most similar target-side voice, so that both languages are spoken by
acoustically similar speakers. We measure the similarity with ECAPA-TDNN
speaker embeddings Desplanques et al. (2020); Ravanelli et al.
(2021),¹⁴¹⁴ 14
[huggingface.co/speechbrain/spkrec-ecapa-voxceleb](https://huggingface.co/speechbrain/spkrec-ecapa-voxceleb)
and keep only voice pairs whose similarity is above a per-language
threshold.

##### Speech synthesis and verification.

We synthesize the source and target sentences with the selected voices.
Then, we transcribe every synthesized utterance with Whisper Large-v3
Turbo7 and align the transcript with the intended text. This check
detects trailing hallucinations and audio that continues after the
intended text ends. We discard the utterances where the recognizer
output differs from the reference; the remaining triples of source
audio, target text, and target audio supervise MT, AST, and S2ST.

#### 3.8.5 Understanding supervision

We construct two kinds of data for the understanding family. Closed
captioning data is synthesized by mixing labeled audio segments.
AudioReason-10M is a large corpus of reasoning-focused audio question
answering.

##### Closed captioning: scene composition.

Rich closed captions describe not only speech but also music, sound
effects, speaker turns, and timing, yet no large corpus provides all of
these annotations jointly. We therefore build each audio scene from
labeled segments and derive its caption from the known composition:
every event, timestamp, and speaker label in the caption is exact with
respect to the labels of the segments.

We draw segments from three libraries: speech, sound effects (SFX), and
music. Each segment has a label, a source (speaker) identifier, and a
duration. We sample segments with a simple transition model that
alternates between speech and sound events; we place them one after
another on a timeline, with randomized blank gaps and optional overlap.
Each scene lasts between 10 and 30 seconds, and we balance the corpus
across four duration bands.

##### Closed captioning: speakers and acoustics.

We control speaker composition and acoustic realism of each scene
explicitly. We can enforce a target number of distinct speakers per
scene, drawing speech segments from speakers with enough utterances and
encouraging natural speaker turns. Segments are mixed with a separate
intensity range for each type: speech is prominent, SFX are attenuated,
and music is quiet. We apply linear fade-in and fade-out and loudness
normalization, and resize SFX segments at random to vary their duration.
Optionally, we add a quiet background music track over the whole scene.
The result is varied multi-speaker scenes with music and sound effects.

##### Closed captioning: caption formats.

We generate several caption formats from the composition of each scene,
one for each annotation task; a single scene therefore supervises
several levels of detail. The formats range from a transcription with
bracketed non-speech events, to timestamped events, to timestamped and
speaker-attributed captions. The most detailed format is a structured
summary, counting the music, speech, and SFX segments, listing the
unique speakers, and describing each event with its label and time span,
similar to Kumar et al. (2026b). Prompts for each task are shown in
Table 20.

##### Closed captioning: languages and stages.

We cover Chinese, English, French, German, Italian, Japanese, and
Spanish. We use a separate segment library for each language, built from
open-source Ardila et al. (2020); Defferrard et al. (2016); Gemmeke et
al. (2017); Koudounas et al. (2023) and internal datasets. We split this
data across two stages: in Stage 2, we use only commercially licensed
segment libraries; in Stage 3, we augment the mix with the rest.

##### AudioReason-10M: reasoning-focused audio QA.

Audio QA built from recognition-oriented benchmarks, such as ASR or
sound event classification, does not train expert-level auditory
reasoning Ghosh et al. (2026). Following the approach of Audio
Flamingo 3 Ghosh et al. (2026), we build a large reasoning-focused
corpus, which we call AudioReason-10M. It contains 10M audio QA pairs,
most of them multiple-choice, on clips of at most 30 seconds. We build
it only from commercially licensed or internally owned audio, and we
exclude research-only and license-restricted corpora.

We organize AudioReason-10M around five reasoning skill families, each
requiring capabilities that go beyond simple transcription or
single-label classification.

- •
  Temporal reasoning. Questions require reasoning over the order,
  duration, and co-occurrence of events, for example which sound comes
  first, how long an event lasts, or whether two events overlap.
- •
  Speech-in-sound. The model must combine the spoken content with the
  surrounding acoustic scene, so that speech and non-speech evidence
  lead to one answer.
- •
  Speaker reasoning. Questions probe speaker attributes such as gender
  and approximate age, as well as the number of distinct speakers and
  their turns, requiring paralinguistic rather than lexical
  understanding.
- •
  Spatial audio perception. Questions ask about the direction, relative
  position, and movement of sound sources, which requires spatial cues
  in the audio.
- •
  Instruction following with verifiable constraints. Each question has
  an explicit output constraint, such as a format, a length, or an
  answer set, whose satisfaction can be checked automatically; this
  teaches the model to follow instructions precisely while reasoning
  about the audio.

For each skill, we design dedicated prompts and QA templates. We
generate distractors for the multiple-choice questions, with the aim of
reducing shortcuts that do not need the audio. All skills share one QA
schema, so that we can mix them uniformly during training.

Whisper Large-v3 (WLv3), SeamlessM4T v2 (Seamless) Barrault et al.
(2023), and VibeVoice-ASR Peng et al. (2026b).

##### Multilingual and English ASR.

On English LibriSpeech, Walrus-R reaches 1.65/3.85 WER on clean/other,
slightly behind AF3 (1.57/3.13). On multilingual benchmarks, it has the
lowest WER on MLS (6.89 against 8.20 for Phi4-MM, Table 8) and on
VoxPopuli (7.84 against 11.78 for Gemma4, Table 10). On FLEURS
(Table 9), it reaches 5.33 WER (94.7% word accuracy, Figure 2); this is
ahead of the 4$`\times`$-bigger Qwen3-Omni (93.8%) and Whisper Large-v3
(92.0%). Phi4-MM reaches 4.00 WER, but it does not support five of our
evaluation languages (Arabic, Dutch, Hindi, Korean, and Polish; see
Table 9). On internal entertainment audio (Table 12), Walrus-R reaches
17.45 WER, against 24.35 for Gemma4.

##### Code-switching ASR.

On CS-FLEURS read speech, Walrus-R reaches 7.23 CER, against 32.30 for
the best prior system (Gemma4). On the XTTS subset, it reaches 15.01
CER, against 44.10. Walrus-CR reaches 26.69 and 35.36, which is already
better than every prior system. Both systems outperform even the 30B Q3O
model, which achieves only 38.70 CER on read speech (Table 13).

##### Multi-talker ASR.

Table 14 shows the results on overlapping speech. Gemma4, Phi4-MM, AF3,
and Q2.5O cannot transcribe overlapping speakers, and they produce no
valid output. On LibriMix clean 2-mix, Walrus-R reaches 19.32 cpWER;
this is about half of the 37.04 of Qwen3-Omni, and far below the 59.34
of the dedicated VibeVoice-ASR. On Japanese Daily Talk, it reaches 56.29
cpCER, against 71.99 for VibeVoice-ASR. Walrus-CR also beats
VibeVoice-ASR on the cp metrics. The time-constrained metrics (tcpWER
and tcpCER) give the same ranking for Walrus-R. Two points limit this
comparison: (i) we evaluate on 30-second segments, which match the input
limit of Walrus but not the long-form design of VibeVoice-ASR; and (ii)
LibriMix is also part of our training data, so it is in-domain for
Walrus. The absolute error on Japanese Daily Talk is still high, thus
showing how natural multi-speaker conversation still remains a hard
task.

##### Speech translation.

On AST, Walrus-R reaches 42.63 BLEU on CoVoST-2 (Table 15) and 33.05 on
FLEURS X$`\to`$En (Table 16), against 39.33 and 29.86 for Phi4-MM. On
FLEURS En$`\to`$X, it reaches 34.33 BLEU, against 32.15. On S2ST, Walrus
is the strongest system: it achieves 43.63 ASR-BLEU on CVSS-C X$`\to`$En
and 32.28 on FLEURS X$`\to`$En, against 36.02 and 25.15 for
Qwen2.5-Omni. On CVSS-C (Table 17), it also surpasses the 30B
Qwen3-Omni, while being close on FLEURS (Table 18). It also supports
En$`\to`$X S2ST (22.94 ASR-BLEU on FLEURS), where the other systems
report no result. Walrus-CR is already ahead of the prior systems on AST
and S2ST X$`\to`$En, but its gains are smaller on both tasks.

##### Audio understanding.

Walrus-R is the strongest system on audio understanding. On MMAU-Pro, it
reaches 71.92% accuracy, far above the best baseline (52.20% for
Qwen2.5-Omni), and also closed-source systems such as for
Gemini-Flash-3.7 (70.40%), and GPT-4o-Audio (52.50%). On MMSU, it
reaches 64.14%, against 61.40% for AF3. On captioning and retrieval,
Walrus is competitive with the best specialized baseline (AF3), leading
on several AudioCaps retrieval metrics and closing most of the gap on
Clotho-v2 captioning. It trails AF3 on IEMOCAP emotion recognition
(49.55% against 63.80%, OOD for our model), which reflects the small
share of emotion data in our mix.

##### Commercial-Ready and Research variants.

Walrus-R improves on Walrus-CR on every benchmark in Table 4. The
largest gains are on CS-FLEURS read speech (26.69 to 7.23 CER), internal
entertainment ASR (26.71 to 17.45 WER), and MMAU-Pro (50.18% to 71.92%).
The two variants share the backbone, but they differ in the training
stage, data license, and task weights (Sec. §3.7). The gains therefore
reflect Stage 3 as a whole, and we do not separate the effect of each
change.

## 5 Discussion

Walrus uses a standard backbone, so we discuss its results in terms of
the data. We highlight three findings that the benchmark tables alone do
not make explicit.

##### Targeted data goes with targeted gains.

The largest gains appear where we built dedicated data. CS-ASR is only
1.1% of the recognition data, which is about 0.5% of all data. Still,
the CER on CS-FLEURS read speech falls from 32.30 for the best prior
system to 26.69 for Walrus-CR and 7.23 for Walrus-R.
Entertainment-domain ASR follows the same pattern: the internal
entertainment WER falls from 26.71 to 17.45 between the two variants,
and the Japanese subset falls from 23.69 to 12.90 (Appendix §C,
Table 12). MMAU-Pro accuracy rises from 50.18% to 71.92% between the two
variants.

##### Weighting and recognition.

Stage 3 raises the weights of generation and understanding and lowers
the ASR weight from 5.0 to 4.0. Even so, Walrus-R improves on Walrus-CR
on every ASR benchmark. This is consistent with the idea that a high
recognition weight protects the core while the mix shifts. However, as
we did not run Stage 3 data with the Stage 2 weights, we cannot fully
separate the effect of the weights from the effect of the new data.

##### Remaining gaps.

Walrus trails the best baseline on emotion recognition and audio
captioning. On the former, it reaches 49.55% on IEMOCAP (OOD) against
63.80% for AF3. This partly reflect the distribution of SER data in our
mix (about 0.3%). On audio captioning, it achieves 0.39 CIDEr on
Clotho-v2 (OOD) against 0.50 (AF3), while captioning represents 15.4% of
the understanding data. Furthermore. on Japanese Daily Talk, Walrus-R
reaches 56.29 cpCER, which beats VibeVoice-ASR but is still high in
absolute terms. Our multi-talker training data is mostly simulated, and
real overlapping speech is harder.

## 6 Conclusion

We presented Walrus, a unified 8B speech-and-audio model that covers
recognition, translation, generation, and understanding. Its core is a
three-stage curriculum, from broad multilingual data to complex and
domain-specific data. Task-aware and dataset-aware weighting balances
the very different token budgets of the tasks. Trained on over 600M
samples across more than 20 tasks and 100 languages, Walrus-R matches or
surpasses comparable open SpeechLLMs on most benchmarks, and leads
clearly on code-switching ASR, multilingual and entertainment-domain
recognition, speech-to-speech translation, and audio understanding. On
several benchmarks, it also outperforms larger or dedicated systems such
as Qwen3-Omni-30B, Seamless v2, and VibeVoice-ASR. These results suggest
that data engineering is a primary driver of quality in multi-task
speech and audio modeling.

## Appendix A Task prompts

Walrus is steered to each of its tasks only through a natural-language
prompt. This appendix describes how we design, sample, and use these
prompts. Table 20 lists the canonical prompt of each task.

### A.1 Prompt pools

We do not use a fixed instruction for each task, because the model could
overfit to a single phrasing. Instead, we keep a pool of 10-20
paraphrased prompts for every task, and we sample one prompt from the
pool for each training example. This encourages robust instruction
following, since the model must read the instruction instead of
recognizing a fixed string.

The prompts in a pool differ in register and in format.

- •
  Imperative prompts give a direct command, for example “Transcribe the
  {lang1} speech:”.
- •
  Interrogative prompts ask a question, for example “What is being said
  in the speech?”.
- •
  Tag-style prompts use a compact marker, for example “\[ASR {lang1}\]”.
- •
  Structured prompts use explicit delimiters, for example
  \<context\>...\</context\>.

All prompts of a task ask for the same output; only the wording and the
format change, so the paraphrases never change the task itself.

### A.2 Slot variables

Prompts are templates with slot variables (Table 5); we fill such slots
with the values of each sample when building the example. One template
thus serves many languages, contexts, and label sets.

| Slot             | Filled with                                        |
| ---------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| {lang1}, {lang2} | Source and target language                         |
| {context}        | Preceding text, as discourse context               |
| {keywords}       | List of candidate keywords                         |
| {labels}         | Closed set of allowed labels, for example emotions |
| {speaker}        | Target speaker for target-speaker queries          |
| {start}, {end}   | Time span for time-conditioned queries             |
| {agent}, {user}  | Roles in dialogue-level context                    |

### A.4 Prompts for conditioned tasks

Many tasks need more than a language name, so their prompts carry extra
inputs. The structure of these inputs is the same across the variants of
a task.

- •
  Context ASR and context AST/S2ST. The prompt contains the preceding
  text in a marked block, such as \<context\>...\</context\>. For
  dialogues, the source and target sides of earlier turns are marked,
  and the roles come from {agent} and {user}.
- •
  Keyword tasks. The prompt contains a shuffled list of candidate
  keywords. The list may contain distractors and may be empty, as
  described in Sec. §3.8.2.
- •
  Timestamp and multi-talker tasks. The prompt states the requested
  output, such as speaker labels or timestamps. For target-speaker ASR
  and timestamp QA, it also contains the target speaker or the time
  span.
- •
  Emotion recognition. The prompt lists the allowed labels with
  {labels}, so that the answer is one of a closed set.
- •
  Closed captioning. Each level of detail has its own prompt, from a
  plain transcription with non-speech events to the full structured
  summary (Sec. §3.8.5).
- •
  Translation and generation. The prompt names the source and target
  language. It also states whether the output is text or speech, and
  whether the translation is direct or cascaded.

## Appendix B Summary of data pipelines

Sec. §3.8 describes the data pipelines of Walrus in five subsections;
this appendix gives a short overview of all of them in one place.
Table 6 lists, for each pipeline, what we build, which tools we use, and
which quality controls we apply. The rows follow the order of Sec. §3.8.

##### How to read the table.

The column “What we build” names the supervision that the pipeline
produces; the column “Tools” names the corpora, models, and recipes that
the pipeline uses; and the column “Quality control” names the filters
that we apply to the output. A dash means that we apply no filter beyond
the construction itself.

##### Real and synthetic data.

Our pipelines use real and synthetic data in different ways. Some
pipelines only restructure real data: context ASR and keyword ASR add
context or keyword inputs to real ASR corpora, and the casing
restoration changes only the text surface. Some pipelines recombine real
audio: simulated multi-talker mixtures rearrange real single-speaker
utterances, and closed-captioning scenes mix real speech, sound-effect,
and music segments. Other pipelines generate new content: code-switching
ASR and the synthetic dialogues generate both text and audio, and the
translation pipeline synthesizes speech for mined text pairs, while
AudioReason-10M builds new questions and answers over audio.

This difference matters for the reliability of the labels. In recombined
data, the labels follow from the construction, but they are only as
accurate as the labels of the source segments. In generated data, the
text is the label, but the audio can differ from it if the TTS system
makes an error. We therefore check the audio of generated data, as
described next.

##### Quality control.

The quality controls in Table 6 fall into three groups.

- •
  Text filters. These filters check the text before any audio is made.
  Examples are the CER check after casing restoration (Sec. §3.8.1), the
  de-duplication of code-switched sentences, the LLM-judge ensemble for
  dialogues (Sec. §3.8.3), and the top-decile similarity filter for
  parallel text (Sec. §3.8.4).
- •
  Audio filters. These filters check the synthesized audio itself.
  Examples are the NISQA threshold for code-switching speech and the
  speaker-similarity threshold for voice pairs.
- •
  Text-audio checks. These filters check that the audio says the
  intended text. We transcribe the synthesized speech with Whisper
  Lv3-Turbo and compare the transcript with the intended text; we use
  this check for code-switching speech, dialogues, and translation
  speech.

Translation quality of the dialogues is checked in a fourth way. We
score the target text with a reference-free estimator: an LLM judge in
Stage 2 and COMET-Kiwi in Stage 3 (Sec. §3.8.3).

##### Models used to build the data.

The tools in Table 6 play four roles.

- •
  Text generation and judging. Gemma 4 26B and Qwen3.5-9B write
  code-switched sentences and dialogues, restore casing, and act as
  judges; Qwen3.5-27B translates dialogues from English onto different
  languages.
- •
  Speech synthesis. Qwen3-TTS and a fast in-house variant of Chatterbox
  create the audio for code-switching, dialogues, and translation.
- •
  Recognition and alignment. Whisper Lv3-Turbo checks synthesized
  speech, and Qwen3-ForcedAligner provides word-level alignments for
  multi-talker mixtures.
- •
  Scoring. NISQA scores speech quality, ECAPA-TDNN scores speaker
  similarity, multi- lingual-e5 scores cross-lingual text similarity,
  and COMET-Kiwi scores translation quality.

S2ST quality beyond ASR-BLEU (Table 19). ASR-BLEU measures translation
accuracy but does not capture the full quality of spoken output. A
complete evaluation of S2ST must also consider speaker consistency,
emotion preservation, prosody preservation, and naturalness, as detailed
in \[30\]. We thus compute several metrics, using the COMPASS
toolkit \[30\]: UTMOS-v2 (UTMOS)¹⁸¹⁸ 18
[github.com/sarulab-speech/UTMOSv2](https://github.com/sarulab-speech/UTMOSv2)
predicts the naturalness of the output speech; speaker similarity (SSim)
is the similarity between the speaker of the source speech and the
speaker of the output, measured via WavLM-SV;¹⁹¹⁹ 19
[huggingface.co/microsoft/wavlm-base-sv](https://github.com/sarulab-speech/UTMOSv2)
emotion similarity (ESim) is the similarity between the emotion of the
source and of the output measured using Emotion2Vec;²⁰²⁰ 20
[huggingface.co/emotion2vec/emotion2vec_plus_base](https://huggingface.co/emotion2vec/emotion2vec_plus_base)
AutoPCP (APCP) predicts how similar the prosody of the output is to the
prosody of the source \[3\]. We compare Walrus with four systems:
Qwen2.5/3-Omni, Kraken \[16\], the system whose speech decoder Walrus
adopts, and Seamless \[3\], a dedicated multilingual S2ST system. Q2.5O
and Kraken support only X$`\to`$En translation.

On X$`\to`$En, Walrus-R reaches 32.28 ASR-BLEU. This is on par with
Kraken (32.30) and Seamless (32.10), 7.18 points above Q2.5O (25.10),
but 1.32 points lower than Q3O (33.60). On En$`\to`$X, Walrus-R achieves
22.94 ASR-BLEU, slightly below both Seamless and Q3O.

The strongest result of Walrus is the preservation of the speaker,
emotion, and prosody. On X$`\to`$En, Walrus-R has the best SSim (0.77),
ESim (0.81), and AutoPCP (2.57) of all systems, including Q3O (0.61,
0.75, and 2.19). Compared with Seamless, it improves SSim by 0.11, ESim
by 0.05, and AutoPCP by 0.52. Compared with Kraken, which shares our
speech decoder, the gains are smaller (0.02, 0.04, and 0.09). On
En$`\to`$X, the gaps are larger: Walrus-R reaches 0.70 SSim, 0.73 ESim,
and 2.45 AutoPCP, compared to 0.58/0.56/2.25 for Seamless and
0.49/0.44/2.12 for Q3O. Walrus conditions its vocoder on the source
speech (Sec. §2), which is consistent with this result.

The naturalness scores are more mixed. Walrus-R has a higher UTMOS than
Seamless in both directions (3.19 vs 3.06 and 3.05 vs 2.82). The Qwen
Omni models score higher on X$`\to`$En (3.48 for Q2.5O and 3.59 for
Q3O), and Q3O also scores higher on En$`\to`$X (3.27). Both these models
generate speech with fixed reference voices: this can make the speech
cleaner and more natural, but it does not preserve the prosody and
emotion of the source, which fits their low ESim and AutoPCP scores.
Walrus, in contrast, conditions its vocoder on the source speech; FLEURS
contains recordings of uneven quality, so this conditioning can carry
the noise of the source into the output and eventually lower the UTMOS.

Walrus-R improves on Walrus-CR on all columns of Table 19. The largest
gains are in ASR-BLEU, with +3.69 on X$`\to`$En (28.59 to 32.28) and
+5.75 on En$`\to`$X (17.19 to 22.94), while the expressivity metrics
improve by smaller amounts: SSim improves by 0.02 and 0.03, ESim by 0.05
and 0.04, and AutoPCP by 0.03 and 0.04. Stage 3 adds S2ST data and
raises the S2ST task weight from 0.5 to 2.0 (Sec. §3.6): translation
quality benefits most, while the voice preservation of Walrus-CR is
already close to that of Walrus-R.
