###### Abstract

Recent advancements in sign language processing can be attributed to
developments in both large-scale dataset collection and algorithmic
complexity in areas such as pose estimation techniques, signal
processing through Transformer models, and model optimization. However,
only a handful of sign languages, such as the American, British, and
German, from over 200 globally recognized sign languages, reap the
benefits of such advancements, while the rest lag behind. In this study,
we aim to address this technological disparity by proposing a framework
for the automatic collection and preparation of sign language datasets,
with very minimal human-labour intervention, using the South African
Sign Language (SASL) as a testbed. Specifically, we automatically
collect SASL video content using public resources such as news
broadcasts. Additionally, we convert their accompanying audio streams
into text using available Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) tools.
Furthermore, we crop the sign language area in the videos and use
gradient descent to locate valleys of non-motion to determine
end-of-sentence points within long videos to create our weakly
sentence-aligned parallel corpora – the Maswao (Signs) dataset. Finally,
we benchmark the weakly aligned dataset on the task of Sign-to-text
Translation using Recurrent Neural Networks and Transformer models to
compare their individual intricacies. Our results show that Transformer
models proposed in the literature outperform recurrent baselines, with
the best-performing Transformer achieving BLEU-1 to BLEU-4 scores of
13.78, 5.21, 2.52, and 1.54, respectively, as well as ChrF = 18.09 and
ROUGE-L = 10.51. These results indicate that the task remains promising
and warrants further investigations.

## 1 Introduction

Sign-to-text translation (STT) and Text-to-sign translation (TTS) are
two complementary tasks aimed at enabling collegial communication
between the Deaf and hearing communities Hwang et al. (2025). In many
cases, these two important subcomponents are addressed independently,
with STT spearheading the race.

STT evolved from the task of sign language recognition Kong and
Ranganath (2014), and can be defined as the process of translating
complete sign-language sentences into grammatically sound spoken
language sentences Camgoz et al. (2018). Many works have been proposed
to tackle this challenge, with earlier methods constrained by feature
extraction techniques Tharwat et al. (2015); Zhang et al. (2016)
transitioning to more advanced methods using convolutional neural
networks (CNN), due to their enhanced hierarchical feature extraction
mechanisms Tran et al. (2015).

More recently, prominent STT techniques adopted hybrid architectures
comprised of deep CNN feature extractors such as ResNet Deng et al.
(2009), I3D Carreira and Zisserman (2017), and Inception Szegedy et al.
(2015) to model spatial relationships within the input images, and
Transformer models Devlin et al. (2019) to learn the temporal
dependencies of input sequences, drawing from advancements in Natural
Language Processing. Regardless, the quality of available video datasets
(e.g., poor video resolutions, inconsistent signer positioning,
variation in signs, poor illumination, etc), and the inaccuracy of pose
estimation techniques remain as the main challenges in STT to date De
Coster et al. (2020).

Text-to-sign (TTS) on the other hand is the reverse process of STT Zhao
et al. (2000). Historically, TTS has often been approached as an
avatar-rendering problem Cox et al. (2002). However, due to a myriad of
issues such as avatar stiffness, unnatural motion, the complexity of
converting input signals into parametric representations, increased
annotations costs, and high reliance on expert knowledge, research
alternatives such as deep learning models using recurrent neural
networks Stoll et al. (2018) and recently Transformer models Saunders et
al. (2020), Diffusion models Tang et al. (2025), and generative
adversarial neural networks Saunders et al. () that generates
photo-realistic sequences of sign sequences, are gaining momentum.
Despite the impressive performance reported by these techniques, several
challenges remain. These include regression to the mean Wahbi and Atsumi
(2023), the accumulation of marginal errors (often referred to as
drift) Saunders et al. (2020), and poor perceptual responses from Deaf
communities Ventura et al. (2020), which continue to limit practical
adoption and impact.

Importantly, the fuel that drives these rapid developments in both STT
and TTS is the large-scale collection of parallel, annotated, and
quality sign language datasets. The availability, and further refinement
of such datasets remains the primary drivers of success in sign language
processing. For example, datasets such as the PHOENIX2014T Koller et al.
(2015), Chinese sign language dataset (CSL) Pu et al. (2020), and the
How2Sign Duarte et al. (2021) are among the highly evaluated
non-proprietary datasets in sign language processing history, with
recent algorithmic advancements such as Transformers benchmarked, their
limitations scribed, and state-of-the-arts (SOTA) established on them
 Fang et al. (2024). However, similar benchmarks that shape research
directions have not been established for many low-resourced sign
languages, including South African Sign Language (SASL).

To address this gap, we propose a framework for the automatic
collection, preparation, and benchmarking of SASL data that aims to
minimise the resources required for dataset development while
maintaining data quality. The framework is designed to be extensible to
other low-resource sign languages with similar resource characteristics,
such as the availability of unstructured public sign-language video
content. More specifically, our collection includes the crawling of
YouTube videos from a popular and notarized South African news outlet -
the South African Broadcasting Corporation (commonly known as the SABC),
which occasionally releases news video broadcasts that include a sign
language interpreter, translating the presenter’s spoken utterances into
SASL as shown in Figure 1(a) SABC ().

From this source, we created the first weakly aligned spoken language to
sign language dataset with over 2944 text-video sentence-level examples,
covering a wide vocabulary (see Figure 1(b)). Lastly, to accelerate
research in STT for SASL, we benchmark the dataset using a set of
state-of-the-art techniques, including MMPose Contributors (2020) for
pose estimation, AlexNet-based CNN feature extraction, Recurrent Neural
Networks, and Transformer models. This benchmarking establishes an
initial state-of-the-art baseline for Maswao SASL dataset using an
AlexNet + Transformer architecture.

The contribution of this paper is succinctly organized as follows:

1.  1.  We present the first weakly aligned parallel video-to-text corpus
        for South African Sign language (SASL) using publicly available news
        content - the Maswao (meaning ’signs’ in Northern Sesotho) dataset.
        This dataset comprises 2,944 weakly aligned spoken-language
        sentences paired with their equivalent sign-language video sentence
        representations.
2.  2.  We present the first benchmark on STT, using various pose estimation
        tools, deep CNNs, Recurrent networks, and Transformer models.
3.  3.  We establish a state-of-the-art AlexNet+Transformer model for our
        weakly aligned Maswao SASL dataset on the STT task.

Our work aims to accelerate advancements in South African Sign Language
(SASL) research by proposing a reproducible and low-cost framework for
the automatic curation of sign language datasets. To foster open and
reproducible research, we publicly release our code, the Maswao dataset,
and supporting resources here ¹¹ 1
https://github.com/dsfsi/maswo-south-african-sign-language-dataset.git,
enabling other researchers to build upon and extend this work.

## 2 Related Works

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), there are over 466
million people suffering from some form of hearing loss, ranging from
mild to severe hearing deficiency (WHO) (); Team (). The WHO further
projected that by the year 2070, approximately 1 billion people of the
global population will be affected by hearing loss Kushalnagar (2019).
The primary mode of communication for many of these hearing-impaired
individuals is sign language, with a footprint of over 200 diverse
languages, with their own developed and complex grammar and structure.
However, while communication between Deaf people with knowledge of the
locally used sign language is possible, communication between the
majority of the Deaf and the hearing communities presents many barriers
reppling down to access challenges (e.g., health facilities, security
services, entertainment, and many more). Interpreters have mostly been
used to mediate communication between the Deaf and the hearing
communities; however, the limited number of qualified interpreters often
fail to meet the high demand Mohamed et al. (2022); Rastgoo et al.
(2022). Often leading to vulnerabilities in society The South African
National Deaf Association (2014). Consequently, automated sign-to-text
translation and text-to-sign are emerging as promising approaches for
bridging the communication divide.

End-to-end Sign-to-text (STT) translation systems historically adopted
statistical approaches such as Hidden Markov Models Dreuw et al. (2007);
Zafrulla et al. (2011). These methods relied on hand-engineered
features, limiting the quality and scalability of their approach to
domain-constrained conditions. Subsequent to statistical approaches,
classical machine learning techniques such as Conditional Random Fields,
Random Forest, Support Vector Machines, and later multi-layered
perceptrons took form Kong and Ranganath (2014). However, these methods
suffered the same fate as their predecessors as they also relied on
feature extraction techniques to extract discriminative features Yang et
al. (2008); Chavan et al. (2016). Recently, with the advent of
sophisticated deep neural network architectures, many STT pipelines have
adopted complex systems using deep convolutional neural networks such as
ResNet, EfficientNet, I3D, and Inception as their backbone, due to their
capabilities in automatically extracting abstract latent representations
of spatial data Alharthi and Alzahrani (2023). Others have adopted deep
recurrent neural networks such as LSTMs and GRUs, due to their ability
to efficiently model temporal dependencies between words Camgoz et al.
(2018). However, a new wave of Transformer models drawing from successes
in NLP has marked a new paradigm shift Ko et al. (2019); Ananthanarayana
et al. (2021); Singla et al. (2024); Hu et al. (2023); Sreemathy et al.
(2024), eliciting magnitudes of performance gains from the former
methods, as datasets continue to become complex and large. For example,
De Coster et al. (2020) compares LSTM models with Transformer models,
using features extracted using OpenPose and ResNet-43. Their study shows
that, while LSTM models perform better on the development samples,
Transformer models towered over them on unseen instances, highlighting
their generalization capabilities. Similarly, Camgoz et al. (2020)
proposed the first implementation of a Transformer architecture on the
challenging PHOENIX2014T dataset, establishing a new state-of-the-art
succeeding recurrent predecessors. Yin (2020) improved on the
state-of-the-art by proposing a spatio-temporal multi-cue model stacked
with a Transformer encoder for STT. De Coster et al. (2021) leverages
the popular transfer learning technique using pre-trained BERT Devlin et
al. (2019), and mBART-50 Tang et al. (2020) models to translate input
videos into spoken language sequences. Recently, López et al. (2025)
presents a CNN-based feature extractor using motion primitives derived
from optical flows as inputs, which are then passed into a Transformer
model for STT translation. Their comprehensive evaluation on multiple
datasets: PHOENIX2014T, and Colombian sign language (CoL-SLTD) datasets,
showed the effectiveness of their proposed technique.

Text-to-sign translation systems on the other hand adopted avatar
rendering applications to produce sign language utterances from spoken
language inputs such as text or voice Zhao et al. (2000); Cox et al.
(2002); Cox et al. (2003); Lincoln and Cox (2003); Glauert et al.
(2006). However, issues such as restricted domain of discource leading
to low vocabularies, increased annotation cost, and high reliance on
specialized skillset for extracting motion capture, limited the
application of these methods. To address these shortcomings, subsequent
methods adopted deep learning methods such as recurrent neural networks,
coupled with the emergence of pose estimation tools such as OpenPose Cao
et al. (2019), and MediaPipe Lugaresi et al. (), to predict a sequence
of joint coordinates of skeletal sequences $`P=\{P^{K}_{M}\}`$ with
$`M`$ frames and $`K`$ joints, equivalent to sign language movements,
given a spoken language sequence $`X=\{x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{W}\}`$, with
$`W`$ words Stoll et al. (2020). Recurrent neural networks, and
alternate modifications such as LSTMs, Gated Recurrent Units, etc, are
notoriously susceptible to the vanishing gradient problem Hochreiter
(1998), and insufficiency in modeling long-temporal dependencies.
Although hybrid models based on recurrent networks and attention
mechanisms were proposed, they were bounded by their costly
computational requirements, stemming from recurrent processing Stoll et
al. (2018). Following these methods, Transformer-based approaches, due
to enhanced contextual representations, gained interest Zelinka and
Kanis (2020); Saunders et al. (2020). Consequently, Saunders et al. ()
proposed a Transformer-based generative adversarial network that
receives spoken language input sentences and converts them into
pose-based sequences congruent to sign language gestures. Saunders et
al. (2021) on the other hand proposed a system of multiple Transformer
models, each specializing in specific feature representations, trained
jointly to optimize the collective goal of sign language translation.
Cui et al. (2022) presents a non-auto-regressive decoding scheme as a
solution to mitigating the regression to the mean challenges, resulting
in an interesting line of work building on their foundation Azevedo et
al. (2024); RI and others (2024). Fang et al. (2024) proposes a large
language model (LLM) framework that trains multiple Transformer models
across several spoken-language–to–sign-language pairs. Meanwhile,
diffusion-based and Transformer-based approaches are gaining traction
for translating input text sequences into pose-based skeletal
representations Tang et al. (2025); Wang et al. (2025).

The continued advancement of both STT and TTS research relies heavily on
the availability of large-scale, high-quality, annotated datasets. In
this regard, Duarte et al. (2021) introduced How2Sign, a multimodal and
multi-view American Sign Language (ASL) corpus comprising over 80 hours
of recordings paired with corresponding gloss annotations for sign
language translation. Despite its scale, the dataset is limited to a
single sign language and primarily supports translation between ASL and
English. More recently, Tanzer and Zhang (2025) leveraged YouTube to
construct a large-scale, open-domain multilingual sign language dataset
spanning 25 sign languages, with the primary objective of translating
sign language into the corresponding regional spoken language.
Similarly, Avramidis et al. (2025) proposed a multilingual sign language
corpus covering 12 sign languages, collected from publicly available
sources including news broadcasts, government communications, and
educational content. Although these datasets have substantially advanced
sign language research, they include little to no representation of
African sign languages or African spoken languages, highlighting a
significant gap in the available resources for the African context.

South African Sign language is amongst the many under-represented
languages, with only four findable literature works proposing
technological solutions for sign language processing van Zijl (2006);
McInnes (2014); Ndlovu and Alatise (2022); Chakoma and Baron (2023),
spanning over 3 decades. Specifically, van Zijl (2006) presented a
Synchronous Tree-Adjoining Grammar (STAG) parser that transforms input
sentences into parse trees used to parametrize an avatar generation
model. McInnes (2014) subsequently developed a low-cost hand glove for
sign gesture collection. Ndlovu and Alatise (2022) uses smartphone
cameras to collect their sign language dataset, and evaluate the quality
of their processed datasets using classical feedforward neural networks.
Recently, Chakoma and Baron (2023) presents a look-up approach that
attempts to match input gestures with pre-stored sign gestures for TTS
translation. However, these existing efforts focus on hand gestures or
isolated signs and do not extend to sentence-level sign language
translations, a limitation that the Maswao dataset seeks to address.
Notably, none of the successive works from the four aforementioned work
builds on the progress of existing work, rendering literature in the
South African context extremely fragmented. Additionally, the two recent
works Ndlovu and Alatise (2022); Chakoma and Baron (2023), do not
explore Transformer advancements. To address these issues and more, we
propose a continuous framework that includes the collection, and
preparation of digital video resources from publicly accessible news
outlets, which are then used to benchmark various processing techniques
such as pose estimation tools, recurrent neural networks, and
Transformer architectures.

## 3 Methodology

In this section we present a largely automated end-to-end pipeline to
build a weakly aligned sign-to-text (STT) data resource from publicly
accessible news broadcasts. Figure 2 shows a summary of our framework
including: (i) downloading Youtube video created by SABC for all
channels (ii) identifying and filtering videos without sign language
interpretation (iii) generating transcriptions from the accompanying
audio streams using ASR (iv) cropping and isolating the interpreter
section of the videos and (v) deriving sentence-level video-to-text
pairs via our proposed motion and gradient-based segmentation technique.
This process resulted in our Maswao weakly aligned corpora. We then use
the resulting Maswao corpus to benchmark key processing and modeling
choices, including pose-estimation inputs, recurrent baselines, and
Transformer-based SLT architectures.

### 3.1 Data Creation Processes

Digital sign language content is difficult to find for many low-resource
sign languages. In the South African context, the South African
Broadcasting Corporation (SABC) is one of the popular public news
outlets that occasionally delivers spoken language news content with
parallel sign language interpretation. The interpretation is done using
the official SASL. The SABC releases their news recording on YouTube,
where we crawled news feeds for all 11 official South African languages.
To crawl the datasets, we used a python open-source library yt-dlp ²² 2
https://pypi.org/project/yt-dlp/ to download Youtube videos, with
properties such as channel (highlighting the language) set as ( English,
Sepedi, Sesotho, Setswana, IsiZulu, etc), and extension type $`.webm`$
to download videos of high quality where options are available –
alternatively, we resorted to $`.mp4`$. Our capacity to download videos
at a larger scale was restricted by Youtube bot detectors, requiring
constant authentication after a few downloads, which we managed using
manually downloaded cookies files. As such, we were able to download a
total of only $`2000`$ videos from SABC for all channels combined,
together with their audio streams. In this study, we only report the
implementation of the framework and experiments for the English (en)
language, as the language with the largest crawl, and categorized the
other official languages as the “Others” as shown in Table 1 describing
the statistics of our Maswao dataset. As a result, the initial crawl for
English resulted in a raw dataset of $`377`$ raw videos. However, not
all videos downloaded included sign language interpretation. Which meant
that we had to first identify videos with interpreters included from the
downloaded set, locate the position of the interpreter, and then crop
the section with sign language interpretations. Fortunately, the SABC
has a standard for placing a sign language interpreter in a box at the
bottom right corner of the video frame, solving the location challenge,
as shown in Figure 1(a). This reduces the search space for
interpreter-box location.

Following this, our automated interpreter box isolation pipeline first
extracts video frames from each video at predefined timestamps of 35
seconds and 65 seconds chosen empirically. Each frame is then divided
into four equal quadrants by placing one horizontal line and one
vertical line through the centre of the frame. Then, MediaPipe Face
Detection Lugaresi et al. () is applied to the bottom-right quarter
regions of these frames using a confidence threshold of 0.3, where the
returned normalized face coordinates are converted into pixel values and
adjusted back to the original frame coordinates to establish the
interpreter’s face position. We focus on this region as this is where
the sign language interpreter is expected to appear, reducing
unnecessary detections from the main video content. If a face is
detected, the pipeline proceeds to the next steps, otherwise a video is
skipped for a manual checking reducing the number of videos manually
checked. For videos detected to contain a face, the final interpreter
box is generated by expanding the detected face bounding box using
padding parameters ("1.8 × face_width" horizontally, "0.5 × face_height"
above, and "3.5 × face_height" below), ensuring that the crop includes
the interpreter’s upper body and surrounding white-bordered area of the
interpreter box. The quality of videos, and actual size of the
interpreter box varied across videos, making automatic box detection
with static bounding box extension sizes sometimes challenging.
Therefore, we manually checked failure cases of the automatic extraction
method and manually cropped the interpreter videos using the Mediapipe
interface to isolate the interpreter section from the rest of the video.

leading to misclassification. We leave further investigations and
improvements targeting trade-offs between TP, TN, FP, and FN for future

The parallel audio file can be trivially extracted from the $`.webm`$ or
$`.mp4`$ audio stream, which we stored as a $`.wav`$ file using
OpenCV ³³ 3 https://pypi.org/project/opencv-python/ and ffmpeg ⁴⁴ 4
https://www.ffmpeg.org. Our objective was to align spoken language
sentences with their corresponding sign language video sequences at
sentence-level. However, the sign videos cropped are long videos that
contain multiple sentences together with long audio files. To segment
the audio streams, we used Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR)
transcription to convert audio streams into text sequences for ease of
tokenization using the NLTK toolkit. We expand on this in the next
subsection – Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) Transcription.

To segment sign language videos, on the other hand, we used a trivial
motion detection algorithm that measures the average pixel-value
difference between adjacent frames to discern motion and non-motion
frames together with our proposed technique that uses gradient
information (e.g direction) to locate probable segmentation points. We
further defined N-breakpoints (where N is the number of sentences
defined in the ASR phase during tokenization) to segment the videos
using N lowest difference scores. We expand on each of these approaches
subsequently.

#### 3.1.1 Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) Transcription

To transcribe the audio streams into text files, we used four
state-of-the-art ASR models, namely, Whisper-large-v3 Radford et al.
(2022), WAV2VEC Baevski et al. (2020), Canary-Qwen-2.5B, and
Granite-speech-3.3-8b. The first two models are primarily selected due
to their widespread use in ASR research, while the last two are the top
two models on an Open ASR leaderboard hosted on HuggingFace⁵⁵ 5
https://huggingface.co/spaces/hf-audio/open_asr_leaderboard, where
adapted versions of Whisper and WAV2VEC are also found to be performing
competitively. However, after using these four models for transcription,
a manual inspection showed that the WAV2VEC transcriptions were not
satisfactory, due to poor punctuation, all words being upper-cased,
among other issues, and were left out for the rest of the experiments
(examples provided in the dataset release repository). Due to not having
annotated reference transcription, it was challenging to automatically
verify the actual quality of all the transcriptions from the models, and
select which model does better compared to others, other than manual
inspection of a few samples. In this case, we resorted to only verifying
using grammar checks and transcriptions’ completeness through manual
intervention. From this, we found Canary-2.5B to be generating quality
transcriptions; however, it generated incomplete transcriptions,
especially for long audio streams. Granite-speech-3.3-8b, on the other
hand generated inconsistent transcriptions with multiple repetitions of
phrases. The only model that generated quality transcriptions that
required very little intervention for fixing was the Whisper-large-v3
model, which we generally adopt as the primary ASR transcriber for the
remainder of this study.

Despite its strong performance, Whisper-large-v3 exhibited several
recurring issues, including – misspelled proper names - especially of
prominent public figures such as political leaders in South Africa
(E.g., the Deputy president of South Africa “Paul Mashatile”, where
Whisper-large-v3 generated - “Paul Machetele”, Canary-Qwen-2.5B
generated “Paul Mashaetele” (close enough), and Granite-speech-3.3-8b
generated “paul mashatele” (no capital letters to show this is a name of
a person)); hallucination - the silence at the end of the audio stream
provided noise to which Whisper-large-v3 transcribed as being audio
signals; traces of multilingual training - where traces of the Chinese
characters occur in transcriptions; repetition of sentences -
Whisper-large-v3 generated duplicated transcriptions where
interestingly, the last sentence would typically be a correction of the
preceding errors resulting in the correct transcription (however still
in the transcription); and incorrect sentence delimitation. However,
these issues are more pronounced in other models, while they seldom
occur in Whisper. Therefore, we used Whisper-large-v3 as the primary
transcription model for this study.

The resulting transcripts were manually post-edited to (i) standardize
punctuation and sentence boundaries, (ii) remove incorrect or
hallucinated segments, and (iii) translate code-switched words or
non-English phrases into English where applicable.

With clean transcription files available, we then used the number of
sentences within each transcription file as a proxy to determine the
number of times to segment the parallel video sequences. For example, if
the parallel transcription file of a specific sign language video
contains $`N`$ sentences, we immediately know we have to break the sign
language video into $`N`$ shorter clips. We expand on the video
segmentation algorithm subsequently.

#### 3.1.2 Sign Segmentation

Many complex algorithms have been proposed for motion detection Noh et
al. (2024) and sign language segmentation Moryossef et al. (2023).
However, in our study, we relied on a simple yet effective algorithm
that subtracts the computed average frame value between two adjacent
frames, as an indicator of motion occurrence or non-occurrence. Firstly,
the frames are converted into greyscale images, and then projected into
numpy array values as a preliminary pre-processing step. Then for each
frame, a single average pixel value is calculated by calculating the
average of all pixels to represent the frame. Then, by subtracting a
predecessor-frame average value from a successor-frame average value
(adjacent frames), we were able to get a value $`\delta`$ that
highlights changes in pixel values (between frames $`F_{t}`$ and
$`F_{t+1}`$ ), which was enough to infer the absence or presence of
motion, given the static background of our sign language videos (see
Figure 1(a) of the cropped section). That is, the only changes outside
of random noise were the movements relative to signing in most videos. A
low difference value indicates no movement, while large difference
values show plausible changes in movement or movements. From the list of
differences $`\delta`$, we identified the lowest $`N`$ values as our
breaking points, where $`N`$ corresponds to the number of sentences in
the equivalent transcription text file created in the ASR phase. That
is, for $`N`$ sentences in the transcription file, we expected $`N`$
short video clips as segments. However, this did not perform well, as
low points can appear within the occurrence of motion, leading to poorly
cut videos. To address this, we used a gradient measure to find $`N`$
points with the highest descent rates (indicating a drop (change) in the
motion difference leading to a plateau (low point), i.e., a stop or a
no-motion indicator), which improved breaking quality. That is, we used
gradients to find $`N`$ valleys of steepest descent. Empirical
evaluation showed that a window size of 3 frames to measure gradient
descent trajectories across the entire video showed improved results.
Additionally, adjusting the video segmentation points based on the
sentence-length distributions of the equivalent transcription files,
further improved the quality of the gradient-based segmentation as shown
in Figure 4(a). This is done so that the longest sentence would have the
longest video segment, while the shortest sentence in the transcription
file will have the shortest video clip. Following this process, we
chunked the long video stream into short segments that we aligned with
the sentences in the equivalent transcribed file temporally (in order of
appearance) to create our Maswao ’weakly’ aligned SASL datasets. This
process resulted in a corpus of 2944 video-to-text weakly aligned
sentence pairs and a diverse vocabulary set (see Table 1). More
sophisticated motion detection and segmentation approaches that are
heavily dependent on quality annotations such as prosody cues, or
optical flow data Moryossef et al. (2023); Rao et al. (2025) are left
for future work.

### 3.2 Sign-to-text Translation Baselines

We validated the quality of the weakly aligned Maswao dataset through a
series of training, development and testing experiments using three
state-of-the-art sign-to-text translation (STT) baselines: Neural SLT
(NSLT) Camgoz et al. (2018), Joint SLT Camgoz et al. (2020), and
Gloss-Free End-to-End SLT (GloFE) Lin et al. (2023). NSLT follows an
encoder–decoder sequence-to-sequence design based on recurrent neural
networks Luong et al. (2015), combined with either Luong et al. (2015)
or Bahdanau et al. (2014) attention mechanisms. In contrast, Joint SLT
and GloFE adopt Transformer backbones. This selection enables a direct
comparison between recurrent sequence-to-sequence models and modern
Transformer-based architectures. The baselines also differ in input
modality. NSLT and Joint SLT operate on RGB image input, using
AlexNet Krizhevsky et al. (2012) as the feature extractor, while GloFE
uses skeletal pose features estimated with MMPose Contributors (2020).
Evaluating both image-based and pose-based inputs provides a more
comprehensive assessment of the dataset and the effects of
representation choices. For evaluation, we report BLEU-1 to BLEU-4
(Bilingual Evaluation Understudy) and ROUGE-L (Recall-Oriented
Understudy for Gisting Evaluation), consistent with common practice in
STT benchmarking Yin and Read (2020); Wang et al. (2024); López et al.
(2025).

Baseline Models Hyperparameters We experimented using mostly default
hyper-parameter setups (where applicable) for training, development, and
testing as originally proposed in respective works of our baseline
models. These include using the same network architectures, such as the
number of layers, batch size (32), optimizing algorithms (Adam
optimizer), evaluation metrics – BLEU 1-4, with an addition of ChrF and
ROUGE-L. Although Transformers showed improved translation performance
over recurrent models, we further explored the hyper-parameter search by
comparing different batch sizes in the set $`\{16,32,48,64,128\}`$ with
various learning rates $`\{0.01,...,0.00001\}`$. This highlighted that a
batch size of 32 and a learning rate of $`0.0001`$ illustrated improved
performance as shown in Figure 5, which we used to train our model for
much longer epochs to establish our best performing model.

recurrent-based networks and skeletal-based models across all metrics,
BLEU 1-4, ChrF, and ROUGE-L. This is in line with observations
highlighted in existing literature, that RGB-based feature extractions
outperforms skeletal based feature extraction techniques Ko et al.
(2019); Orbay and Akarun (2020). This behaviour is compounded when we
use larger dimension size (4096) features of the AlexNet model. We
hypothesize that the performance degradation of skeletal estimation may
be affected by the quality of the sign language videos, which warrants
further investigation as studies showed that the quality of videos
negatively impacted the estimation of subtle but crucial features, such
the fingers De Coster et al. (2020). Compounding the poor performance of
competing models NSLT and GloFE, is that, due to a lack of matching
resources used in their respective studies, their techniques, such as
the contrastive learning (which required linguistic insights such as
parts of speech tags) in GloFE, and supervision signals from glosses in
NSLT where omitted from experiments, which may have negatively impacted
training performance. Consequently, we adopted Joint SLT, with optimal
training parameters (batch size=32, learning rate=$`1e^{-4}`$), with
longer early stopping patience=$`50`$ to train for longer times (epochs
= 616) to establish our model – Our STT. Our STT translation model
established a benchmark for our automatically collected and prepared,
weakly aligned Maswao dataset, curving new paths for future works in
sign language translation for the South African Sign Language (SASL)
community. Moreover, this study pioneers works in automatic collection
and preparation (i.e., alignment of sentences to parallel video chunks
in long videos) of low-resourced sign language datasets with similar
resource-availability characteristics.

Comparative analysis of recurrent architectures demonstrates that an
GRU-based recurrent models integrated with Luong attention Luong et al.
(2015) achieves superior performance compared to GRU with Bahdanau
attention Bahdanau et al. (2014) or LSTM configurations using either
Luong attention Luong et al. (2015) or Bahdanau attention Bahdanau et
al. (2014). Nevertheless, despite these findings, all recurrent-based
models underperform relative to Transformer architectures and
additionally incur substantially higher training times.

#### 4.1.1 Additional Results

To complement lexical-overlap metrics, which penalise valid paraphrases
and say little about meaning, we evaluated the best-performing
sign-to-text model with BERTScore and MetricX. BERTScore compares
candidate and reference sentences using contextual token embeddings from
a pretrained language model, computing precision, recall and F1-score
from soft token matches, so it rewards semantic similarity rather than
exact word overlap Zhang et al. (2019). MetricX-24 is a learned,
regression-based translation quality metric built on mT5 and trained on
human quality ratings, which predicts an error-style score where lower
values indicate better translations Juraska et al. (2024). Together,
these metrics matter for our weakly aligned Maswao dataset because a
sign video can be translated in several valid ways while only a single
reference is available, so generated outputs may be semantically related
to the reference without being an exact match. As shown in Table 4, our
best-performing model obtains a BERTScore of 12.20 (P), 14.60 (R) and
13.23 (F1), and a MetricX error score of 21.85. These values indicate
limited semantic fidelity and substantial translation errors, and the
higher recall than precision suggests that the model captures part of
the reference meaning but also produces additional or irrelevant
content. Such results are expected given the difficulty of open-domain
sign-to-text translation, which may be compounded by the diverse
vocabulary of Maswao and by signer variability. Importantly, the
objective of this work was not to develop the best possible model but to
propose a dataset; the model serves as a reproducible baseline showing
that Maswao can be used to train and evaluate sign-to-text systems, and
the remaining headroom provides a clear benchmark for future work. To
better understand these results, we further analyse the outputs of our
best model through a qualitative comparison between the generated and
reference translations, reported in the following section.

## 6 Conclusion

The Deaf and Hard-of-hearing communities have been sidelined from many
societal benefits, lending perpetuated marginalization of the
communities and reduced access to important socio-economic activities.
While advancements in sign-to-text and text-to-sign research have been
used to address this from a communication-facilitation perspective,
issues of unequal research efforts arise, where only a few languages
reap the benefits of important strides. To address this disparity, we
propose a reproducible framework that details the automatic collection
and preparation of digital news content of sign languages, using the
South African Sign Language (SASL) as a case study. Following this, we
create sequences of weakly aligned sign language short videos to spoken
language text sentences, using simple yet effective techniques such as
automated speech recognition, non-motion detection with gradient
descent, and sentence-length distributions of the accompanying
transcription file weighting. The quality of our datasets is then
evaluated using MMPose, AlexNet features, recurrent neural networks and
Transformer architectures, highlighting the efficacy of our automated
approach to dataset collection and preparation. Concretely, our results
show that Transformer-based modeling with RGB features extracted using
AlexNet, outperforms recurrent neural networks with the same features,
and Transformer models with skeletal pose estimation features, on our
proposed Maswao dataset.

We hope to see future works exploring other pose estimation techniques,
such as OpenPose and MediaPipe, for improved skeletal pose features.
Additionally, we hope to see improvements in video segmentation for
better alignments of sentences. Furthermore, with this complex task and
dataset, we hope to see improvements in model complexity to capture the
intricacies in sign language translation. Moreover, Text-to-sign (TTS)
has been considered a much more challenging task compared sign-to-text.
As such, our future works will build on this to include experiments for
TTS for building end-to-end sign language translation systems for Deaf
communities. Finally, our next milestone is to scale the framework we
proposed here to include multiple South African languages, with rigorous
validation steps across phases to improve dataset quality.

## 7 Limitations

Due to financial and resource constraints, this study did not annotate
ground truth video segments to evaluate the automatic segmentation
technique proposed to create our weakly aligned sentence-level Maswao
dataset. However, through a manual inspection of video samples, it can
be observed that our proposed technique segments videos exactly where
the movement of the signer is reaching a stop. Notably, isolated lexical
items (e.g., the word “Interesting”), which appeared as sentence number
93 from a total of 94 sentences, from the video with YouTube id
“1-UpxWii8Yg” was segmented correctly as shown in Segment 93 of folder
“1-UpxWii8Yg” of our released dataset. This provides evidence of the
effectiveness of the proposed gradient-descent and sentence-length–based
segmentation strategy.

Similarly, the study did not have ground truth or labeled transcriptions
to compare ASR techniques. As future work, it would be valuable to
produce ground-truth transcriptions for a representative subset of the
corpus and to investigate automatic transcriber selection using recent
SOTA large language models (e.g., ChatGPT, LLaMA, and Gemini). In
addition, exploring ranking-based selection frameworks may further
improve the reliability and consistency of transcription model choice.

Although the adopted Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) system produced
high-quality transcriptions overall, the generated transcripts were not
systematically validated by independent human translators. This is
particularly relevant in cases where news presenters or interviewed
persons code-switched into local languages, which can reduce ASR
accuracy. Instead in this work, the authors triaged through the
transcriptions to manually fix identified errors, similar to Tanzer and
Zhang (2025). While this approach enabled the construction of the
dataset, a comprehensive human validation of the transcriptions would
further improve dataset quality, quantify the impact of transcription
errors, and help identify potential sources of noise that remain
undetected.

Owing to YouTube’s licensing terms and our ethical responsibility to
protect the privacy of the sign language interpreters whose faces appear
in the videos, we are unable to publicly redistribute the original sign
language video clips included in the Maswao dataset. Instead, we release
the corresponding YouTube video identifiers (IDs) and the accompanying
sentence-level text annotations, allowing researchers to reconstruct the
dataset from the original publicly available sources while respecting
both platform licensing requirements and the privacy of the individuals
depicted.
