# Introduction

In recent years, neural network architectures and training recipes demonstrated unprecedented performance in a wide range of applications. In particular, <span acronym-label="DC" acronym-form="singular+short">DC</span> and <span acronym-label="PIT" acronym-form="singular+short">PIT</span> are pioneering approaches to separate unseen speakers in a single channel mixture . A plethora of subsequent work refined the architectures and training recipes, e.g. with respect to signal reconstruction performance by using a more direct reconstruction loss , using multiple complementary losses  or by addressing phase reconstruction .

In today’s applications, e.g. digital home assistants/ meeting assistants, multiple microphones are the de-facto standard. Thus, it is natural to generalize or apply neural network-based source separation to multi-channel scenarios. Earlier work simply exploited spatial information for beamforming to extract the sources as a final processing step but did not make any use of spatial information to improve the clustering performance itself . Subsequently, two rather different approaches emerged, which made direct use of both spatial and spectral features: On the one hand, directly feeding multi-channel features to a neural network yielded great improvements even for more than two channels . On the other hand, the embedding vectors stemming from either <span acronym-label="DC" acronym-form="singular+short">DC</span> or a <span acronym-label="DAN" acronym-form="singular+short">DAN</span> can be seen as additional spectral features for an integrated <span acronym-label="EM" acronym-form="singular+short">EM</span> algorithm which jointly models spatial and spectral features in a single probabilistic model .

However, all of the aforementioned approaches need access to parallel clean data, i.e. recordings of the speech source or speech image at the microphones before any mixing took place. Although it may seem that mixing signals artificially is sufficient for training, recent work with the CHiME 5 challenge dataset  turned out to be surprisingly complicated: Quasi-parallel data from the in-ear microphones was insufficiently related to the array recordings such that directly training a source separation neural network was impractical. In particular, a correct transmission model of the room, the Lombard effect, and realistic background noise is hard to entirely simulate artificially. One way may be to train an acoustic model with a neural network source separation system end-to-end. However, this again requires transcriptions for the mixtures at hand  and may require pretraining the individual components using again parallel data .

Therefore, we here propose to train a source separation neural network from scratch by leveraging only spatial cues already during training. This can be seen as a student-teacher approach , where the unsupervised teacher turns out to be weaker than the student. We demonstrate that a rough unsupervised spatial clustering approach is sufficient to guide the training of the neural network.

# Signal Model

A convolutive mixture of $`K`$ independent source signals $`s_{tfk}`$, captured by $`D`$ sensors is approximated in the <span acronym-label="STFT" acronym-form="singular+short">STFT</span> domain:
``` math
\begin{align}
\mathbf y_{tf}
= \sum\limits_k \mathbf h_{fk} \, s_{tfk} + \mathbf n_{tf}
= \sum\limits_k \mathbf x_{tfk} + \mathbf n_{tf}, \\[-7mm] \nonumber
\end{align}
```
where $`\mathbf y_{tf}`$, $`\mathbf h_{fk}`$ $`\mathbf n_{tf}`$, and $`\mathbf x_{tfk}`$ are the $`D`$-dimensional observed signal vector, the unknown acoustic transfer function vector of source $`k`$, the noise vector, and the source images at the sensors, respectively. Furthermore, $`t`$ and $`f`$ specify the time frame index and the frequency bin index, respectively. Since speech signals are sparse in the <span acronym-label="STFT" acronym-form="singular+short">STFT</span> domain, we may assume that a time frequency slot is occupied either by a single source and noise or by noise only.

# Proposed framework

We propose to use an unsupervised spatial clustering approach as a teacher for a neural network-based source separation student, thus rendering the whole setup to be unsupervised. Subsec. <a href="#ssec:cacgmm" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="ssec:cacgmm">3.1</a> introduces the <span acronym-label="cACGMM" acronym-form="singular+short">cACGMM</span> as an instance of an unsupervised spatial clustering algorithm and names inherent limitations. Subsec. <a href="#ssec:dc" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="ssec:dc">3.2</a> explains <span acronym-label="DC" acronym-form="singular+short">DC</span> as an example for a neural network-based source separation system which will then serve as the student. Lastly, Subsec. <a href="#ssec:training" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="ssec:training">3.3</a> and <a href="#ssec:prediction" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="ssec:prediction">3.4</a> detail, how training and prediction are performed.

## Unsupervised spatial clustering

We here decided to use a <span acronym-label="cACGMM" acronym-form="singular+short">cACGMM</span> for unsupervised spatial clustering , where the normalized complex-valued observation vectors $`\mathbf{\tilde y}_{tf} = \mathbf y_{tf} / \Vert \mathbf y_{tf} \Vert`$ are modeled as follows:
``` math
\begin{align*}
p(\mathbf{\tilde y}_{tf}; \boldsymbol\theta) = \sum_k \pi_{kf} \mathrm{cACG}(\mathbf{\tilde y}_{tf}, \mathbf B_{kf})
\end{align*}
```
with the model parameters $`\boldsymbol\theta = \left\{\pi_{kf}, \mathbf B_{kf} \forall k,f\right\}`$. The observation model is a complex angular central Gaussian:
``` math
\begin{align}
\mathrm{cACG}(\mathbf{\tilde y}_{tf}, \mathbf B_{kf})
&= \frac{(D-1)!}{2\pi^{D}\det \mathbf B_{kf}} \frac{1}{\left(\mathbf{\tilde y}_{tf}^{\mathsf H} \mathbf B_{kf}^{-1} \mathbf{\tilde y}_{tf}\right)^{D}}.
\end{align}
```

All latent variables and parameters can be estimated on each mixture signal using an <span acronym-label="EM" acronym-form="singular+short">EM</span> algorithm<sup><a href="#pb_bss" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="pb_bss">1</a></sup>. The <span acronym-label="EM" acronym-form="singular+short">EM</span> update equations coincide with the update equations of the <span acronym-label="TV-cGMM" acronym-form="singular+short">TV-cGMM</span>  as demonstrated in the appendix of . The obtained class affiliation posteriors can then be used to extract the sources either by masking or beamforming.

However, it is worth noting that mixture models, in general, tend to be very susceptible to initialization. This will be addressed in more detail in the evaluation section.

The <span acronym-label="cACGMM" acronym-form="singular+short">cACGMM</span> neglects frequency dependencies. Thus, when used without any kind of guidance, it will yield a solution where the speaker index is inconsistent over frequency bins. This issue is the so-called frequency permutation problem . We address it by calculating that <span acronym-label="PA" acronym-form="singular+short">PA</span> (bin by bin) which maximizes the correlation of the masks along neighboring frequencies similar to [^1].

## Deep Clustering

<span acronym-label="DC" acronym-form="singular+short">DC</span> is a technique which aims at blindly separating unseen speakers in a single-channel mixture. The training procedure described in the original work  assumes that <span acronym-label="IBM" acronym-form="plural+short">IBMs</span> for each speaker are available to train a multi-layer <span acronym-label="BLSTM" acronym-form="singular+short">BLSTM</span>  to map from $`T \cdot F`$ spectral features (e.g. log spectrum) to the same number of $`E`$-dimensional embedding vectors $`\mathbf e_{tf}`$, where $`\Vert \mathbf e_{tf} \Vert^2 = 1`$. The objective during training is to minimize the Frobenius norm of the difference between the estimated and true affinity matrix (for a discussion of improved loss functions see  ):
``` math
\begin{align}
\ell = \left\Vert\smash{\mathbf{\hat A}} - \mathbf A^{\vphantom{\mathsf T}}\right\Vert^2_{\mathrm F}
= \left\Vert\mathbf E \mathbf E^{\mathsf T} - \mathbf C \mathbf C^{\mathsf T}\right\Vert^2_{\mathrm F},\label{dc_loss}
\end{align}
```
where $`\mathbf{\hat A}`$ and $`\mathbf A`$ are the estimated and ground truth affinity matrices. The entries $`A_{n,n'}`$ encode, whether observation $`n`$ and $`n'`$ belong to the same source ($`A_{n,n'}=1`$, and zero else). Correspondingly, the embeddings are stacked in a single matrix $`\mathbf E`$ with shape $`(TF \times E)`$ and the ground truth one-hot vectors describing which time frequency slot belongs to which source are stacked in a single matrix $`\mathbf C`$ with shape $`(TF \times K)`$, such that $`C_{nk} = 1`$, if observation $`n`$ belongs to source $`k`$ and $`C_{nk} = 0`$ otherwise.

During training, the network is encouraged to move embeddings belonging to the same source closer together while pushing embeddings which belong to different sources further apart. After training, the embeddings, which are normalized to unit-length, can be clustered to obtain time frequency masks for each source. The original work used k-means clustering. This yields masks which can be used in a subsequent source extraction scheme e.g. masking or beamforming.

## Unsupervised network training

To train a <span acronym-label="DC" acronym-form="singular+short">DC</span> system, we need to optimize the affinity loss in Eq. <a href="#dc_loss" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="dc_loss">[dc_loss]</a>. Since ground truth is not available, we instead minimize the Frobenius norm of the difference between the affinity matrix of the embeddings $`\mathbf E \mathbf E^{\mathsf T}`$ and the affinity matrix according to masks predicted by an unsupervised clustering approach. Here, we use the class predictions (masks) $`M_{ktf}^{'}`$ of a <span acronym-label="cACGMM" acronym-form="singular+short">cACGMM</span> (Subsec. <a href="#ssec:cacgmm" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="ssec:cacgmm">3.1</a>) and apply an additional frequency permutation alignment (Subsec. <a href="#ssec:cacgmm" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="ssec:cacgmm">3.1</a>) to obtain $`M_{ktf}^{''}`$ (Fig. <a href="#fig:overview" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="fig:overview">1</a> (a)), which is then used to guide the optimization.

It is worth noting that the predicted classes (masks) from the spatial mixture model are first of all less sharp than the unavailable <span acronym-label="IBM" acronym-form="plural+short">IBMs</span>. Furthermore, the predicted masks often contain frequency permutations errors, when the permutation alignment step did not resolve all permutations (see Fig. <a href="#fig:masks" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="fig:masks">2</a>, left). The assumption which has to be proven in the evaluation section, therefore, is that on average, the <span acronym-label="cACGMM" acronym-form="singular+short">cACGMM</span> results are sufficiently good.

Since any kind of ground-truth signal is not available in the unsupervised case, early stopping  can only be performed with respect to the aforementioned loss and not with respect to <span acronym-label="SDR" acronym-form="singular+short">SDR</span> gains or similar.

## Prediction

To predict masks at test time two different ways are natural to investigate. First, one can use the trained <span acronym-label="DC" acronym-form="singular+short">DC</span> network to predict embeddings $`\mathbf e_{tf}`$ which are then clustered using k-means as in Fig. <a href="#fig:overview" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="fig:overview">1</a> (b). This approach is the recommended approach according to . Second, one may use the k-means masks as initialization for a <span acronym-label="cACGMM" acronym-form="singular+short">cACGMM</span> as in Fig. <a href="#fig:overview" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="fig:overview">1</a> (c). This can be seen as some kind of weak integration (in contrast to ) between the <span acronym-label="DC" acronym-form="singular+short">DC</span> model, which just uses spectral features and the <span acronym-label="cACGMM" acronym-form="singular+short">cACGMM</span> which just uses spatial features due to the normalization as mentioned in Subsec. <a href="#ssec:cacgmm" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="ssec:cacgmm">3.1</a>.

# Beamforming

Both aforementioned models provide a time-frequency mask for each speaker and noise. This can be multiplied with the observed signal <span acronym-label="STFT" acronym-form="singular+short">STFT</span> to extract each of the sources. Alternatively, we may use it for classic statistical beamforming as a linear time-invariant way to extract each source. To do so, we first calculate covariance matrices for each target speaker:
``` math
\begin{align}
\boldsymbol\Phi_{kf}^{\mathrm{(target)}}
&= \left. \sum_t M_{ktf}^{\mathrm{(target)}} \mathbf y_{tf} \mathbf y_{tf}^{\mathsf H} \middle/
\sum_t M_{ktf}^{\mathrm{(target)}} \right..
\end{align}
```
Similarly, the covariance matrix of all interferences and noise is calculated with $`\smash{M_{ktf}^{\mathrm{(inter)}} = 1 - M_{ktf}^{\mathrm{(target)}}}`$.

These covariance matrices can then be used to derive a statistically optimal beamformer. In this particular case, we opted for a specific formulation of the <span acronym-label="MVDR" acronym-form="singular+short">MVDR</span> beamformer which avoids explicit knowledge of any kind of steering vector :
``` math
\begin{align}
\mathbf w
&= \frac{1}{\lambda_{kf}} \boldsymbol \Phi_{kf} \mathbf u_k, ~~\text{with}~~
\boldsymbol \Phi_{kf} = {\boldsymbol\Phi_{kf}^{\mathrm{(inter)}}}^{-1} \boldsymbol\Phi_{kf}^{\mathrm{(target)}},
\end{align}
```
where $`\lambda_{kf} = \mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\left(\boldsymbol \Phi_{kf}\right)`$ and $`\mathbf u_k = [0 ... 1 ... 0]^{\mathsf T}`$ selects the reference microphone. Here, the reference microphone is selected by maximizing the expected <span acronym-label="SNR" acronym-form="singular+short">SNR</span> gain . The beamforming vector is then used to linearly project the multi-channel mixture to a single-channel estimate of clean speech: $`z_{ktf} = \smash{\mathbf w_{kf}^{\mathsf H} \mathbf y_{tf}}`$.

# Acoustic model

For an objective comparison, we train a state of the art <span acronym-label="AM" acronym-form="singular+short">AM</span>. The hybrid <span acronym-label="AM" acronym-form="singular+short">AM</span> consists of a combination of a wide residual network to model local context and a <span acronym-label="BLSTM" acronym-form="singular+short">BLSTM</span> to model long term dependencies. The <span acronym-label="AM" acronym-form="singular+short">AM</span> is thus dubbed wide bi-directional residual network . To train the <span acronym-label="AM" acronym-form="singular+short">AM</span> alignments are extracted with a vanilla DNN-HMM recipe from Kaldi . The <span acronym-label="AM" acronym-form="singular+short">AM</span> is trained on artificially reverberated <span acronym-label="WSJ" acronym-form="singular+short">WSJ</span> utterances without any interfering speaker. Thus, the <span acronym-label="AM" acronym-form="singular+short">AM</span> never saw mixed speech and never saw possible artifacts produced by any kind of separation system. For decoding, we use the <span acronym-label="WSJ" acronym-form="singular+short">WSJ</span> trigram language model without additional rescoring.

To evaluate the proposed algorithm, we artificially generated 30000, 500 and 1500 six channel mixtures with a sampling rate of 8 kHz with source signals obtained from three non-overlapping <span acronym-label="WSJ" acronym-form="singular+short">WSJ</span> sets (`train_si284`, `cv_dev93`, `test_eval92`). We generated room impulse responses with the image method  with random room dimensions, a random position of the circular array and random positions of the two concurring speakers. The minimum angular distance was set to 15 °. The reverberation time (T60) was uniformly sampled between 200 and 500 ms. Additive white Gaussian noise with 20 to 30 dB <span acronym-label="SNR" acronym-form="singular+short">SNR</span> was added to the mixture. The source separation algorithms operate on <span acronym-label="STFT" acronym-form="singular+short">STFT</span> signals with a <span acronym-label="DFT" acronym-form="singular+short">DFT</span> size of 512 and a shift of 128. The <span acronym-label="DC" acronym-form="singular+short">DC</span> network consists of 2 <span acronym-label="BLSTM" acronym-form="singular+short">BLSTM</span> layers with 600 forward and 600 backward units and a final linear layer which yields $`E=20`$ dimensional embeddings. The <span acronym-label="AM" acronym-form="singular+short">AM</span> uses 40 Mel filterbank features extracted with an <span acronym-label="STFT" acronym-form="singular+short">STFT</span> with a <span acronym-label="DFT" acronym-form="singular+short">DFT</span> size of 256, a window size of 200 and a shift of 80.

To get a good impression of the system performance, we present results in terms of mean and <span acronym-label="STD" acronym-form="singular+short">STD</span> of BSS-Eval <span acronym-label="SDR" acronym-form="singular+short">SDR</span> gain , mean and <span acronym-label="STD" acronym-form="singular+short">STD</span> of invasive <span acronym-label="SDR" acronym-form="singular+short">SDR</span> gain similar as in , PESQ gain , STOI gain  and finally <span acronym-label="WER" acronym-form="plural+short">WERs</span>. The invasive <span acronym-label="SDR" acronym-form="singular+short">SDR</span> is the power ratio of applying the obtained mask or beamforming vector to the target speech image and the sum of all interference images.

First of all, we evaluated unsupervised spatial clustering with a <span acronym-label="cACGMM" acronym-form="singular+short">cACGMM</span> using a random initialization. Comparing row 1 and 7, we observe that although masking yields higher BSS-Eval <span acronym-label="SDR" acronym-form="singular+short">SDR</span> and STOI gains, the invasive <span acronym-label="SDR" acronym-form="singular+short">SDR</span> gain, <span acronym-label="PESQ" acronym-form="singular+short">PESQ</span> gain and foremost the <span acronym-label="WER" acronym-form="singular+short">WER</span> are better for the <span acronym-label="MVDR" acronym-form="singular+short">MVDR</span> beamforming approach. This can be explained by the observation that masking often leads to musical tones which are avoided with a time-invariant projection approach such as beamforming.

Next, we trained the <span acronym-label="DC" acronym-form="singular+short">DC</span> system according to the proposed training scheme using masks exported according to row 1 or 7. The performance of the unsupervisedly trained <span acronym-label="DC" acronym-form="singular+short">DC</span> (row 8, abbreviated as U-DC) system does not quite reach the performance of the supervised <span acronym-label="DC" acronym-form="singular+short">DC</span> in row 10 (S-DC). Comparing the training times, the unsupervised approach takes at least twice as many training steps (here 500k steps, batch size 4) as the supervised <span acronym-label="DC" acronym-form="singular+short">DC</span> system. The sometimes misleading <span acronym-label="cACGMM" acronym-form="singular+short">cACGMM</span> masks which result in more noisy gradients are a possible explanation.

Rows 3 and 9 summarize the results obtained when initializing the <span acronym-label="cACGMM" acronym-form="singular+short">cACGMM</span> with the U-DC result of row 2 or 8, respectively. When using masking, this results in a gain of 2.6 dB invasive <span acronym-label="SDR" acronym-form="singular+short">SDR</span> over the randomly initialized <span acronym-label="cACGMM" acronym-form="singular+short">cACGMM</span> in row 1. Also, when using beamforming, a gain of 2.6 dB invasive <span acronym-label="SDR" acronym-form="singular+short">SDR</span> can be observed. Comparing this with supervisedly trained <span acronym-label="DC" acronym-form="singular+short">DC</span> in row 10 and the S-DC initialized <span acronym-label="cACGMM" acronym-form="singular+short">cACGMM</span> in row 11, it can be seen that the proposed approach outperforms both oracle baselines. Interestingly, the initialization is sufficient to address the permutation problem such that an additional alignment step is not necessary anymore. It is therefore valid to conclude that the main drawback of the teacher is its initialization. However, the performance of a <span acronym-label="cACGMM" acronym-form="singular+short">cACGMM</span> initialized with oracle ideal binary masks is not quite reached. Overall, the spatial mixture model initialized with the unsupervised <span acronym-label="DC" acronym-form="singular+short">DC</span> (row 9) result yields a relative <span acronym-label="WER" acronym-form="singular+short">WER</span> reduction of 26 % over the unsupervised teacher (row 7) and beats the best supervised system (row 11) by 2 % relative.

# Conclusion

In this work, we demonstrated that a neural network-based blind source separation system can indeed be trained from scratch without any clean or parallel data. We opted for a training scheme with an unsupervised teacher and ended up with a student outperforming the teacher. This is of particular interest when addressing real data such as the CHiME 5 challenge recordings, where regular training is impeded due to the lack of well-matching targets. We see this work as an alternative/ complementary tool to train the frond-end entirely end-to-end or to better match real recordings and plan to extend the present work to CHiME 5 challenge recordings.
