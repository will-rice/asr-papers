# A Unified Framework of Constrained Robust Submodular Optimization with Applications

\nameRishabh Iyer \emailrishabh.iyer@utdallas.edu\
\addrUniversity of Texas at Dallas, USA

###### Abstract

Robust optimization is becoming increasingly important in machine learning applications. In this paper, we study a unified framework of robust submodular optimization. We study this problem both from a minimization and maximization perspective (previous work has only focused on variants of robust submodular maximization). We do this under a broad range of combinatorial constraints including cardinality, knapsack, matroid as well as graph based constraints such as cuts, paths, matchings and trees. Furthermore, we also study robust submodular minimization and maximization under multiple submodular upper and lower bound constraints. We show that all these problems are motivated by important machine learning applications including robust data subset selection, robust co-operative cuts and robust co-operative matchings. In each case, we provide scalable approximation algorithms and also study hardness bounds. Finally, we empirically demonstrate the utility of our algorithms on synthetic data, and real world applications of robust cooperative matchings for image correspondence, robust data subset selection for speech recognition, and image collection summarization with multiple queries.

## 1 Introduction

Submodular functions provide a rich class of expressible models for a variety of machine learning problems. They occur naturally in two flavors. In minimization problems, they model notions of cooperation, attractive potentials, and economies of scale, while in maximization problems, they model aspects of coverage, diversity, and information. A set function $`f:{2^{V}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}}`$ over a finite set $`V = {\{ 1,2,\ldots,n\}}`$ is *submodular* (?) if for all subsets $`{S,T} \subseteq V`$, it holds that

$`{{f\hspace{0pt}{(S)}} + {f\hspace{0pt}{(T)}}} \geq {{f\hspace{0pt}{({S \cup T})}} + {f\hspace{0pt}{({S \cap T})}}}`$

Given a set $`S \subseteq V`$, we define the *gain* of an element $`j \notin S`$ in the context $`S`$ as $`{f\hspace{0pt}{(\left. j \middle| S \right.)}} = {{f\hspace{0pt}{({S \cup j})}} - {f\hspace{0pt}{(S)}}}`$. A perhaps more intuitive characterization of submodularity is as follows: a function $`f`$ is submodular if it satisfies *diminishing marginal returns*, namely $`{f\hspace{0pt}{(\left. j \middle| S \right.)}} \geq {f\hspace{0pt}{(\left. j \middle| T \right.)}}`$ for all $`{S \subseteq T},{j \notin T}`$, and is *monotone* if $`{f\hspace{0pt}{(\left. j \middle| S \right.)}} \geq 0`$ for all $`{j \notin S},{S \subseteq V}`$.

Two central optimization problems involving submodular functions are submodular minimization and submodular maximization. Moreover, it is often natural to want to optimize these functions subject to combinatorial constraints (?, ?, ?, ?).

In this paper, we shall study the problem of robust submodular optimization. Often times in applications we want to optimize several objectives (or criteria) together. There are two natural formulations of this. One is the average case, where we can optimize the (weighted) sum of the submodular functions. Examples of this have been studied in data summarization applications (?, ?, ?). The other is robust or worst case, where we want to minimize (or maximize) the maximum (equivalently minimum) among the functions. Examples of this have been proposed for sensor placement and observation selection (?). Robust or worst case optimization is becoming increasingly important since solutions achieved by minimization and maximization can be unstable to perturbations in data. Often times submodular functions in applications are instantiated from various properties of the data (features, similarity functions, clusterings etc.) and obtaining results which are robust to perturbations and variations in this data is critical.

Given monotone submodular functions $`f_{1},f_{2},\cdots,f_{l}`$ to minimize and $`g_{1},g_{2},\cdots,g_{k}`$ to maximize, consider the following problems:

$`{\text{Problem 1:~}\hspace{0pt}{\min\limits_{X \in \mathcal{C}}{\max\limits_{{i = 1}:l}f_{i}}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}},{\text{Problem 2:~}\hspace{0pt}{\max\limits_{X \in \mathcal{C}}{\min\limits_{{i = 1}:k}g_{i}}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}`$

$`\mathcal{C}`$ stands for combinatorial constraints, which include cardinality, matroid, spanning trees, cuts, s-t paths etc. We shall call these problems Robust-SubMin and Robust-SubMax. Note that when $`{k = 1},{l = 1}`$, we get back constrained submodular minimization and constrained submodular maximization. We will also study special cases of Robust-SubMin and Robust-SubMax when the constraints are defined via another submodular function. We study two problems: a) minimize the functions $`f_{i}`$’s while having lower bound constraints on the $`g_{i}`$’s (Robust-scsc), and b) maximize the functions $`g_{i}`$’s subject to upper bound constraints on $`f_{i}`$’s (Robust-scsk).

$`{\left. {\text{Problem 3:~}\hspace{0pt}{\min\limits_{X \subseteq V}{\max\limits_{{i = 1}:l}f_{i}}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \middle| {g_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \right. \geq c_{i}},{i = {1,{\cdots\hspace{0pt}k}}}`$$`{\left. {\text{Problem 4:~}\hspace{0pt}{\max\limits_{X \subseteq V}{\min\limits_{{i = 1}:k}g_{i}}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \middle| {f_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \right. \leq b_{i}},{i = {1,{\cdots\hspace{0pt}l}}}`$

Problems 3 and 4 attempt to simultaneously minimize the functions $`f_{i}`$ while maximizing $`g_{i}`$. Finally, a natural extension of these problems is to have a joint average/worst case objective where we optimize $`{{({1 - \lambda})}\hspace{0pt}{\min_{{i = 1}:k}g_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} + {{\lambda/k}\hspace{0pt}{\sum_{i = 1}^{k}{g_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}}}`$ and $`{{({1 - \lambda})}\hspace{0pt}{\max_{{i = 1}:l}f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} + {{\lambda/l}\hspace{0pt}{\sum_{i = 1}^{l}{f_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}}}`$ in Problems 1 - 4. We shall call these problems the Mixed versions (Mixed-SubMax, Mixed-SubMin etc.) However we point out that the Mixed case for Problems 1-4 is a special case of Robust optimization. Its easy to see that we can convert this into a Robust formulation by defining $`{f_{i}'\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} = {{{({1 - \lambda})}\hspace{0pt}f_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} + {{\lambda/l}\hspace{0pt}{\sum_{i = 1}^{l}{f_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}}}}`$ and $`{g_{i}'\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} = {{{({1 - \lambda})}\hspace{0pt}g_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} + {{\lambda/k}\hspace{0pt}{\sum_{i = 1}^{k}{g_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}}}}`$ and then defining the Robust optimization on $`f_{i}'`$’s and $`g_{i}'`$.

#### Special Cases of Problems 3 and 4.

We get several special cases of Robust-scsc and Robust-scsk. When $`l = k = 1`$, Problems 3 and 4 generalize Scsc and Scsk (?). Similarly, when the functions $`f_{i}`$’s are modular, Problem 4 (Robust-scsk) becomes Robust submodular maximization (Robust-SubMax) under multiple knapsack constraints, which in turn generalizes Robust-SubMax subject to a single knapsack constraint (studied in (?)), and single cardinality constraint (studied in (?). When $`f_{i}`$’s are modular and $`k = 1`$, Problem 3 (Robust-scsc) becomes Robust Submodular Set Cover (Robust-ssc), and when $`g_{i}`$’s are modular, Problem 3 becomes robust submodular minimization (Robust-SubMin) under multiple knapsack constraints. Similarly, when $`g_{i}`$’s are modular, Problem 4 (Robust-scsk) becomes Robust Submodular Span (Robust-ss) problem.

#### Conference version of this paper:

This work significantly extends our conference paper (?). The conference version just studied Problem 1, while in this work, we also study Problems 2, 3 and 4 and provide a unified perspective to the different instantiations of Robust Submodular Optimization. In particular, we provide algorithms for Problem 2 with multiple knapsack constraints, and introduce and study Problems 3 and 4 (which to the best of our knowledge are new problems) with multiple modular and submodular constraints. This paper also significantly extends upon the conference version from an empirical perspective, by considering new applications of robust data selection with vocabulary constraints, and query focused summarization with multiple queries/targets (see motivating applications below).

#### Robust Co-operative Cuts:

Markov random fields with pairwise attractive potentials occur naturally in modeling image segmentation and related applications (?). While models are tractably solved using graph-cuts, they suffer from the shrinking bias problem, and images with elongated edges are not segmented properly. When modeled via a submodular function, however, the cost of a cut is not just the sum of the edge weights, but a richer function that allows cooperation between edges, and yields superior results on many challenging tasks (see, for example, the results of the image segmentations in (?)). This was achieved in (?) by partitioning the set of edges $`\mathcal{E}`$ of the grid graph into groups of similar edges (or types) $`\mathcal{E}_{1},\cdots,\mathcal{E}_{k}`$, and defining a function $`{{f\hspace{0pt}{(S)}} = {\sum_{i = 1}^{k}{\psi_{i}\hspace{0pt}{({w\hspace{0pt}{({S \cap \mathcal{E}_{i}})}})}}}},{S \subseteq \mathcal{E}}`$, where $`\psi_{i}`$s are concave functions and $`w`$ encodes the edge potentials. This ensures that we offer a *discount* to edges of the same type. The left image in Figure 1 illustrates co-operative cuts (?). However, instead of taking a single clustering (i.e. a single group of edges $`\mathcal{E}_{1},\cdots,\mathcal{E}_{k}`$), one can instantiate several clusterings $`\{{(\mathcal{E}_{1}^{1},\cdots,\mathcal{E}_{k}^{1})},\cdots,{(\mathcal{E}_{1}^{l},\cdots,\mathcal{E}_{k}^{l})}\}`$ and define a robust objective: $`{{f_{r\hspace{0pt}o\hspace{0pt}b\hspace{0pt}u\hspace{0pt}s\hspace{0pt}t}\hspace{0pt}{(S)}} = {\max_{{i = 1}:l}\hspace{0pt}{\sum_{j = 1}^{k}{\psi_{j}\hspace{0pt}{({w\hspace{0pt}{({S \cap \mathcal{E}_{j}^{i}})}})}}}}},{S \subseteq \mathcal{E}}`$. Minimizing $`f_{r\hspace{0pt}o\hspace{0pt}b\hspace{0pt}u\hspace{0pt}s\hspace{0pt}t}`$ over the family of s-t cuts can achieve segmentations that are robust to many such groupings of edges thereby achieving *robust co-operative segmentations*. We call this *Robust Co-operative Cuts* and this becomes instance of Robust-SubMin with $`f_{r\hspace{0pt}o\hspace{0pt}b\hspace{0pt}u\hspace{0pt}s\hspace{0pt}t}`$ defined above and the constraint $`\mathcal{C}`$ being the family of s-t cuts. Cooperative cuts is illustrated in Figure 1 (top-left).

#### Robust Co-operative Matchings:

The problem of matching key-points in images, also called image correspondence, is an important problem in computer vision (?). The simplest model for this problem constructs a matching with linear scores, i.e., a max bipartite matching, called a *linear assignment*. This model does not allow a representation of interaction between the pixels, and as a result, we see many spurious matches in the results of min-matching (see top right group of plots in Figure 1). One way to address this is by handling interaction or cooperation between the keypoints (?), and one kind of desirable interaction is that similar or neighboring pixels be matched together. We can achieve this as follows. First we cluster the key-points in the two images into $`k`$ groups and the induced clustering of edges can be given a discount via a submodular function. Let $`{\{ V_{i}^{(1)}\}}_{i = 1}^{k}`$ and $`{\{ V_{i}^{(2)}\}}_{i = 1}^{k}`$ be the two sets of clusters. We then compute the linear assignment problem, letting $`\mathcal{M} \subseteq \mathcal{E}`$ be the resulting maximum matching. We then partition the edge set $`\mathcal{E} = {\mathcal{E}_{1} \cup \mathcal{E}_{2} \cup {\ldots\hspace{0pt}\mathcal{E}_{k}} \cup \mathcal{E}'}`$ where $`\mathcal{E}_{i} = {\mathcal{M} \cap {({V_{\ell}^{(1)} \times V_{s}^{(2)}})}}`$ for $`{\ell,s} \in {\{ 1,2,\ldots,k\}}`$ corresponding to the $`i`$’th largest intersection, and $`\mathcal{E}' = {\{\mathcal{E}\backslash \cup_{i = 1}^{k}\mathcal{E}_{i}\}}`$ are the remaining edges either that were not matched or that did not lie within a frequently associated pair of image key-point clusters. We then define a submodular function as follows: $`{f\hspace{0pt}{(S)}} = {{\sum_{i = 1}^{k}{\psi_{i}\hspace{0pt}{({w\hspace{0pt}{({S \cap \mathcal{E}_{i}})}})}}} + {w\hspace{0pt}{({S \cap \mathcal{E}'})}}}`$, which provides an additional discount to the edges $`{\{\mathcal{E}_{i}\}}_{i = 1}^{k}`$ corresponding to key-points that were frequently associated in the initial pass. The difference between minimum matching and cooperative matching is shown in the top-right plots in Figure 1, and we show that through cooperation, we can effectively remove these spurious matchings. Similar to cooperative cuts, we can consider several clusterings, and try to solve a robust cooperative matching problem. To do this, we can consider several clusterings $`\{{(\mathcal{E}_{1}^{1},\cdots,\mathcal{E}_{k}^{1})},\cdots,{(\mathcal{E}_{1}^{l},\cdots,\mathcal{E}_{k}^{l})}\}`$, and define $`f_{i}{(S)} = \sum_{j = 1}^{k}\psi_{j}{(w{(S \cap \mathcal{E}_{j}^{i})})} + w{(S \cap \mathcal{E}^{i}{{}_{}')}}`$. We then seem to minimize $`{f_{r\hspace{0pt}o\hspace{0pt}b\hspace{0pt}u\hspace{0pt}s\hspace{0pt}t}\hspace{0pt}{(S)}} = {{\min_{i}f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(S)}}`$ subject to a matching constraint, and this becomes an instance of Robust-SubMin.

#### Robust Data Subset Selection:

Submodular functions have successfully been used for several data subset selection in domains such as image classification (?), speech recognition (?) and machine translation (?). (?, ?) prove that the problem of selecting the maximum likelihood subset of a training dataset is a submodular optimization problem for several classifiers and loss functions. Another approach, which we shall study in this paper, is selecting data-sets which are robust to several data subset selection models $`g_{1},\cdots,g_{k}`$. These models can be defined via different submodular functions, different choice of features, perturbations in the feature space and different target distributions where we want to use these models. In this case, we can pose this as an instance of Robust-SubMax where we want to maximize the minimum among the utility functions Furthermore, it is also natural to select subsets of data which minimize the complexity of the dataset (?, ?). In this case, the functions $`f`$ captures the complexity of the data-sets $`X`$ (for example, vocabulary size in speech recognition and number of objects in object detection). This is naturally an instance of Robust-SCSK with $`g_{i}`$ being the data selection models, while $`f`$ is the complexity of the selected subset ($`l = 1`$). We can also define multiple complexity functions $`f_{i}`$, where each function is defined via different perturbations in the vocabulary (obtained by say randomly deleting a certain fraction of words from the vocabulary function).

#### Data Subset Selection and Data Summarization with Multiple Targets/Queries:

In many applications, it makes sense to select a subset (or summary) $`X \subseteq V`$, which is both diverse but also relevant to a given target or query $`Q`$ (?, ?, ?, ?). One way to frame such a problem is to maximize a diversity function $`g\hspace{0pt}{(X)}`$ while simultaneously minimizing the conditional gain $`h\hspace{0pt}{(\left. X \middle| Q \right.)}`$ (?, ?), where $`h`$ is an appropriate submodular function for the query relevance. Note that $`h`$ and $`g`$ could also be the same function. Two natural formulations of this are $`\max_{X \subseteq V}g{(X)}|h{(X|Q)} \leq \epsilon`$ or $`\left. {{\min_{X \subseteq V}h}\hspace{0pt}{(\left. X \middle| Q \right.)}} \middle| {g\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \right. \geq c`$ – these problems are instances of Scsc and Scsk respectively (?). A simple and realistic extension of this is where we have multiple query sets (equivalently targets) $`Q_{1},\cdots,Q_{l}`$. Essentially this means we want to maximize $`g\hspace{0pt}{(X)}`$ while minimizing $`h{(X|Q_{i}}`$ for $`i = {1,\cdots,l}`$. In other words, we want to select a diverse summary while yet being *equally* relevant to all the $`l`$ queries. Two natural formulations are: $`\max_{X \subseteq V}g{(X)}|h{(X|Q_{i})} \leq \epsilon_{i},i = 1:l,`$ and $`\left. {{\min_{X \subseteq V}{\max_{{i = 1}:l}h}}\hspace{0pt}{(\left. X \middle| Q_{i} \right.)}} \middle| {g\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \right. \geq c`$. These are instances of Robust-Scsk and Robust-Scsc with $`k = 1`$ and $`l`$ the number of queries. In our experiments, we study the Robust-Scsc variant, and show that the robust approach ensures fairness among the queries (i.e. the summary is equally relevant of each query) much better compared to just taking a sum of the conditional functions ($`\sum_{i = 1}^{l}{h\hspace{0pt}{(\left. X \middle| Q_{i} \right.)}}`$ or the union conditional function ($`h\hspace{0pt}{(\left. X \middle| {\cup_{i = 1}^{l}Q_{i}} \right.)}`$. The application of query focused summarization with multiple queries is illustrated in Figure 1 (right), where the goal is to select a summary which is relevant to all four queries simultaneously. Another related application of this is targeted data subset selection for machine learning, where the goal is to select among a set of unlabeled data points, a set of points related to (possibly multiple) targets (?). Examples of targets could be slices where the model is currently not performing well (e.g. in a self driving car application, the model may not be performing well to detect people at night, or vehicles in rainy and foggy conditions). It is also realistic in such scenarios that we have multiple targets/queries and the goal is to select a subset of unlabeled data while being relevant to all the target slices.

### 1.2 Related Work

Submodular Minimization and Maximization: Most Constrained forms of submodular optimization (minimization and maximization) are NP hard even when $`f`$ is monotone (?, ?, ?, ?). The greedy algorithm achieves a $`1 - {1/e}`$ approximation for cardinality constrained maximization and a $`1/2`$ approximation under matroid constraints (?). (?) achieved a tight $`1 - {1/e}`$ approximation for matroid constraints using the continuous greedy. (?) later provided a similar $`1 - {1/e}`$ approximation under multiple knapsack constraints. Constrained submodular minimization is much harder – even with simple constraints such as a cardinality lower bound constraints, the problems are not approximable better than a polynomial factor of $`\Omega\hspace{0pt}{(\sqrt{n})}`$ (?). Similarly the problems of minimizing a submodular function under covering constraints (?), spanning trees, perfect matchings, paths (?) and cuts (?) have similar polynomial hardness factors. In all of these cases, matching upper bounds (i.e approximation algorithms) have been provided (?, ?, ?, ?). In (?, ?), the authors provide a scalable semi-gradient based framework and curvature based bounds which improve upon the worst case polynomial factors for functions with bounded curvature $`\kappa_{f}`$ (which several submodular functions occurring in real world applications have).

Robust Submodular Optimization: Among the four problems we consider in this work, Robust-SubMax has been extensively studied in literature. One of the first papers to study robust submodular maximization was (?), where the authors study Robust-SubMax with cardinality constraints. The authors reduce this problem to a submodular set cover problem using the *saturate* trick to provide a bi-criteria approximation guarantee. (?) extend this work and study Robust-SubMax subject to matroid constraint. They provide bi-criteria algorithms by creating a union of $`O\hspace{0pt}{({\log{l/\epsilon}})}`$ independent sets, with the union set having a guarantee of $`1 - \epsilon`$. They also discuss extensions to knapsack and multiple matroid constraints and provide bicriteria approximation of $`({1 - \epsilon},{O\hspace{0pt}{({\log{l/\epsilon}})}})`$. (?) also study the same problem. However, they take a different approach by presenting a bi-criteria algorithm that outputs a feasible set that is good only for a fraction of the $`k`$ submodular functions $`g_{i}`$. (?, ?) study a slightly general problem of robust non-convex optimization (of which robust submodular optimization is a special case), but they provide weaker guarantees compared to (?, ?). We introduced and studied Robust-SubMin in the conference version of this paper (?), and Robust-scsc and Robust-scsk are new problems we study in this work.

Robust Min-Max Combinatorial Optimization: From a minimization perspective, several researchers have studied robust min-max combinatorial optimization (a special case of Robust-SubMin with modular functions) under different combinatorial constraints (see (?, ?) for a survey). Unfortunately these problems are NP hard even for constraints such as knapsack, s-t cuts, s-t paths, assignments and spanning trees where the standard linear cost problems are poly-time solvable (?, ?). Moreover, the lower bounds on hardness of approximation is $`\Omega\hspace{0pt}{({l\hspace{0pt}o\hspace{0pt}g^{1 - \epsilon}\hspace{0pt}l})}`$ ($`l`$ is the number of functions) for s-t cuts, paths and assignments (?) and $`\Omega\hspace{0pt}{({l\hspace{0pt}o\hspace{0pt}g^{1 - \epsilon}\hspace{0pt}n})}`$ for spanning trees (?) for any $`\epsilon > 0`$. For the case when $`l`$ is bounded (a constant), fully polynomial time approximation schemes have been proposed for a large class of constraints including s-t paths, knapsack, assignments and spanning trees (?, ?, ?). From an approximation algorithm perspective, the best known general result is an approximation factor of $`l`$ for constraints where the linear function can be exactly optimized in poly-time. ### 1.3 Our Contributions

While past work has mainly focused on Robust-SubMax (Problem 2), This paper is the first work to provide approximation bounds for Robust-SubMin, Robust-SCSC, and Robust-SCSK. Next, we enumerate our contribution for each of the four problems.

- •
  Robust-SubMin: We start by first providing the hardness results which follows from the hardness bounds of constrained submodular minimization and robust min-max combinatorial optimization. Next, we provide four families of algorithms for Robust-SubMin. The first and simplest approach approximates the $`\max`$ among the functions $`f_{1},\cdots,f_{l}`$ in Robust-SubMin with the average. This in turn converts Robust-SubMin into a constrained submodular minimization problem. We show that solving the constrained submodular minimization with the average of the functions still yields an approximation factor for Robust-SubMin though it is worse compared to the hardness by a factor of $`l`$. While this approach is conceptually very simple, we do not expect this perform well in practice. We next study the Majorization-Minimization (mmin) family of algorithms, where we sequentially approximate the functions $`f_{1},\cdots,f_{l}`$ with their modular upper bounds. Each resulting sub-problem involves solving a min-max combinatorial optimization problem and we use specialized solvers for the different constraints. The third algorithm is the Ellipsoidal Approximation (ea) where we replace the functions $`f_{i}`$’s with their ellipsoidal approximations. We show that this achieves the tightest approximation factor for several constraints, but is slower to the majorization-minimization approach in practice. The fourth technique is a continuous relaxation approach where we relax the discrete problem into a continuous one via the Lovász extension. We show that the resulting robust problem becomes a convex optimization problem with an appropriate recast of the constraints. We then provide a rounding scheme thereby resulting in approximation factors for Robust-SubMin. Tables 1 and 2 show the hardness and the resulting approximation factors for several important combinatorial constraints. Along the way, we also provide a family of approximation algorithms and heuristics for Robust-Min (i.e. with modular functions $`f_{i}`$ under several combinatorial constraints). We finally compare the four algorithms for different constraints and in each case, discuss heuristics which will make the resulting algorithms practical and scalable for real world problems.
- •
  Robust-SubMax: For Robust-SubMax, we complement previous work by providing an approximation guarantee for multiple knapsack constraints. Previous works have only focused on cardinality constraints (?), knapsack constraints and matroid constraints (?).
- •
  Robust-SCSC and Robust-SCSK: We show that Robust-SCSK and Robust-SCSC are closely related in that given an approximation algorithm for one of the problems, we can achieve approximation algorithms for the other. Next, we provide a framework for approximation algorithms for these problems and in turn analyze the results for special case. Significantly, our results provide the first set of approximation algorithms for robust submodular maximization under multiple knapsack constraints, robust submodular minimization under single or multiple knapsack constraints and robust submodular set cover. Next, we provide a clear picture of the hardness results for these problems.
- •
  Finally, we empirically show the performance of our algorithms on synthetic and real world datasets. We demonstrate the utility of our models in robust co-operative matching and show that our robust model outperforms simple co-operative model from (?) for this task. We also demonstrate the utility of our algorithms for Robust SCSC and Robust SCSK, specifically for robust data subset selection.

## 2 Preliminaries

In this section, we will review some of the constructs and techniques used in this paper to provide approximation algorithms for Robust-SubMin.

Curvature: Given a submodular function $`f`$, the curvature (?, ?, ?):

$`\kappa_{f} = {1 - {\min\limits_{j \in V}\frac{f\hspace{0pt}{(\left. j \middle| {V\backslash j} \right.)}}{f\hspace{0pt}{(j)}}}}`$

Thanks to submodularity, it is easy to see that $`0 \leq \kappa_{f} \leq 1`$. We define a quantity:

$`{K\hspace{0pt}{(f,\kappa)}} = \frac{f}{1 + {{({1 - \kappa})}\hspace{0pt}{({f - 1})}}}`$

where $`\kappa`$ is the curvature. Note that $`K\hspace{0pt}{(f,\kappa)}`$ interpolates between $`{K\hspace{0pt}{(f,1)}} = f`$ and $`{K\hspace{0pt}{(f,0)}} = 1`$. This quantity shall repeatedly come up in the approximation and hardness bounds in this paper.

The Submodular Polyhedron and Lovász extension For a submodular function $`f`$, the submodular polyhedron $`\mathcal{P}_{f}`$ and the corresponding base polytope $`\mathcal{B}_{f}`$ are respectively defined as

$`\mathcal{P}_{f} = {{\{ x:{{{x\hspace{0pt}{(S)}} \leq {f\hspace{0pt}{(S)}}},{{\forall S} \subseteq V}}\}}\hspace{0pt}\mathcal{B}_{f}} = {\mathcal{P}_{f} \cap {\{ x:{{x\hspace{0pt}{(V)}} = {f\hspace{0pt}{(V)}}}\}}}`$

For a vector $`x \in {\mathbb{R}}^{V}`$ and a set $`X \subseteq V`$, we write $`{x\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} = {\sum_{j \in X}{x\hspace{0pt}{(j)}}}`$. Though $`\mathcal{P}_{f}`$ is defined via $`2^{n}`$ inequalities, its extreme point can be easily characterized (?, ?). Given any permutation $`\sigma`$ of the ground set $`\{ 1,2,\cdots,n\}`$, and an associated chain $`\varnothing = S_{0}^{\sigma} \subseteq S_{1}^{\sigma} \subseteq \cdots \subseteq S_{n}^{\sigma} = V`$ with $`S_{i}^{\sigma} = {\{{\sigma\hspace{0pt}{(1)}},{\sigma\hspace{0pt}{(2)}},\ldots,{\sigma\hspace{0pt}{(i)}}\}}`$, a vector $`h_{\sigma}^{f}`$ satisfying,

$`h_{\sigma}^{f}{(\sigma{(i)} = f{(S_{i}^{\sigma})} - f{(S_{i - 1}^{\sigma})} = f{(\sigma{(i)}|S_{i - 1}^{\sigma})},\forall i = 1,\cdots,n}`$

forms an extreme point of $`\mathcal{P}_{f}`$. Moreover, a natural convex extension of a submodular function, called the Lovász extension (?, ?) is closely related to the submodular polyhedron, and is defined as $`{\hat{f}\hspace{0pt}{(x)}} = {\max_{h \in \mathcal{P}_{f}}{\langle h,x\rangle}}`$. Thanks to the properties of the polyhedron, $`\hat{f}\hspace{0pt}{(x)}`$ can be efficiently computed: Denote $`\sigma_{x}`$ as an ordering induced by $`x`$, such that $`{x\hspace{0pt}{({\sigma_{x}\hspace{0pt}{(1)}})}} \geq {x\hspace{0pt}{({\sigma_{x}\hspace{0pt}{(2)}})}} \geq {\cdots\hspace{0pt}x\hspace{0pt}{({\sigma_{x}\hspace{0pt}{(n)}})}}`$. Then the Lovász extension is $`{\hat{f}\hspace{0pt}{(x)}} = {\langle h_{\sigma_{x}}^{f},x\rangle}`$ (?, ?). The gradient of the Lovász extension $`{{\nabla\hat{f}}\hspace{0pt}{(x)}} = h_{\sigma_{x}}^{f}`$.

Modular lower bounds (Sub-gradients): Akin to convex functions, submodular functions have tight modular lower bounds. These bounds are related to the sub-differential $`\partial_{f}{(Y)}`$ of the submodular set function $`f`$ at a set $`Y \subseteq V`$, which is defined (?, ?, ?) as:

$`{\partial_{f}{(Y)}} = {\{{y \in {\mathbb{R}}^{n}}:{{{{f\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} - {y\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}} \geq {{f\hspace{0pt}{(Y)}} - {y\hspace{0pt}{(Y)}}}},{{\text{for all~}\hspace{0pt}X} \subseteq V}}\}}`$

Denote a sub-gradient at $`Y`$ by $`h_{Y} \in {\partial_{f}{(Y)}}`$. Define $`h_{Y} = h_{\sigma_{Y}}^{f}`$ (see the definition of $`h_{\sigma}^{f}`$ from the previous paragraph) forms a lower bound of $`f`$, tight at $`Y`$ — i.e., $`{{h_{Y}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} = {\sum_{j \in X}{h_{Y}\hspace{0pt}{(j)}}} \leq {f\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}},{{\forall X} \subseteq V}`$ and $`{h_{Y}\hspace{0pt}{(Y)}} = {f\hspace{0pt}{(Y)}}`$. Notice that the extreme points of a sub-differential are a subset of the extreme points of the submodular polyhedron.

Modular upper bounds (Super-gradients): We can also define super-differentials $`\partial^{f}{(Y)}`$ of a submodular function (?, ?, ?, ?) at $`Y`$:

$`{\partial^{f}{(Y)}} = {\{{y \in {\mathbb{R}}^{n}}:{{{{f\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} - {y\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}} \leq {{f\hspace{0pt}{(Y)}} - {y\hspace{0pt}{(Y)}}}};{{\text{for all~}\hspace{0pt}X} \subseteq V}}\}}`$

It is possible, moreover, to provide specific super-gradients (?, ?, ?, ?, ?) that define the following two modular upper bounds:

$`{{m_{X,1}^{f}\hspace{0pt}{(Y)}} \triangleq {{{f\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} - {\sum\limits_{j \in {X\backslash Y}}{f\hspace{0pt}{(\left. j \middle| {X\backslash j} \right.)}}}} + {\sum\limits_{j \in {Y\backslash X}}{f\hspace{0pt}{(\left. j \middle| \varnothing \right.)}}}}},`$$`{{m_{X,2}^{f}\hspace{0pt}{(Y)}} \triangleq {{{f\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} - {\sum\limits_{j \in {X\backslash Y}}{f\hspace{0pt}{(\left. j \middle| {V\backslash j} \right.)}}}} + {\sum\limits_{j \in {Y\backslash X}}{f\hspace{0pt}{(\left. j \middle| X \right.)}}}}}.`$

Then $`{m_{X,1}^{f}\hspace{0pt}{(Y)}} \geq {f\hspace{0pt}{(Y)}}`$ and $`{{m_{X,2}^{f}\hspace{0pt}{(Y)}} \geq {f\hspace{0pt}{(Y)}}},{{\forall Y} \subseteq V}`$ and $`{m_{X,1}^{f}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} = {m_{X,2}^{f}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} = {f\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}`$. Also note that $`{m_{\varnothing,1}^{f}\hspace{0pt}{(Y)}} = {m_{\varnothing,2}^{f}\hspace{0pt}{(Y)}} = {\sum_{j \in Y}{f\hspace{0pt}{(\left. j \middle| \varnothing \right.)}}}`$. For simplicity denote this as $`m_{\varnothing}^{f}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}`$. Then the following result holds:

###### Lemma 1.

The main result states that any polymatroid (monotone submodular) function $`f`$, can be approximated by a function of the form $`\sqrt{w^{f}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}`$ for a certain modular weight vector $`w^{f} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{V}`$, such that $`{\sqrt{w^{f}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \leq {f\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \leq {O\hspace{0pt}{({\sqrt{n}\hspace{0pt}{\log n}})}\hspace{0pt}\sqrt{w^{f}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}}},{{\forall X} \subseteq V}`$. ## 3 Robust-SubMin

In this section, we shall go over the hardness and approximation algorithms for Robust-SubMin. Recall that the Robust-SubMin optimization problem is:

$`\text{Problem 1:~}\hspace{0pt}{\min\limits_{X \in \mathcal{C}}{\max\limits_{{i = 1}:l}f_{i}}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}`$

We shall consider two cases, one where $`l`$ is bounded (i.e. its a constant), and the other where $`l`$ is unbounded. We start with some notation. We denote the graph as $`G = {(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})}`$ with $`{{|\mathcal{V}|} = n},{{|\mathcal{E}|} = m}`$. Depending on the problem at hand, the ground set $`V`$ can either be the set of edges ($`V = \mathcal{E}`$) or the set of vertices ($`V = \mathcal{V}`$). The groundset is the set of edges in the case of trees, matchings, cuts, paths and edge covers, while in the case of vertex covers, they are defined on the vertices. $`\mathcal{C}`$ is the combinatorial constraints which enforces that the set of edges/vertices is either an s-t cut, s-t path, vertex cover, edge cover, spanning tree, or matching etc. Tables 1 and 2 show the hardness and the approximation factors for several important combinatorial constraints and settings.

### 3.1 Hardness of Robust-SubMin

Since Robust-SubMin generalizes robust min-max combinatorial optimization (when the functions are modular), we have the hardness bounds from (?, ?). For the modular case, the lower bounds are $`\Omega\hspace{0pt}{({l\hspace{0pt}o\hspace{0pt}g^{1 - \epsilon}\hspace{0pt}l})}`$ ($`l`$ is the number of functions) for s-t cuts, paths and assignments (?) and $`\Omega\hspace{0pt}{({l\hspace{0pt}o\hspace{0pt}g^{1 - \epsilon}\hspace{0pt}n})}`$ for spanning trees (?) for any $`\epsilon > 0`$. These hold unless $`{N\hspace{0pt}P} \subseteq {D\hspace{0pt}T\hspace{0pt}I\hspace{0pt}M\hspace{0pt}E\hspace{0pt}{(n^{p\hspace{0pt}o\hspace{0pt}l\hspace{0pt}y\hspace{0pt}{\log n}})}}`$(?, ?). Moreover, since Robust-SubMin also generalizes constrained submodular minimization, we get the curvature based hardness bounds from (?, ?, ?). The hardness results are in the first column of Table 1. The curvature $`\kappa`$ corresponds to the worst curvature among the functions $`f_{i}`$ (i.e. $`\kappa = {\max_{i}\kappa_{i}}`$).

### 3.2 Algorithms with Modular functions $`f_{i}`$’s

In this section, we shall study approximation algorithms for Robust-SubMin when the functions $`f_{i}`$’s are modular, i.e. $`{f_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} = {\sum_{j \in X}{f_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(j)}}}`$. We call this problem Robust-Min. Most previous work (?, ?, ?) has focused on fully polynomial approximation schemes when $`l`$ is small. These algorithms are exponential in $`l`$ and do not scale beyond $`l = 3`$ or $`4`$. Instead, we shall study approximation algorithms for this problem. Define two simple approximations of the function $`{F\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} = {{\max_{i}f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}`$ when $`f_{i}`$’s are modular. The first is $`{\hat{F}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} = {\sum_{i \in X}{{\max_{{j = 1}:l}f_{j}}\hspace{0pt}{(i)}}}`$ and the second is $`{\overset{\sim}{F}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} = {{1/l}\hspace{0pt}{\sum_{i = 1}^{l}{\sum_{j \in X}{f_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(j)}}}}} = {{1/l}\hspace{0pt}{\sum_{i = 1}^{l}{f_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}}}`$.

###### Lemma 3.

Given $`{f_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(j)}} \geq 0`$, it holds that $`{\hat{F}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \geq {F\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \geq {\frac{1}{l}\hspace{0pt}\hat{F}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}`$. Furthermore, $`{\overset{\sim}{F}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \leq {F\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \leq {l\hspace{0pt}\overset{\sim}{F}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}`$. Given a $`\beta`$-approximate algorithm for optimizing linear cost functions over the constraint $`\mathcal{C}`$, we can achieve an $`l\hspace{0pt}\beta`$-approximation algorithm for Robust-Min.

###### Proof.

We first prove the second part. Its easy to see that $`{F\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} = {{\max_{i}f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \leq {\sum_{i}{f_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}} = {l\hspace{0pt}\overset{\sim}{F}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}`$ which is the second inequality. The first inequality follows from the fact that $`{\sum_{i}{f_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}} \leq {l\hspace{0pt}{\max_{i}f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}`$. To prove the first result, we start with proving $`{F\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \leq {\hat{F}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}`$. For a given set $`X`$, let $`i_{X}`$ be the index which maximizes $`F`$ so $`{F\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} = {\sum_{j \in X}{f_{i_{X}}\hspace{0pt}{(j)}}}`$. Then $`{f_{i_{X}}\hspace{0pt}{(j)}} \leq {{\max_{i}f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(j)}}`$ from which we get the result. Next, observe that $`{\hat{F}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \leq {\sum_{i = 1}^{l}{f_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}} = {l\hspace{0pt}\overset{\sim}{F}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \leq {l\hspace{0pt}F\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}`$ which follows from the inequality corresponding to $`\overset{\sim}{F}`$.

Now given a $`\beta`$-approximation algorithm for optimizing linear cost functions over the constraint $`\mathcal{C}`$, denote $`\overset{\sim}{X}`$ by optimizing $`\overset{\sim}{F}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}`$ over $`\mathcal{C}`$. Also denote $`X^{\ast}`$ as the optimal solution by optimizing $`F`$ over $`\mathcal{C}`$ and $`\overset{\sim}{X^{\ast}}`$ be the optimal solution for optimizing $`\overset{\sim}{F}`$ over $`\mathcal{C}`$. Since $`\overset{\sim}{X}`$ is a $`\beta`$ approximation for $`\overset{\sim}{F}`$ over $`\mathcal{C}`$, $`{\overset{\sim}{F}\hspace{0pt}{(\overset{\sim}{X})}} \leq {\beta\hspace{0pt}\overset{\sim}{F}\hspace{0pt}{(\overset{\sim}{X^{\ast}})}} \leq {\beta\hspace{0pt}\overset{\sim}{F}\hspace{0pt}{(X^{\ast})}}`$. The last inequality holds since $`X^{\ast}`$ is feasible (i.e. it belongs to $`\mathcal{C}`$) so it must hold that $`{\overset{\sim}{F}\hspace{0pt}{(X^{\ast})}} \geq {\overset{\sim}{F}\hspace{0pt}{(\overset{\sim}{X^{\ast}})}}`$. A symmetric argument applies to $`\hat{F}`$. In particular, $`{F\hspace{0pt}{(\hat{X})}} \leq {\hat{F}\hspace{0pt}{(\hat{X})}} \leq {\beta\hspace{0pt}\hat{F}\hspace{0pt}{(X^{\ast})}} \leq {\beta\hspace{0pt}l\hspace{0pt}F\hspace{0pt}{(X^{\ast})}}`$. The inequality $`{\hat{F}\hspace{0pt}{(\hat{X})}} \leq {\beta\hspace{0pt}\hat{F}\hspace{0pt}{(X^{\ast})}}`$ again holds due to an argument similar to the $`\overset{\sim}{F}`$ case. ∎

Note that for most constraints $`\mathcal{C}`$, $`\beta`$ is $`1`$. In practice, we can take the better of the two solutions obtained from the two approximations above.

Next, we shall look at higher order approximations of $`F`$. Define the function $`{F_{a}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} = {({\sum_{i = 1}^{l}{\lbrack{f_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}\rbrack}^{a}})}^{\frac{1}{a}}`$. Its easy to see that $`F_{a}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}`$ comes close to $`F`$ as $`a`$ becomes large.

###### Lemma 4.

Define $`{F_{a}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} = {({\sum_{i = 1}^{l}{\lbrack{f_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}\rbrack}^{a}})}^{\frac{1}{a}}`$. Then it holds that $`{F\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \leq {F_{a}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \leq {l^{\frac{1}{a}}\hspace{0pt}F\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}`$. Moreover, given a $`\beta`$-approximation algorithm for optimizing $`F_{a}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}`$ over $`\mathcal{C}`$, we can obtain a $`\beta\hspace{0pt}l^{\frac{1}{a}}`$-approximation for Robust-Min.

###### Proof.

Its easy to see that $`{F\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \leq {F_{a}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}`$ since $`{\lbrack{F\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}\rbrack}^{a} \leq {\sum_{i = 1}^{l}{\lbrack{f_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}\rbrack}^{a}}`$. Next, note that $`{{\forall i},{f_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}} \leq {F\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}`$ and hence $`{\sum_{i = 1}^{l}{\lbrack{f_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}\rbrack}^{a}} \leq {l\hspace{0pt}{\lbrack{F\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}\rbrack}^{a}}`$ which implies that $`{F_{a}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \leq {k^{\frac{1}{a}}\hspace{0pt}F\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}`$ which proves the result. The second part follows from arguments similar to the Proof of Lemma 2. ∎

Note that optimizing $`F_{a}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}`$ is equivalent to optimizing $`\sum_{i = 1}^{l}{\lbrack{f_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}\rbrack}^{a}`$ which is a higher order polynomial function. For example, when $`a = 2`$ we get the quadratic combinatorial program (?) which can possibly result in a a $`\sqrt{l}`$ approximation given a exact or approximate quadratic optimizer. Unfortunately, optimizing this problem is NP hard for most constraints when $`a \geq 2`$. However, several heuristics and efficient solvers (?, ?, ?, ?) exist for solving this in the quadratic setting. Moreover, for special cases such as the assignment (matching) problem, specialized algorithms such as the Graduated Assignment algorithm (?) exist for solving this. While these are not guaranteed to theoretically achieve the optimal solution, they work quite in practice, thus yielding a family of heuristics for Robust-Min.

### 3.3 Average Approximation based Algorithms

In this section, we shall look at a simple approximation of $`{\max_{i}f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}`$, which in turn shall lead to an approximation algorithm for Robust-SubMin. We first show that $`{f_{a\hspace{0pt}v\hspace{0pt}g}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} = {{1/l}\hspace{0pt}{\sum_{i = 1}^{l}{f_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}}}`$ is an $`l`$-approximation of $`{\max_{{i = 1}:l}f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}`$, which implies that minimizing $`f_{a\hspace{0pt}v\hspace{0pt}g}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}`$ implies an approximation for Robust-SubMin. The results in this Section are summarized in Table 1 (specifically, the mmin-aa and ea-aa columns of Table 1).

###### Theorem 5.

Given a non-negative set function $`f`$, define $`{f_{a\hspace{0pt}v\hspace{0pt}g}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} = {\frac{1}{l}\hspace{0pt}{\sum_{i = 1}^{l}{f_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}}}`$. Then $`{f_{a\hspace{0pt}v\hspace{0pt}g}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \leq {{\max_{{i = 1}:l}f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \leq {l\hspace{0pt}f_{a\hspace{0pt}v\hspace{0pt}g}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}`$. Denote $`\hat{X}`$ as $`\beta`$-approximate optimizer of $`f_{a\hspace{0pt}v\hspace{0pt}g}`$. Then $`{{\max_{{i = 1}:l}f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(\hat{X})}} \leq {l\hspace{0pt}\beta\hspace{0pt}{\max_{{i = 1}:l}f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(X^{\ast})}}`$ where $`X^{\ast}`$ is the exact minimizer of Robust-SubMin.

###### Proof.

To prove the first part, notice that $`{f_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \leq {{\max_{{i = 1}:l}f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}`$, and hence $`{{1/l}\hspace{0pt}{\sum_{i}{f_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}}} \leq {{\max_{{i = 1}:l}f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}`$. The other inequality also directly follows since the $`f_{i}`$’s are non-negative and hence $`{{\max_{{i = 1}:l}f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \leq {\sum_{i}{f_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}} = {l\hspace{0pt}f_{a\hspace{0pt}v\hspace{0pt}g}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}`$. To prove the second part, observe that $`{{\max_{i}f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(\hat{X})}} \leq {l\hspace{0pt}f_{a\hspace{0pt}v\hspace{0pt}g}\hspace{0pt}{(\hat{X})}} \leq {l\hspace{0pt}\beta\hspace{0pt}f_{a\hspace{0pt}v\hspace{0pt}g}\hspace{0pt}{(X^{\ast})}} \leq {l\hspace{0pt}\beta\hspace{0pt}{\max_{i}f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(X^{\ast})}}`$. The first inequality holds from the first part of this result, the second inequality holds since $`\hat{X}`$ is a $`\beta`$-approximate optimizer of $`f_{a\hspace{0pt}v\hspace{0pt}g}`$ and the third part of the theorem holds again from the first part of this result. ∎

Since $`f_{a\hspace{0pt}v\hspace{0pt}g}`$ is a submodular function, we can use the majorization-minimization (which we call mmin-aa) and ellipsoidal approximation (ea-aa) for constrained submodular minimization (?, ?, ?, ?). The following corollary provides the approximation guarantee of mmin-aa and ea-aa for Robust-SubMin.

###### Corollary 6.

### 3.4 Majorization-Minimization Algorithm

The Majorization-Minimization algorithm is a sequential procedure which uses upper bounds of the submodular functions defined via supergradients. Starting with $`X^{0} = \varnothing`$, the algorithm proceeds as follows. At iteration $`t`$, it constructs modular upper bounds for each function $`f_{i}`$, $`m_{X^{t}}^{f_{i}}`$ which is tight at $`X^{t}`$. We can use either one of the two modular upper bounds defined in Section 2. The set $`X^{t + 1} = {\text{argmin}_{X \in \mathcal{C}}\hspace{0pt}{\max_{i}m_{X^{t}}^{f_{i}}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}`$. This is a min-max robust optimization problem. The following theorem provides the approximation guarantee for mmin.

###### Theorem 7.

If $`l`$ is a constant, mmin achieves an approximation guarantee of $`{({1 + \epsilon})}\hspace{0pt}K\hspace{0pt}{({|X^{\ast}|},\kappa_{w\hspace{0pt}c})}`$ for the knapsack, spanning trees, matching and s-t path problems. The complexity of this algorithm is exponential in $`l`$. When $`l`$ is unbounded, mmin achieves an approximation guarantee of $`l\hspace{0pt}K\hspace{0pt}{({|X^{\ast}|},\kappa_{w\hspace{0pt}c})}`$. For spanning trees and shortest path constraints, mmin achieves a\
$`O\hspace{0pt}{({{\min{({\log n},l)}}\hspace{0pt}K\hspace{0pt}{(n,\kappa_{w\hspace{0pt}c})}})}`$ and a $`O\hspace{0pt}{({{\min{(\sqrt{n},l)}}\hspace{0pt}K\hspace{0pt}{(n,\kappa_{w\hspace{0pt}c})}})}`$ approximation. Under cardinality and partition matroid constraints, mmin achieves a $`O\hspace{0pt}{({{{\log{l\hspace{0pt}K}}\hspace{0pt}{(n,\kappa_{w\hspace{0pt}c})}}/{\log{\log l}}})}`$ approximation.

Substituting the appropriate bounds on $`|X^{\ast}|`$ for the various constraints, we get the results in Tables 1 and 2. $`\kappa_{w\hspace{0pt}c}`$ corresponds to the worst case curvature $`\max_{i}\kappa_{f_{i}}`$.

$`{X^{t + 1} = {\text{argmin}_{X \in \mathcal{C}}\hspace{0pt}{\max\limits_{i}m_{X^{t}}^{f_{i}}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}}.`$

### 3.5 Ellipsoidal Approximation Based Algorithm

Next, we use the Ellipsoidal Approximation to approximate the submodular function $`f_{i}`$. To account for the curvature of the individual functions $`f_{i}`$’s, we use the curve-normalized Ellipsoidal Approximation (?). We then obtain the functions $`{\hat{f}}_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}`$ which are of the form $`{{({1 - \kappa_{f_{i}}})}\hspace{0pt}\sqrt{w_{f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}} + {\kappa_{f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{\sum_{j \in X}{f_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(j)}}}}`$, and the problem is then to optimize $`{\max_{i}{\hat{f}}_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}`$ subject to the constraints $`\mathcal{C}`$. This is no longer a min-max optimization problem. The following result shows that we can still achieve approximation guarantees in this case.

###### Theorem 8.

For the case when $`l`$ is a constant, ea achieves an approximation guarantee of\
$`O\hspace{0pt}{({K\hspace{0pt}{(\sqrt{{|\mathcal{V}|}\hspace{0pt}{\log{|\mathcal{V}|}}},\kappa_{w\hspace{0pt}c})}})}`$ for the knapsack, spanning trees, matching and s-t path problems. The complexity of this algorithm is exponential in $`l`$. When $`l`$ is unbounded, the ea algorithm achieves an approximation guarantee of $`O\hspace{0pt}{({\sqrt{l}\hspace{0pt}\sqrt{|\mathcal{V}|}\hspace{0pt}{\log{|\mathcal{V}|}}})}`$ for all constraints. In the case of spanning trees, shortest paths, the ea achieves approximation factors of $`O\hspace{0pt}{({{\min{(\sqrt{\log n},\sqrt{l})}}\hspace{0pt}\sqrt{m}\hspace{0pt}{\log m}})}`$, and $`O\hspace{0pt}{({{\min{(n^{0.25},\sqrt{l})}}\hspace{0pt}\sqrt{m}\hspace{0pt}{\log m}})}`$. Under cardinality and partition matroid constraints, ea achieves a $`O\hspace{0pt}{({\sqrt{\log{l/{\log{\log l}}}}\hspace{0pt}\sqrt{n}\hspace{0pt}{\log n}})}`$ approximation.

For the case when $`l`$ is bounded, we reduce the optimization problem after the Ellipsoidal Approximation into a multi-objective optimization problem, which provides an FPTAS for knapsack, spanning trees, matching and s-t path problems (?, ?). When $`l`$ is unbounded, we further reduce the ea approximation objective into a linear objective which then provides the approximation guarantees similar to mmin above. However, as a result, we loose the curvature based guarantee.

###### Proof.

First we start with the case when $`l`$ is a constant. Observe that the optimization problem is

$`{{\min\limits_{X \in \mathcal{C}}{\max\limits_{i}{\hat{f}}_{i}}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} = {{\min\limits_{X \in \mathcal{C}}{{\max\limits_{i}{({1 - \kappa_{f_{i}}})}}\hspace{0pt}\sqrt{w_{f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}}} +}`$$`\kappa_{f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{\sum\limits_{j \in X}{f_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(j)}}}`$

This is of the form $`{\min_{X \in \mathcal{C}}{\max_{i}\sqrt{w_{1}^{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}}} + {w_{2}^{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}`$. Define:

$`{h\hspace{0pt}{(y_{1}^{1},y_{2}^{1},y_{2}^{2},y_{2}^{2},\cdots,y_{1}^{l},y_{2}^{l})}} = {{\max\limits_{i}\sqrt{y_{1}^{i}}} + y_{2}^{i}}`$

Note that the optimization problem is $`{\min_{X \in \mathcal{C}}h}\hspace{0pt}{({w_{1}^{1}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}},{w_{2}^{1}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}},\cdots,{w_{1}^{l}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}},{w_{2}^{l}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}})}`$. Observe that $`{h\hspace{0pt}{(\mathbf{y})}} \leq {h\hspace{0pt}{(\mathbf{y}')}}`$ if $`\mathbf{y} \leq \mathbf{y}'`$. Furthermore, note that $`\mathbf{y} \geq 0`$. Then given a $`\lambda > 1`$, $`{h\hspace{0pt}{({\lambda\hspace{0pt}\mathbf{y}})}} = {{\max_{i}\sqrt{\lambda\hspace{0pt}y_{1}^{i}}} + {\lambda\hspace{0pt}y_{2}^{i}}} \leq {{\lambda\hspace{0pt}\sqrt{y_{1}^{i}}} + {\lambda\hspace{0pt}y_{2}^{i}}} \leq {\lambda\hspace{0pt}h\hspace{0pt}{(\mathbf{y})}}`$. As a result, we can use Theorem 3.3 from (?) which provides an FPTAS as long as the following exact problem can be solved on $`\mathcal{C}`$: Given a constant $`C`$ and a vector $`c \in \mathbf{R}^{n}`$, does there exist a $`x`$ such that $`{\langle c,x\rangle} = C`$? A number of constraints including matchings, knapsacks, s-t paths and spanning trees satisfy this (?). For these constraints, we can obtain a $`1 + \epsilon`$ approximation algorithm in complexity exponential in $`l`$.

When $`l`$ is unbounded, we directly use the Ellipsoidal Approximation and the problem then is to optimize $`\min_{X \in \mathcal{C}}{\max_{i}\sqrt{w_{f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}}`$. We then transform this to the following optimization problem:\
$`{\min_{X \in \mathcal{C}}{\max_{i}w_{f_{i}}}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}`$. Assume we can obtain an $`\alpha`$ approximation to the problem\
$`{\min_{X \in \mathcal{C}}{\max_{i}w_{f_{i}}}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}`$. This means we can achieve a solution $`\hat{X}`$ such that:

$`{{\max\limits_{i}w_{f_{i}}}\hspace{0pt}{(\hat{X})}} \leq {\alpha\hspace{0pt}{\max\limits_{i}w_{f_{i}}}\hspace{0pt}{(X^{e\hspace{0pt}a})}}`$

where $`X^{e\hspace{0pt}a}`$ is the optimal solution for the problem $`{\min_{X \in \mathcal{C}}{\max_{i}w_{f_{i}}}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}`$. Then observe that $`{\max_{i}\sqrt{w_{f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(X^{e\hspace{0pt}a})}}} \leq {\max_{i}\sqrt{w_{f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(X^{\ast})}}} \leq {{\max_{i}f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(X^{\ast})}}`$. Combining all the inequalities and also using the bound of the Ellipsoidal Approximation, we have $`{{\max_{i}f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(\hat{X})}} \leq {\beta\hspace{0pt}{\max_{i}\sqrt{w_{f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(\hat{X})}}}} \leq {\beta\hspace{0pt}\sqrt{\alpha}\hspace{0pt}{\max_{i}\sqrt{w_{f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(X^{e\hspace{0pt}a})}}}} \leq {\beta\hspace{0pt}\sqrt{\alpha}\hspace{0pt}{\max_{i}\sqrt{w_{f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(X^{\ast})}}}} \leq {\beta\hspace{0pt}\sqrt{\alpha}\hspace{0pt}{\max_{i}f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(X^{\ast})}}`$ where $`\beta`$ is the approximation of the Ellipsoidal Approximation.

We now use this result to prove the theorem. Consider two cases. First, we optimize the *avg* and *max* versions of $`{\max_{i}w_{f_{i}}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}`$ which provide $`\alpha = l`$ approximation. Secondly, for the special cases of spanning trees, shortest paths, cardinality and partition matroid constraints, there exist LP relaxation based algorithms which achieve approximation factors $`\alpha`$ being $`O\hspace{0pt}{({\log n})}`$, $`O\hspace{0pt}{(\sqrt{n\hspace{0pt}{\log{l/{\log{\log l}}}}})}`$, $`O\hspace{0pt}{({\log{l/{\log{\log l}}}})}`$ and $`O\hspace{0pt}{({\log{l/{\log{\log l}}}})}`$ respectively. Substitute these values of $`\alpha`$ and using the fact that $`\beta = {O\hspace{0pt}{({\sqrt{|V|}\hspace{0pt}{\log{|V|}}})}}`$, we get the approximation bound. ∎

### 3.6 Continuous Relaxation Algorithm

Here, we use the continuous relaxation of a submodular function. In particular, we use the relaxation $`{{{\max_{i}\hat{f_{i}}}\hspace{0pt}{(x)}},x} \in {\lbrack 0,1\rbrack}^{|\mathcal{V}|}`$ as the continuous relaxation of the original function $`{\max_{i}f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}`$ (here $`\hat{f}`$ is the Lovász extension). Its easy to see that this is a continuous relaxation. Since the Lovász extension is convex, the function $`{\max_{i}\hat{f_{i}}}\hspace{0pt}{(x)}`$ is also a convex function. This means that we can exactly optimize the continuous relaxation over a convex polytope. The remaining question is about the rounding and the resulting approximation guarantee due to the rounding. Given a constraint $`\mathcal{C}`$, define the up-monotone polytope similar to (?) $`\hat{\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}}} = {\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}} + {\lbrack 0,1\rbrack}^{|\mathcal{V}|}}`$. We then use the observation from (?) that all the constraints considered in Table 1 can be expressed as:

$`{\hat{\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}}} = \left. \{{x \in {\lbrack 0,1\rbrack}^{n}} \middle| {{\sum\limits_{i \in W}x_{i}} \geq {b_{W}\hspace{0pt}{\text{, for all~}{W \in \mathcal{W}}}}}\} \right.}.`$

We then round the solution using the following rounding scheme. Given a continuous vector $`\hat{x}`$ (which is the optimizer of $`{{{\max_{i}\hat{f_{i}}}\hspace{0pt}{(x)}},x} \in \hat{\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}}}`$, order the elements based on $`\sigma_{\hat{x}}`$. Denote $`X_{i} = {\lbrack{\sigma_{\hat{x}}\hspace{0pt}{\lbrack 1\rbrack}},\cdots,{\sigma_{\hat{x}}\hspace{0pt}{\lbrack i\rbrack}}\rbrack}`$ so we obtain a chain of sets $`\varnothing \subseteq X_{1} \subseteq X_{2} \subseteq \cdots \subset X_{n}`$. Our rounding scheme picks the smallest $`k`$ such that $`X_{k} \in \hat{\mathcal{C}}`$. Another way of checking this is if there exists a set $`X \subset X_{k}`$ such that $`X \in \mathcal{C}`$. Since $`\hat{\mathcal{C}}`$ is up-monotone, such a set must exist. The following result shows the approximation guarantee.

###### Theorem 9.

Given submodular functions $`f_{i}`$ and constraints $`\mathcal{C}`$ which can be expressed as $`\{ x \in {\lbrack 0,1\rbrack}^{n}|\sum_{i \in W}x_{i} \geq b_{W}`$ for all $`W \in \mathcal{W}`$} for a family of sets $`\mathcal{W} = {\{ W_{1},\cdots\}}`$, the continuous relaxation scheme achieves an approximation guarantee of $`{{\max_{W \in \mathcal{W}}{|W|}} - b_{W}} + 1`$. If we assume the sets in $`\mathcal{W}`$ are disjoint, the integrality bounds matches the approximation bounds.

###### Proof.

The proof of this theorem is closely in line with Lemma 2 and Theorem 1 from (?). We first show the first part. Given monotone submodular functions $`{{f_{i},i} \in 1},{\cdots,l}`$, and an optimizer $`\hat{x}`$ of $`{\max_{i}\hat{f}}\hspace{0pt}{(x)}`$, define $`\hat{X_{\theta}} = {\{ i:{{\hat{x}}_{i} \geq \theta}\}}`$. We choose $`\theta`$ such that $`\hat{X_{\theta}} \in \mathcal{C}`$. Then $`{{\max_{i}f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(\hat{X_{\theta}})}} \leq {{1/\theta}\hspace{0pt}{\max_{i}f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(X^{\ast})}}`$ where $`X^{\ast}`$ is the optimizer of $`{\min_{X \in \mathcal{C}}{\max_{i}f_{i}}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}`$. To prove this, observe that, by definition $`{\theta\hspace{0pt}1_{\hat{X_{\theta}}}} \leq \hat{x}`$²²2$`1_{A}`$ is the indicator vector of set $`A`$ such that $`{1_{A}\hspace{0pt}{\lbrack i\rbrack}} = 1`$ if and only if $`i \in A`$.. As a result, $`{{\forall i},{\hat{f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{({\theta\hspace{0pt}1_{\hat{X_{\theta}}}})}}} = {\theta\hspace{0pt}f_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(\hat{X_{\theta}})}} \leq {\hat{f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(\hat{x})}}`$ – this follows because of the positive homogeneity of the Lovász extension. This implies that $`{\theta\hspace{0pt}{\max_{i}f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(\hat{X_{\theta}})}} \leq {\hat{f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(\hat{x})}} \leq {{\min_{x \in \mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}}}\hat{f_{i}}}\hspace{0pt}{(\hat{x})}} \leq {{\min_{X \in \mathcal{C}}{\max_{i}f_{i}}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}`$. The last inequality holds from the fact that the discrete solution is greater than the continuous one since the continuous one is a relaxation. This proves this part of the theorem.

Next, we show that the approximation guarantee holds for the class of constraints defined as $`\left. \{{x \in {\lbrack 0,1\rbrack}^{n}} \middle| {{\sum_{i \in W}x_{i}} \geq b_{W}}\} \right.`$. From these constraints, note that for every $`W \in \mathcal{W}`$, at least $`b_{W} \leq {|W|}`$ elements need to “covered”. Consequently, to round a vector $`x \in \hat{\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}}}`$, it is sufficient to choose $`\theta = {\min_{W \in \mathcal{W}}x_{\lbrack b_{W},W\rbrack}}`$ as the rounding threshold, where $`x_{\lbrack k,A\rbrack}`$ denotes the $`k^{\text{th}}`$ largest entry of $`x`$ in a set $`A`$. The worst case scenario is that the $`b_{W} - 1`$ entries of $`x`$ with indices in the set $`W`$ are all $`1`$, and the remaining mass of $`1`$ is equally distributed over the remaining elements in $`W`$. In this case, the value of $`x_{\lbrack b_{W},W\rbrack}`$ is $`1/{({{{|W|} - b_{W}} + 1})}`$. Since the constraint requires $`{\sum_{i \in W}x_{i}} \geq b_{W}`$, it must hold that $`x_{\lbrack b_{W},W\rbrack} \geq {1/{({{{|W|} - b_{W}} + 1})}}`$. Combining this with the first part of the result proves this theorem. ∎

We can then obtain the approximation guarantees for different constraints including cardinality, spanning trees, matroids, set covers, edge covers and vertex covers, matchings, cuts and paths by appropriately defining the polytopes $`\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}}`$ and appropriately setting the values of $`\mathcal{W}`$ and $`{{\max_{W \in \mathcal{W}}{|W|}} - b_{W}} + 1`$. ### 3.7 Analysis of the Bounds for Various Constraints

Given the bounds in Tables 1 and 2, we discuss the tightness of these bounds viz-a-via the hardness. In the case when $`l`$ is a constant, mmin achieves tight bounds for Trees, Matchings and Paths while the ea achieves tight bounds up to $`\log`$ factors for knapsack constraints. In the case when $`l`$ is not a constant, mmin achieves a tight bound up to $`\log`$ factors for spanning tree constraints. In the case when the functions $`f_{i}`$ have curvature $`\kappa = 1`$, CR also obtains tight bounds for edge-covers and matchings. We also point out that the bounds of average approximation (AA) depend on the average case curvature as opposed to the worst case curvature. However, in practice, the functions $`f_{i}`$ often belong to the same class of functions in which case all the functions $`f_{i}`$ have the same curvature.

## 4 Robust-SubMax

In this section, we will study Robust-SubMax. Recall that the Robust-SubMax optimization problem is:

$`\text{Problem 2:~}\hspace{0pt}{\max\limits_{X \in \mathcal{C}}{\min\limits_{{i = 1}:k}g_{i}}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}`$

(?) show that Robust-SubMax is inapproximable (in a single criteria manner) upto any polynomial factor unless P = NP even with cardinality constraints. This is in contrast to Robust-SubMin, which achieves bounded approximation factors (Section 3). (?) provide a bi-criteria approximation factor for Robust-SubMax under a single cardinality constraint. (?) extend this to matroid and knapsack constraints. (?, ?) provide a bi-criteria approximation factor of $`({1 - \epsilon},{O\hspace{0pt}{({\log{k/\epsilon}})}})`$ for cardinality constraints. Anari et al (?) also obtained a $`({1 - \epsilon},{O\hspace{0pt}{({\log{k/\epsilon}})}})`$ approximation for a single knapsack constraint. In the following theorem, we complete the picture of Robust-SubMax by providing a bi-criteria approximation for Robust-SubMax under multiple knapsack constraints. In particular, we consider the following problem:

$`{{{\max\limits_{X \subseteq V}{\min\limits_{{i = 1}:k}g_{i}}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}},{\text{~s.t~}\hspace{0pt}w_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}} \leq b_{i}`$

Here $`w_{i}`$ are modular functions.

### 5.2 Hardness of Robust-scsc and Robust-scsk

Next we discuss the hardness of Robust-scsc and Robust-scsk. We shall assume the functions $`f_{i}`$’s and $`g_{i}`$’s belong to the same family and hence have the same curvature. The first is $`{\hat{F}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} = {\sum_{i \in X}{{\max_{{j = 1}:l}f_{j}}\hspace{0pt}{(i)}}}`$ and the second is $`{\overset{\sim}{F}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} = {{1/l}\hspace{0pt}{\sum_{i = 1}^{l}{\sum_{j \in X}{f_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(j)}}}}} = {\sum_{i = 1}^{l}{f_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}}`$.

###### Lemma 14.

Given $`{f_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(j)}} \geq 0`$, it holds that $`{\hat{F}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \geq {F\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \geq {\frac{1}{l}\hspace{0pt}\hat{F}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}`$. Furthermore, $`{\overset{\sim}{F}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \leq {F\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \leq {l\hspace{0pt}\overset{\sim}{F}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}`$.

###### Proof.

We first prove the second part. Its easy to see that $`{F\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} = {{\max_{i}f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \leq {\sum_{i}{f_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}} = {l\hspace{0pt}\overset{\sim}{F}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}`$ which is the second inequality. The first inequality follows from the fact that $`{\sum_{i}{f_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}} \leq {l\hspace{0pt}{\max_{i}f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}`$. To prove the first result, we start with proving $`{F\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \leq {\hat{F}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}`$. For a given set $`X`$, let $`i_{X}`$ be the index which maximizes $`F`$ so $`{F\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} = {\sum_{j \in X}{f_{i_{X}}\hspace{0pt}{(j)}}}`$. Then $`{f_{i_{X}}\hspace{0pt}{(j)}} \leq {{\max_{i}f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(j)}}`$ from which we get the result. Next, observe that $`{\hat{F}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \leq {\sum_{i = 1}^{l}{f_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}} = {l\hspace{0pt}\overset{\sim}{F}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \leq {l\hspace{0pt}F\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}`$ which follows from the inequality corresponding to $`\overset{\sim}{F}`$. Hence proved. ∎

Finally, we show the impact of using the two modular approximations $`\hat{F}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}`$ and $`\overset{\sim}{F}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}`$ in the place of the function $`F`$ in Robust-scsc and Robust-scsk. Lets call these Problems 1^(′) and 2^(′).

Now note that $`{F\hspace{0pt}{(\overset{\sim}{X})}} \leq {\alpha\hspace{0pt}\overset{\sim}{F}\hspace{0pt}{(X^{\ast})}} \leq {l\hspace{0pt}\alpha\hspace{0pt}F\hspace{0pt}{(X^{\ast})}}`$ which proves the bound. ### 5.4 Submodular $`f_{i}`$’s, Submodular (or Modular) $`g_{i}`$’s

In this section, we shall assume for simplicity that all the submodular functions $`f_{i}`$’s have the same curvature $`\kappa_{f}`$. The results can be trivially be extended even when the curvatures are not the same. In this section, we study the following main classes of algorithms.

- •
  Majorization-Minimization: The first is Majorization Minimization (mmin). mmin proceeds as follows. Initialize $`X^{0} = \varnothing`$. - •
  Ellipsoidal Approximation: The second algorithm is the Ellipsoidal Approximation (ea). Recall that any polymatroid (monotone submodular) function $`f`$, can be approximated by a function of the form $`\sqrt{w^{f}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}`$ for a certain modular weight vector $`w^{f} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{V}`$. Once we convert this into a problem with modular $`f_{i}`$’s, we can use the Algorithms from Section 5.3.
- •
  Average Approximations of mmin and ea: Another framework of algorithms is to approximate the function $`{F\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} = {{\max_{i}f_{i}}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}`$ by the *average approximation* $`{\overset{\sim}{F}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} = \frac{\sum_{i = 1}^{l}{f_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}}}{l}`$ (very similar to what we did in the case of Robust-SubMin. Note that we immediately get back the special case of $`l = 1`$. We can similarly use mmin and ea and we call the resulting algorithms mmin-aa and ea-aa. ### 6.2 Co-operative Matchings

In this set of experiments, we compare Robust-SubMin in co-operative matchings. We follow the experimental setup in (?), which we describe here for completeness. We begin by describing the choice of the submodular functions. We cluster the keypoints separately for each of the two images into $`k = 3`$ groups. The clustering can be performed based on the pixel color map, or simply the distance of the key-points. That is, each image has $`k`$ clusters. Let $`{\{ V_{i}^{(1)}\}}_{i = 1}^{k}`$ and $`{\{ V_{i}^{(2)}\}}_{i = 1}^{k}`$ be the two sets of clusters. We then compute the linear assignment problem, letting $`\mathcal{M} \subseteq \mathcal{E}`$ be the resulting maximum matching. We then partition the edge set $`\mathcal{E} = {\mathcal{E}_{1} \cup \mathcal{E}_{2} \cup {\ldots\hspace{0pt}\mathcal{E}_{k}} \cup \mathcal{E}'}`$ where $`\mathcal{E}_{i} = {\mathcal{M} \cap {({V_{\ell}^{(1)} \times V_{s}^{(2)}})}}`$ for $`{\ell,s} \in {\{ 1,2,\ldots,k\}}`$ corresponding to the $`i`$’th largest intersection, and $`\mathcal{E}' = {\{\mathcal{E}\backslash \cup_{i = 1}^{k}\mathcal{E}_{i}\}}`$ are the remaining edges either that were not matched or that did not lie within a frequently associated pair of image key-point clusters. We then define a submodular function as follows:

$`{{f\hspace{0pt}{(S)}} = {{\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{k}{\psi_{i}\hspace{0pt}{({w\hspace{0pt}{({S \cap \mathcal{E}_{i}})}})}}} + {w\hspace{0pt}{({S \cap \mathcal{E}'})}}}},`$

which provides an additional discount to the edges $`{\{\mathcal{E}_{i}\}}_{i = 1}^{k}`$ corresponding to key-points that were frequently associated in the initial pass. The problem of co-operative matching then becomes an instance of constrained submodular minimization with the submodular function (over the edges) defined above, and a constraint that the edges form a matching. To instantiate Robust-SubMin, we construct $`l`$ clusterings of the pixels: $`\{{(\mathcal{E}_{1}^{1},\cdots,\mathcal{E}_{k}^{1})},\cdots,{(\mathcal{E}_{1}^{l},\cdots,\mathcal{E}_{k}^{l})}\}`$ and define a robust objective:

$`f_{r\hspace{0pt}o\hspace{0pt}b\hspace{0pt}u\hspace{0pt}s\hspace{0pt}t}{(S)} = \max\limits_{{i = 1}:l}\sum\limits_{j = 1}^{k}\psi_{j}{(w{(S \cap \mathcal{E}_{j}^{i})})} + w{(S \cap \mathcal{E}^{i}{{}_{}')}}`$

We run this on the House and Hotel Datasets (?). The results in Figure 3.The house dataset has $`111`$ images, while the hotel dataset has $`101`$ images. We consider all possible pairs of images, with differences between the two images ranging from $`0:10:90`$ in both cases. We consider three algorithms. The first is simple Linear assignment (i.e. modular function), the second is a single submodular function minimization subject to matching constraint (SubMod), and the third is Robust-SubMin subject to a matching constraint. We use the PLA algorithm from (?) for SubMod, since that was observed to outperform mmin. Note that SubMod just uses a single clustering. For Robust-SubMin, we construct $`l = 10`$ clusterings. We observe that $`l = 10`$ tends to perform the best, since with too many clusterings, the algorithms become both slower and also perform worse. The different clustering each obtained by different random initializations of k-means. From the results (Figure 3), we see that Robust-SubMin consistently outperforms both Linear and Sub-Min by about 2 - 5% in accuracy.

### 6.3 Robust Data Subset Selection

In this section, we highlight the scalability and efficacy of our algorithms for Robust-Scsc and Robust-Scsk on robust data subset selection with limited vocabulary. We follow the experimental setup from (?). The function $`f`$ is the vocabulary function, $`{f\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} = {w\hspace{0pt}{({\gamma\hspace{0pt}{(X)}})}}`$ (?). In our experiments, we define several different functions $`f_{i}`$ (with $`l = 10`$). To define each function, we randomly delete 20% of the words from the vocabulary and define $`{f_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} = {w\hspace{0pt}{({\gamma_{i}\hspace{0pt}{(X)}})}}`$. The function $`g`$ is the diversity function and we use the feature based function from (?). Similar to the vocabulary functions, we define $`k = 10`$ different functions $`g_{i}`$ each defined via small perturbations of the feature values. The functions $`g_{i}`$ are normalized so they are all between $`\lbrack 0,1\rbrack`$. The intuition of selecting $`f_{i}`$’s and $`g_{i}`$’s in this way is to be robust to perturbations in features and vocabulary words. These then become instances of Robust-Scsc or Robust-Scsk. In this experiment, we apply the $`f_{i}`$’s as constraints, and as a result, we use the formulation of Robust-Scsk. We run these experiments on Switchboard-I (?) and we restrict ourselves to a ground-set size of $`{|V|} = 100`$. We compare the four algorithms: mmin-aa, ea-aa, mmin, and mmin. The results are shown in Figure 4. As expected, mmin-aa and ea-aa don’t perform as well because they just average the $`\max`$ with the average so do not model the *robustness*. Furthermore, though ea achieves the best theoretical approximation factors, it performs comparably to mmin empirically, though mmin is much faster in practice. mmin runs in around 1.2 seconds while ea takes around 3 minutes. For larger data-sets, the difference is seconds versus hours.

### 6.4 Image Collection Summarization with Multiple Query Sets

Next, we study the performance of Robust-Scsk in the real world application of image collection summarization. In particular, we study the problem of maximizing a single submodular function $`g`$ while minimizing the query-conditional functions $`h\hspace{0pt}{(\left. X \middle| Q_{i} \right.)}`$ for all queries $`Q_{i}`$. To evaluate the algorithms, we use the image collection summarization dataset from (?). The dataset has 14 image collections with 100 images each and provides many human summaries per collection. We use the approach of (?) to consider query focused summarization. In particular, (?) extend the dataset of (?) by creating dense noun concept annotations for every image. They start by designing the universe of concepts based on the 600 object classes in OpenImagesv6 (?) and 365 scenes in Places365 (?). They eliminate concepts common to both (for example, closet) to get a unified list of 959 concepts. The authors then use pretrained YOLOv3 (?) and ResNet50 (?) models for objects and scenes and get initial annotations, which are then cleaned by human annotators.

We use this dataset and create a set of ten query sets, with each query set containing four concepts each. Examples of these concepts are: ”Person, Indoor, Outdoor, Forest, Trees, Beach, Bench, Building” etc. We represent each image using the probabilistic feature vector taken from the output layer of YOLOv3 model pre-trained on OpenImagesv6 and concatenate it with the probability vector of scenes from the output layer of ResNet50 trained on Places365 dataset.

In our experiments, we use two choices for $`g`$. For $`g`$, we use either the cardinality or the facility location function, while we use the facility location function as the choice for $`h`$. As discussed in the motivating section, we pose the problem as minimizing the maximum query conditional function, while setting a coverage constraint on $`g`$. In other words, we solve:

$`\left. {{\min\limits_{X \subseteq V}{\max\limits_{{i = 1}:l}h}}\hspace{0pt}{(\left. X \middle| Q_{i} \right.)}} \middle| {g\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \right. \geq c`$

Since in our experiments $`g`$ is monotone, as we vary $`c`$, we vary the size of the obtained subset. We compare Robust-Scsc (i.e. the optimization problem above) with two baselines. The first is where we just take the sum, i.e. minimize a single average function $`\sum_{i = 1}^{l}{h\hspace{0pt}{(\left. X \middle| Q_{i} \right.)}}`$ and pose this as Scsc: $`{\min_{X \subseteq V}\hspace{0pt}{\sum i}} = \left. {1^{l}\hspace{0pt}h\hspace{0pt}{(\left. X \middle| Q_{i} \right.)}} \middle| {g\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \right. \geq c`$. Note that this is very similar to the Average-Approximation scheme and we call this Avg-Scsc. The second baseline is we simply take a union over all the the query concepts and use the function $`h\hspace{0pt}{(\left. X \middle| {\cup_{i}Q_{i}} \right.)}`$. In this case, the optimization problem is: $`\left. {{\min_{X \subseteq V}h}\hspace{0pt}{(\left. X \middle| {\cup_{i}Q_{i}} \right.)}} \middle| {g\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} \right. \geq c`$. We call this Union-Scsc. For the facility location function, we compute a similarity kernel over the feature vector constructed above. To evaluate the algorithms, we plot the tradeoff between the values of $`g`$ (which is either the subset size or the representation) and the query relevance. We measure query relevance for each query $`Q_{i}`$ by finding the number of images in $`X`$ which are relevant to $`Q_{i}`$ (we denote this by $`r\hspace{0pt}{(X,Q_{i})}`$). We then plot $`g\hspace{0pt}{(X)}`$ on the y-axis and $`{r\hspace{0pt}{(X)}} = {{\min_{i}r}\hspace{0pt}{(X,Q_{i})}}`$ on the x-axis as we vary the subset size.

Since we know the mmin performs comparable to ea, we use mmin as the algorithm here due to its scalability. Similarly for the baselines (Avg-Scsc and Union-Scsc), we use the mmin algorithms since both those problems are instances of Scsc (?). The results are shown in Figure 5. We run this on the first two datasets out of the 14 image collection sets. We see that the Robust-Scsk with mmin consistently outperforms the single query terms with both the sum and the union. This implies that considering all queries individually (via the robust or worst case formulation) does make sure we obtain a set which is relevant to *all* the queries and is therefore fair. We see that Robust-Scsc has a higher value of the minimum query relevance $`{\min_{i}r}\hspace{0pt}{(X,Q_{i})}`$ across the queries compared to Union-Scsc and Avg-Scsc. This is even more clear in the bottom plot in figure 5, where we see that because of the min-max formulation, Robust-Scsc ensures that all of the four queries are equally represented in the subset $`X`$, in contrast to the Avg and Union versions.

## 7 Conclusions

In this paper, we study four formulations Robust Submodular Optimization (Robust-SubMin, Robust-SubMax, Robust-Scsc and RobustScsk) from a theoretical and empirical perspective. We study approximation algorithms and hardness results. We propose a scalable family of algorithms (including the majorization-minimization algorithm) and theoretically and empirically contrast their performance. We empirically compare and contrast the performance of our algorithms on three real world applications: Robust-SubMin with matching constraints for image correspondence, Robust-Scsk for data subset selection with vocabulary constraints, and Robust-Scsc for image collection summarization with multiple queries. In each case, we show that, a) the designed algorithms, and particularly, the majorization-minimization perform better than other heuristics like the average approximation, and also perform comparably to the much more complicated ellipsoidal approximation approach; and b) the robust formulation in fact outperforms the single submodular function based non-robust approaches. In future work, we would like to address the gap between the hardness and approximation bounds, and achieve tight curvature-based bounds in each case. We would also like to study other settings and formulations of robust optimization in future work.
