# ufakzeka-kulak: Building and Evaluating a 31.6M-Parameter Turkish Speech Recognizer from Scratch

Sait Furkan Teke Affiliation: ufak AI
Email: [furkan@ufakai.com](mailto:)

October 2026

###### Abstract

ufakzeka-kulak is an open Turkish speech-to-text model of 31.6M
parameters, a FastConformer encoder with a token-and-duration transducer
(TDT) and a CTC head, trained from scratch and released under
Apache-2.0. It writes cased, punctuated Turkish with numbers as words.
Its 4,977 training hours come from sources whose licences allow
commercial use; 281 hours are human-transcribed, and for the other 4,696
the training text is an uploader subtitle or a machine transcript, kept
only where a second, independent reading of the audio agrees with it.
The recipe, checkpoint, labelling and export choices were made on
development data, and the test sets were scored once, in a release gate
fixed before training. With greedy TDT decoding the normalised word
error rate (WER) is 0.0921 (95 percent interval 0.0792 to 0.1055) on
FLEURS Turkish test and 0.0916 (0.0873 to 0.0958) on Common Voice 27.0
Turkish test, lower than Whisper base and Whisper small on both sets
with paired intervals wholly below zero; Whisper large-v3 (greedy)
scores 0.0604 and 0.0767. On conversational speech the WER is 0.3807. We
also report punctuation and casing, degraded audio, and how closely each
shipped export reproduces the trained model.

## 1 Introduction

Speech recognition for Turkish is available from large multilingual
models such as Whisper \[3\], OWSM \[5\] and Omnilingual ASR \[6\].
ufakzeka-kulak (_kulak_ is Turkish for ear) is a small model for one
language, meant to run offline on a laptop, a server or in a browser
page, with a licence that allows commercial use and a published list of
every training source. It follows the lab’s language model ufakzeka-1
\[1\] and decision model ufakzeka-karar \[2\].

The model is a FastConformer \[8\] with a TDT decoder \[9\] and a CTC
head \[10\] on the same encoder, trained from scratch with NVIDIA NeMo
\[38\]. We make four contributions: (i) the model, with exports for
transformers \[39\], sherpa-onnx \[40\], parakeet.cpp \[42\] and
parakeet-mlx \[43\], each measured against NeMo; (ii) a labelling
pipeline for speech without trusted transcripts, in which one open
recogniser writes the text and an independent one checks it, with both
models and the agreement threshold chosen on human-transcribed speech
that is not trained on; (iii) a recipe chosen by design pilots read by
rules fixed before the runs, and a release gate fixed before any
training; and (iv) reports beyond the gate on punctuation and casing,
degraded audio, a media test set and the runtime differences we found.
Every number for the released model traces to a result file committed in
the project’s repository. Square brackets give 95 percent intervals from
a blockwise percentile bootstrap \[20\] with 2,000 resamples (seed 0),
in blocks of speakers for Common Voice and Real-TurnTurk, of sentences
for FLEURS, which publishes no speaker ids, and of clips for ISSAI TSC
dev and MediaSpeech, which have none; a difference between two systems
is paired, with the same draws for both.

## 2 Model

The encoder has 16 FastConformer layers of width 256 with 4 attention
heads, a feed-forward expansion of 4, a convolution kernel of 9 and
depthwise-separable striding that reduces time by a factor of 8. The
encoder feeds two outputs, a TDT decoder and a CTC head. The TDT decoder
has a one-layer prediction network and a joint network, both of width
640, and predicts each token with a duration of 0 to 4 frames; the TDT
loss uses $`\sigma=0.02`$ and $`\omega=0.1`$ \[9\]. The CTC head is
trained with weight 0.3, and an intermediate CTC loss \[11\] of weight
0.3 is applied after the eighth encoder layer. Input is 16,000 Hz mono
audio as 80 log-mel features with a 25 ms window and a 10 ms stride,
with SpecAugment \[15\] (two frequency masks of width up to 27 and ten
time masks of up to 0.05 of the utterance). The vocabulary is a
SentencePiece \[14\] BPE of 1,024 pieces trained only on the training
transcripts after decontamination. The model has 31,564,935 parameters.
Unless stated otherwise, decoding is greedy, in float32, TDT with at
most 10 tokens per frame.

The model writes Turkish with capital letters, the full stop, comma and
question mark, the apostrophe and the hyphen inside words. Other
punctuation in the training text was mapped to these marks, circumflex
letters are written without the circumflex, and numbers are written as
words (_iki bin yirmi ikide_, not _2022’de_). A training text with a
number form these rules cannot read, such as a dot decimal or a Roman
ordinal, was dropped; Section 3 describes how ambiguous readings were
resolved. It is a full-utterance model, so long recordings must be cut
into utterances first.

## 3 Data

### Sources.

Table 1 lists the training pool; every source’s licence allows
commercial use. The human-transcribed sources are Common Voice Scripted
Speech 27.0 in Turkish \[24\], from which every clip whose sentence or
speaker is in its development or test split was removed, and Common
Voice Spontaneous Speech 5.0 in Turkish; antalia-voice-corpus \[27\],
one professional voice actor; and the train split of the Turkish Speech
Corpus (TSC) of ISSAI \[26\]. ISSAI publishes the corpus under CC BY
4.0; the Hugging Face copy is tagged MIT, and CC BY 4.0 is the licence
followed here. The rest of the pool is YouTube audio collected by YODAS
\[28\] and YODAS v3 \[29\], whose authors say they took only videos
published under a Creative Commons licence and release the audio under
CC BY 3.0; on YouTube the one Creative Commons licence an uploader can
choose for a video is the Creative Commons Attribution licence (CC BY)
\[30\]. Its three parts are the Turkish yodas0.10 subset of ESPnet’s
OWSM v4 data \[5\], the yodas0.00 recordings not in it, and Turkish
YODAS v3 audio cut along the spans of Alania Turkish Speech Style
Captions \[31\] (CC BY 4.0) and merged into units of at most 30 s; only
the span boundaries of the Alania dataset were used, and its transcript
column was never read. We call these the YODAS recordings.

### Text for the YODAS recordings.

Automatic captions are never training text. Whisper large-v3-turbo \[4\]
(MIT) transcribed every unit. Where an utterance has an uploader
subtitle that agrees with that transcript within a WER of 0.2, the
subtitle is the training text. Otherwise, with no subtitle or one that
disagrees, the training text is the transcript, kept only where
Omnilingual ASR CTC 7B v2 \[6\] (Apache-2.0), a CTC model from another
maker, reads the same audio within a character error rate (CER) of 0.10;
this check removed 1,134.62 hours. A number form with more than one
spoken reading takes the reading that Omnilingual ASR scores highest on
the audio if it beats the next by 2.0 nats; otherwise the unit keeps its
rule-based reading or is dropped.

Both models and the threshold were chosen on the ISSAI TSC development
split (3,428 clips, 4.26 h), which is never trained on, under rules
fixed before any score was computed. Whisper large-v3 did not transcribe
it significantly better than large-v3-turbo (WER 0.1573 against 0.1630,
difference $`-0.0057`$ \[$`-0.0170`$, $`+0.0014`$\]), so the faster
turbo was kept. Among the checking models, Omnilingual ASR CTC 7B v2 had
the lowest CER, 0.0514, against 0.0599 for its 3B version and 0.0624 for
Qwen3-ASR-1.7B \[7\] (Apache-2.0). The threshold is the loosest
agreement at which the kept transcripts met fixed targets for their WER
against the references and for the share of clips kept. At a CER of
0.10, 2,541 of 3,428 clips were kept, and their turbo transcripts have a
WER of 0.1249, against 0.1630 for all clips. The ISSAI references are
lowercase and unpunctuated and split some words, which raises every
model’s absolute error rate here; all models are scored against the same
references. The makers of Whisper, Omnilingual ASR and Qwen3-ASR do not
fully disclose their training data. No output of these models other than
the transcripts and readings above is training text.

### Decontamination.

One exclusion set holds the normalised references of every development
and test set used here. It was matched against every training transcript
and tokenizer line, by exact match and by shared word $`n`$-grams, and
audio fingerprints matched the training audio against the evaluation
audio. The matches removed 432 training utterances, 206 of them ISSAI
TSC clips matched by their audio; near-duplicate recordings across the
three YODAS parts were removed as well. FLEURS (any split), the Common
Voice development and test splits, Real-TurnTurk and the 2,511
MediaSpeech clips reported here were never trained on.

### Mix and augmentation.

The training mix samples corpora by a temperature of 0.5 with at most 4
repeats of a source per pass, so the human-transcribed sources make up
25.7 percent of the hours of a pass. The human sources are
speed-perturbed \[21\]. Noise from the MUSAN noise subset \[23\] (only
files that MUSAN’s licence files mark as public domain or CC BY) was
mixed into training audio at random, reverberation was added at random
with the simulated room responses of the OpenSLR room impulse response
database \[22\] (Apache-2.0), and noise cuts with an empty transcript
make up 0.8 percent of the hours of a pass as examples of non-speech.
Random gain, chosen by a pilot (Section 4), multiplies half of the
training cuts by a level drawn uniformly between $`-40`$ and $`+6`$ dB,
after noise and room responses, so speech, noise and reverberation scale
together. The cooldown mix (Section 4) raises the human share to 40
percent and draws its YODAS units only from those whose deciding
agreement is at most 0.05.

## 4 Training and selection

### Recipe.

AdamW \[17\] with $`\beta=(0.9,0.98)`$ and weight decay 0.001, and the
Noam schedule \[16\] with 8,000 warm-up steps, a peak learning rate of
0.0026 and a floor of $`10^{-6}`$. Training starts with a short phase on
shorter clips without noise or room responses, which earlier
from-scratch runs of this project needed to leave the stage in which the
model outputs only blanks. The released weights are an exponential
moving average of the training weights.

### Design pilots.

Before the full run, eleven pilot runs on the full data pool, each a
trunk of the full schedule plus a short cooldown, were read at step
14,500 by rules fixed before any of them started. Eight formed a
$`2^{3}`$ factorial over consistency-regularised CTC \[12\] against
intermediate CTC, a CTC-side weight of 0.1 against 0.3, and BPE-dropout
\[13\] of 0.1 against none; two more seeds of the baseline cell measured
seed noise; an eleventh run added random gain to the baseline cell. The
metric was the WER pooled over the words of both development sets. The
three baseline seeds scored 0.1608, 0.1583 and 0.1601, so the noise term
was set by its floor of 1 percent of their mean (0.0016). A change was
adopted only if it passed a Holm correction \[19\] over the three main
effects at one-sided 0.05, lowered the metric by at least 2 percent, and
its interval on FLEURS alone did not lie wholly above zero. Gain was
judged on the same metric at the original level, which it had to keep
within 1 percent, and on audio attenuated by 20 and 30 dB, which it had
to lower by at least 5 percent and by at least 1.645 standard errors. As
Table 2 shows, none of the three recipe changes helped, and gain was
adopted. The full run continued the gain pilot’s trunk with the baseline
recipe and gain.

### Full run and cooldowns.

The trunk ran to step 119,500; a rule fixed in advance would have
extended it had the in-training validation WER on Common Voice dev
(cased and punctuated text, moving-average weights) fallen by more than
2 percent relative over the last 10,000 steps, and it had not (0.1839 to
0.1845). Two cooldowns of 16,000 steps each then ran from the same
checkpoint to step 135,500, lowering the learning rate $`\eta`$ from its
value at step 119,500, $`\eta_{0}`$, to $`10^{-6}`$ as
$`\eta_{k}=10^{-6}+(\eta_{0}-10^{-6})\,(1-\sqrt{k/K})`$ after $`k`$ of
the $`K=16{,}000`$ steps \[18\], one on the main mix and one on the
cooldown mix. The candidates were each cooldown’s last checkpoint and
the average of its last three saved checkpoints. The rule, fixed before
any was scored, took the lowest mean WER over the two development sets,
sent means equal to three decimals to the candidate with fewer
checkpoints, and between two such to the main mix. All four means were
0.095, so the released model is the main-mix cooldown’s last checkpoint
(0.0830 on Common Voice dev, 0.1071 on FLEURS dev). The two mixes do not
differ significantly (cooldown-mix average minus main-mix average
$`-0.0009`$ \[$`-0.0022`$, $`+0.0005`$\] on Common Voice dev and
$`+0.0007`$ \[$`-0.0033`$, $`+0.0048`$\] on FLEURS dev). This training
line, from the gain pilot’s first step through the main-mix cooldown,
ran on A100 80 GB GPUs for 52.3 GPU session hours, which count setup,
validation passes and checkpoint saves as well as training steps.

## 5 Evaluation

### Protocol.

Every recipe, checkpoint and export choice above was made on Common
Voice 27.0 Turkish dev (11,864 utterances, 12.35 h, 163 speakers) and
FLEURS Turkish dev \[25\] (338 utterances, 1.12 h); the labelling
choices were made on ISSAI TSC dev. The release gate was fixed before
any training. The model ships only if it beats Whisper base on FLEURS
Turkish test and on Common Voice 27.0 Turkish test, each by a paired
interval of the WER difference that lies wholly below zero. The test
sets were scored once, for one model, in a first attempt that passed.
Normalised WER passes reference and hypothesis through our Turkish
normaliser, which applies Unicode NFC, removes bracketed spans, writes
digits as separate words with a verbaliser adapted from the
TurkASR-Bench normaliser \[37\], lowercases in the Turkish way (I to ı,
İ to i), removes in-word apostrophes and all other punctuation, folds
circumflexes and drops filler words such as _ı_, _ee_, _hmm_ and _aa_.
Whisper base, small and large-v3 \[3\] (MIT) were scored by us with the
same normaliser, greedy and, for base and small, with the Whisper
package’s decoding settings as transformers implements them (beam 5 with
temperature fallback); each comparison uses the better of the two on
that set. These scores were computed before any training, those on
MediaSpeech before the gate.

### Release gate.

Both comparisons pass (Table 3), and the model is also below Whisper
small on both sets; Whisper large-v3 remains lower on both. The model
was trained once, with one seed; the intervals come from resampling the
test sets, not from repeated training. On the development sets the TDT
decoder has the lower WER (Common Voice dev 0.0830 \[0.0750, 0.0921\]
against 0.1075 \[0.0983, 0.1175\] for the CTC head; FLEURS dev 0.1071
against 0.1306); no paired test was run.

### Conversational and media speech.

On Real-TurnTurk \[32\], conversational speech of 6 speakers, the WER is
0.3807, about four times that of read speech, and lower than Whisper
base and small. MediaSpeech \[33\] is media speech. Its makers say part
of its utterances come from Mozilla Common Voice, so every clip linked
by text or audio to Common Voice or to our training audio was removed
first; 2 of 2,513 clips matched our training audio, and no clip matched
a Common Voice 27.0 sentence or Common Voice dev or test audio. Its
references join compound numbers into one word, so part of its WER comes
from the reference style; the CER with spaces removed is 0.0420.
Whisper’s training data is not fully disclosed, so this set may not be
unseen for Whisper.

### Punctuation and casing.

The text as written is scored on the test sets against references whose
punctuation is mapped to the model’s marks (Table 4). PC-WER counts
marks and capitals as words; PuncER and CaseER are the parts of the
error that come from marks and from capitals \[34\] (the count of
reference words with a capital, which that paper’s equation does not
define, is our reading); PER is the punctuation error rate of
LibriSpeech-PC \[35\], computed as NeMo implements it. Full stops are
recalled far more often than commas (FLEURS test 0.839 against 0.359,
Common Voice test 0.939 against 0.612). On Real-TurnTurk, where every
reference ends in a mark, the model writes few marks (full-stop recall
0.171, comma recall 0.031) and often misplaces capitals (capital
precision 0.365, recall 0.272).

sherpa-onnx rows are the int8 files the examples load; the float32
copies also ship.

### transformers and parakeet.cpp.

The transformers TDT and CTC folders give NeMo’s token ids on every
development utterance, and the pipeline interface gave the same text as
generate on 10 of 10 utterances of each set and head. One GGUF file
serves both heads in parakeet.cpp; its token ids differ from NeMo’s on
some utterances, but WER stays within 0.0018 of NeMo.

### sherpa-onnx front end.

The sherpa-onnx feature front end differs from NeMo’s in six options. On
an earlier model of the same architecture, with its own data and
tokenizer, an ablation that moved each option alone found only one that
closed the gap, the small constant added before the logarithm, which
weighs most on low-energy frames; the others moved WER slightly. On
FLEURS dev the sherpa-onnx TDT path was $`+0.0108`$ \[$`+0.0055`$,
$`+0.0163`$\] against NeMo’s features and $`-0.0015`$ \[$`-0.0052`$,
$`+0.0021`$\] with only NeMo’s constant, the change merged upstream on
21 October 2026 and released in sherpa-onnx v2.0.0 on 23 October 2026
\[41\]; the files were measured with 1.13.8. Multiplying the input by 8
before decoding is a workaround that holds at normal recording levels,
and the files ship with it. For the released model the float32 files
also pass at gain 1, but the int8 files fail there ($`+0.0071`$ for TDT
and $`+0.0068`$ for CTC with the pad on FLEURS dev) and pass at gain 8.
The gain does not help on quiet audio. At $`-30`$ dB the shipped TDT and
CTC files lose 0.0369 and 0.0427 on FLEURS test, against 0.0205 for
NeMo.

### sherpa-onnx CTC length.

The sherpa-onnx NeMo CTC runtime sets the output length to the number of
feature frames divided by the time reduction, rounded down, rather than
to the encoder’s own length, so the last encoder frame is dropped
whenever the frame count is not a multiple of 8. In this cased model
that frame often carries the final mark. Normalised WER does not see the
loss. Of 11,864 Common Voice dev hypotheses, 1,788 end in a mark with
the stock runtime and 11,644 with NeMo’s own CTC decoding; appending
zeros to the input up to the next multiple of 8 frames, which our
example code does, gives 11,637. The TDT files are not affected.

### parakeet-mlx.

parakeet-mlx computes its input features differently from NeMo, so the
MLX folder ships float32 weights with a feature function that computes
NeMo’s features with numpy and passes them to parakeet-mlx’s decoder,
which runs the weights at bfloat16, its default; the package’s own front
end is not a checked path. bfloat16 counted only if two independent full
runs gave identical token ids on every development utterance, which they
did. These numbers were measured on mlx’s CPU backend under Linux; the
MLX files were not run on Apple silicon.

### Shipped files on the test sets.

The int8 sherpa-onnx TDT file at gain 8 scores 0.0921 on FLEURS test and
0.0932 on Common Voice test, 0.0000 and $`+0.0016`$ from NeMo’s TDT
decoding. The int8 CTC file with the pad scores 0.1128 and 0.1200,
$`+0.0207`$ and $`+0.0284`$ from NeMo’s TDT decoding. That comparison
includes the difference between the heads. In NeMo the CTC head’s WER is
0.0245 higher than TDT’s on Common Voice dev, and the CTC file is within
0.0032 of NeMo’s own CTC decoding there.

### Speed.

The shipped sherpa-onnx files were timed on 60 development utterances in
three length buckets (2 to 5, 5 to 15 and 15 to 30 s), each row in a
fresh process with one untimed and five timed passes. On an Apple M1 at
one thread (sherpa-onnx 1.13.8, Python) the median real-time factor in
the 15 to 30 s bucket is 0.0101 for the int8 TDT files and 0.0155 for
the float32 ones, and the median int8 TDT decode time there is 0.170 s;
in headless Chrome 155 on the same machine the demo’s int8 TDT files run
at 0.0311. On a 2-vCPU x86-64 Linux cloud server (Intel Xeon at 2.20
GHz, a shared virtual machine) the int8 TDT files run at a median
real-time factor of 0.0364 at one thread, and every two-thread row there
had a higher real-time factor than the same file at one thread. Cold
load, decode times and peak memory for every file are in the model card.

## 7 Limitations

The model reads Turkish only; other languages and Turkish mixed with
them were not measured. It is not a streaming model and decodes one
utterance at a time, so long recordings need voice activity detection
first, with a few hundred milliseconds of audio kept around each
segment; a segment cut tight to the speech can come out without capitals
or punctuation. It does not separate speakers, and its word timestamps
were not checked. Numbers come out as words, so an application that
needs digits must convert them. Conversational speech is much harder for
it than read speech, and Real-TurnTurk has only 6 speakers. Accuracy by
speaker gender, age or regional accent was not measured. Commas are
often missed. Most of the training text of the YODAS recordings
(4,326.59 of 4,696.21 hours) was written by one recogniser and checked
by another, where no uploader subtitle agreed with the transcript;
errors on which the two agreed remain in the training text. The model
was trained with one seed, so seed variance is not measured. The
sherpa-onnx files need the input gain and, for CTC, the pad, and they
lose more than NeMo on quiet audio; the MLX path was not measured on
Apple silicon; transformers decodes TDT without NeMo’s per-frame token
cap, which changed no development utterance. MediaSpeech’s interval may
be too narrow, and its references join compound numbers.
