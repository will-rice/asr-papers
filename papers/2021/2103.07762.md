###### Abstract

From Word2Vec to GloVe, word embedding models have played key roles in the current State-of-the-Art results achieved in Natural Language Processing. Designed to give significant and unique vectorized representations of words and entities, those models have proven to efficiently extract similarities and establish relationships reflecting semantic and contextual meaning among words and entities. African Languages, representing more than 31% of the worldwide spoken languages, have recently been subject to lots of research. However, to the best of our knowledge, there are currently no word embedding models for those languages words and entities, like Standford’s Glove or Google’s Word2Vec models. After describing Glove, Word2Vec, and Poincaré embeddings functionalities, we build Word2Vec and Poincaré word embedding models for Fon and Nobiin, which show promising results. We test the applicability of transfer learning between these models as a landmark for African Languages to jointly involve in mitigating the scarcity of their resources and attempt to provide linguistic and social interpretations of our results. Our main contribution is to arouse more interest in creating word embedding models proper to African Languages, ready for use, and that can significantly improve the performances of Natural Language Processing downstream tasks on them.

## 1 Introduction

Fon, and Nobiin are the two African Indigenous Languages (ALs) chosen for this study. Fon is a native language of Benin Republic, spoken in average by more than 2.2 million people in Benin, in Nigeria, and Togo. Nobiin is native to Northern Sudan and Southern Egypt, spoken in average by a million of people. Both languages cover a wide differential range of cultures as the speakers are from Western and Northern Africa.

Fon alphabet is based on the latin alphabet, with the addition of the letters so o, d, e, and the digraphs gb, hw, kp, ny, and xw. There are 10 vowel phonemes in Fon: 6 said to be $`c\hspace{0pt}l\hspace{0pt}o\hspace{0pt}s\hspace{0pt}e\hspace{0pt}d`$ \[i, u, ĩ, ũ\], and 4 said to be $`o\hspace{0pt}p\hspace{0pt}e\hspace{0pt}n\hspace{0pt}e\hspace{0pt}d`$ \[(e, o, a, ã\]. There are 22 consonants (m, b, n, d, p, t, d, c, j, k, g, kp, gb, f, v, s, z, x, h, xw, hw, w).

Nobiin alphabet is primarily based on greek alphabet with some meroitic characters, but in most of the resources and modern usage of the languages, it uses the following schema of 28 letters: there are 10 vowels: 5 are $`o\hspace{0pt}p\hspace{0pt}e\hspace{0pt}n\hspace{0pt}e\hspace{0pt}d`$: (a, e, i, o, u) and 5 are $`c\hspace{0pt}l\hspace{0pt}o\hspace{0pt}s\hspace{0pt}e\hspace{0pt}d`$: (â, ê, î, ô, û). There are 18 consonants: (b, d, f, g, h, j, k, m, n, r, s, t, w, y, sh, ch, gn, ģ).

Word Embedding (WE) modeling is an approach that provides a dense vector representation of words and captures something about their meaning. The goal of embedding methods is to organize symbolic objects (words, entities, concepts etc.) in a way such that their similarities in the embedding space reflects their semantic or functional similarities. WEs models are improvements of naive bag-of-word (BOW) modeling that relies on statistics like word counts and frequencies to create large sparse vectors, describing documents but not the meaning of the words.

WEs work by using an algorithm to train a set of fixed-length dense and continuous-valued vectors based on a large corpus of text. Each word is represented by a point in the embedding space and these points are learned and moved around based on the words that surround the target word. Their massive use in text representations is one of the key methods that led to breakthrough performances in many fields of natural language processing like machine translation, and named entity recognition, just to mention a few. The most famous WEs are GloVe glove, Word2Vec word2vec, and Poincaré Embeddings pointcare.

## 2 Word Embeddings: Related Works

### 2.1 GloVe

GloVe, or Global Vectors for Word Representation, is an approach to capture the meaning of one word embedding towards the corpus (set of documents - a document is a sentence). The GloVe model trains on global co-occurrence counts of words and makes a sufficient use of statistics by minimizing least-squares error. This produces a word vector space with meaningful substructure, that sufficiently preserves words similarities with vector distance. The probability that word at a given index $`t'`$ occurs in the context of a word $`t`$ is defined as:

$`P_{t\hspace{0pt}t'} = \frac{A_{t\hspace{0pt}t'}}{A_{t}}`$ (1)

where $`A`$ is the words co-occurrence matrix. Each entry of $`A`$, is the number of times word $`t'`$ occurs in the context of word $`t`$. The function F, encoding the information about the ratio co-occurrence between two vectors is defined as:

$`{F\hspace{0pt}{({v_{t} - v_{t'}};\hat{v_{k}})}} = \frac{P_{t\hspace{0pt}k}}{P_{t'\hspace{0pt}k}}`$ (2)

where $`v_{t}`$, $`v_{t'}`$ are word vectors with indices $`t`$ and $`t'`$, and $`\hat{v_{k}}`$ a context vector with index $`k`$. The left arguments of F are vectors and the right side is a scalar. Since F could be taken as a complicated parameterized function like a neural network for instance, doing so would obfuscate the linear structure we are trying to capture. To avoid this issue, we can first take the dot product of the arguments, preventing F from mixing the vector dimensions in undesirable ways. The equation (2) becomes then:

$`{F\hspace{0pt}{({{({v_{t} - v_{t'}})}^{T}\hspace{0pt}\hat{v_{k}}})}} = \frac{P_{t\hspace{0pt}k}}{P_{t'\hspace{0pt}k}}`$ (3)

Since in the word-word co-occurrence matrix, the distinction between context words and standard words is arbitrary, probabilities ratio are replaced and the equation (3) becomes:

$`{F\hspace{0pt}{({{({v_{t} - v_{t'}})}^{T}\hspace{0pt}\hat{v_{k}}})}} = \frac{F\hspace{0pt}{({v_{t}^{T}\hspace{0pt}\hat{v_{k}}})}}{F\hspace{0pt}{({v_{t'}^{T}\hspace{0pt}\hat{v_{k}}})}}`$ (4)

In the original paper, glove set the equation (5) and solved it for $`F`$ being the exponential function:

$`{F\hspace{0pt}{({v_{t}^{T}\hspace{0pt}\hat{v_{k}}})}} = P_{t\hspace{0pt}k} = \frac{A_{t\hspace{0pt}k}}{A_{t}}`$ (5)

The final solution of the equation (5) is:

$`{{v_{t}^{T}\hspace{0pt}\hat{v_{k}}} + b_{t} + \hat{b_{k}}} = {l\hspace{0pt}o\hspace{0pt}g\hspace{0pt}{(A_{t\hspace{0pt}k})}}`$ (6)

where $`b_{t}`$ and $`\hat{b_{k}}`$ are respective bias for $`v_{t}`$ and $`\hat{v_{k}}`$, added to restore symmetry.

Finally the loss function to minimize is hence a linear regression function defined as:

```math
{J = {\sum\limits_{{t,t'} = 1}^{V}{({{{f\hspace{0pt}{(A_{t\hspace{0pt}t'})}\hspace{0pt}v_{t}^{T}\hspace{0pt}\hat{v_{t^{\prime}}}} + b_{i} + \hat{b_{j}}} - {l\hspace{0pt}o\hspace{0pt}g\hspace{0pt}{(A_{t\hspace{0pt}t'})}}})}^{2}}},
```

with $`V`$ being the size of the WE’s vocabulary. $`f\hspace{0pt}{(A_{t\hspace{0pt}t'})}`$ is a pre-defined weighting function, that should be continuous, non-decreasing and relatively small for large values of the argument. Obviously, there are infinite functions that could be constructed to satisfy these criterias. The authors glove used the function $`f`$ defined as:

$`{f\hspace{0pt}{(x)}} =`$ {(xxmax)α, x \< xmax1, x ≥xmax\
\
where $`\alpha \in {(0;1)}`$

### 2.2 Word2Vec

There are 3 different types of Word2Vec parameter learning, and all of them are based on neural network models word2vec.

#### 2.2.1 One-Word Context

This approach is known as Continuous Bag-Of-Word (CBOW). The main idea is the consideration of a single word per context i.e. we have to predict one word given only one word. The input of the neural network in this context is a one-hot encoded vector of size (V,) followed by a hidden layer of size N with an input hidden layer weights matrix W of size V×N, and an output layer weights matrix W’ of size N×V, with softmax activation function. The objective here is to compute as probability, the vector representation of the word with index $`i`$:

$`p\hspace{0pt}{(\left. w_{j} \middle| w_{i} \right.)}`$

Let $`a`$ be our input vector filled with zeros, and a single 1 at the position t. The hidden vector $`h`$ is computed with the formula below:

$`h = {W^{T}\hspace{0pt}a} = z_{w_{i}}^{T}`$

where $`z`$ is the output vector of the word $`w_{i}`$. We can look at $`h`$ as the «input vector» of the word $`a`$. At the next step, we take the vector $`h`$ and apply a matrix multiplication similar to the previous one:

$`o_{j} = {z_{w_{j}}^{'T}\hspace{0pt}h}`$

where $`z\hspace{0pt}’`$ is the output vector of the word $`w_{j}`$ with. This multiplication is performed for every entry $`o`$ with index $`j`$. The $`s\hspace{0pt}o\hspace{0pt}f\hspace{0pt}t\hspace{0pt}m\hspace{0pt}a\hspace{0pt}x`$ activation is defined as followed:

$`{p\hspace{0pt}{(\left. w_{j} \middle| w_{i} \right.)}} = y_{j} = \frac{e^{o_{j}}}{\sum_{j' = 1}^{V}e^{o_{j'}}}`$

#### 2.2.2 Multi-Word Context

The concept of Multi-word Context is very similar to the concept of CBOW. The only difference is that we want to capture the relationship between our target word and other words from the corpus. The probability distribution is then defined as:

$`p\hspace{0pt}{(\left. w_{i} \middle| {w_{1,1},\ldots,w_{1,c}} \right.)}`$

obtained by changing the hidden layer function to:

$`h = {\frac{1}{C}\hspace{0pt}{({\sum_{i = 1}^{C}a_{i}})}}`$

The optimization function becomes then:

$`- {l\hspace{0pt}o\hspace{0pt}g\hspace{0pt}{({p\hspace{0pt}{(\left. w_{i} \middle| {w_{1,1},\ldots,w_{1,c}} \right.)}})}}`$

#### 2.2.3 Skip-Gram Model

The concept of Skip-Gram Model is opposite to the Multi-Word Model: the task is to predict $`c`$ context words having one target word on the input. The process of Skip-Gram is the reverse procedure of the Multi-Word Context word2vec. The optimization function is then defined as followed:

```math
\frac{1}{T}\hspace{0pt}{\sum\limits_{t = 1}^{T}{\sum\limits_{{- c} \leq i \leq c}{l\hspace{0pt}o\hspace{0pt}g\hspace{0pt}{({p\hspace{0pt}{(\left. w_{t + i} \middle| w_{t} \right.)}})}}}}
```

$`- c`$ and $`c`$ are limits of the context window and $`w_{t}`$ is a word at index $`t`$. $`T`$ is the total number number of words in the vocabulary. The hidden vector $`h`$ is computed the same way as in the case of CBOW and Multi-Word Context. The output layer is computed with:

$`o_{c,j} = o_{j} = {z_{w_{j}}^{'T}\hspace{0pt}h}`$

and the activation function is defined as followed:

$`{p\hspace{0pt}{({w_{c,j} = \left. w_{j,c} \middle| w_{i} \right.})}} = y_{c,j} = \frac{e^{o_{c,j}}}{\sum_{j' = 1}^{V}e^{o_{j'}}}`$

### 2.3 Poincaré Embeddings

The concept of Poincaré Embeddings uses hyperbolic geometry to capture hierarchical properties of the words that can not be captured directly in Euclidean space. There is a need to use such kind of geometry together with Poincaré ball to capture the fact that distance from the root of the tree to its leaves grows exponentially with every new child, and hyperbolic geometry is able to represent this property. Hyperbolic geometry studies non-Euclidean spaces of constant negative curvature. Its main 2 axioms and theorems are:

- •
  $`\forall`$ line $`a`$ and $`\forall`$ point $`p`$ $`\notin`$ a, there are at least two distinct parallels passing through $`p`$.
- •
  all triangles have angles sum less than 180 degrees.

For 2-dimensional hyperbolic space, both the area $`s`$ and length $`l`$ of a circle, grow exponentially. The are defined with the following formulas:

$`l = {2\hspace{0pt}\pi\hspace{0pt}s\hspace{0pt}i\hspace{0pt}n\hspace{0pt}h\hspace{0pt}{(r)}}`$ and $`s = {2\hspace{0pt}\pi\hspace{0pt}{({{c\hspace{0pt}o\hspace{0pt}s\hspace{0pt}h\hspace{0pt}{(r)}} - 1})}}`$

where $`r`$ denotes the radius. The Poincaré Ball is defined then as:

$`B^{d} = \left. \{{x \in R^{d}} \middle| {{\| x\|} < 1}\} \right.`$

The distance measure between 2 WEs $`t`$ and $`t'`$ is defined as:

$`{d\hspace{0pt}{(t,t')}} = {a\hspace{0pt}r\hspace{0pt}c\hspace{0pt}o\hspace{0pt}s\hspace{0pt}h\hspace{0pt}{({1 + {2\hspace{0pt}\frac{\left\| {t - t'} \right\|^{2}}{{(1 - ||t||^{2})}{({(1 - ||t'||^{2})}}}}})}}`$.

pointcare argued that, this measure allows not only to capture effectively the similarity between the two WEs but also preserves their hierarchy (through their norm).

### 3.1 Contextualization

A lookup at Google’s Word2Vec model, can allow us to define queen as followed: $`{q\hspace{0pt}u\hspace{0pt}e\hspace{0pt}e\hspace{0pt}n} = {{({{k\hspace{0pt}i\hspace{0pt}n\hspace{0pt}g} - {m\hspace{0pt}a\hspace{0pt}n}})} + {w\hspace{0pt}o\hspace{0pt}m\hspace{0pt}a\hspace{0pt}n}}`$. $`W\hspace{0pt}o\hspace{0pt}m\hspace{0pt}a\hspace{0pt}n`$ and $`M\hspace{0pt}a\hspace{0pt}n`$ are genders, while $`q\hspace{0pt}u\hspace{0pt}e\hspace{0pt}e\hspace{0pt}n`$ and $`k\hspace{0pt}i\hspace{0pt}n\hspace{0pt}g`$ could be referred as titles. However, this equation, gives a meaningful and unique representation to the word $`q\hspace{0pt}u\hspace{0pt}e\hspace{0pt}e\hspace{0pt}n`$, and its similarities with the words $`k\hspace{0pt}i\hspace{0pt}n\hspace{0pt}g`$, $`m\hspace{0pt}a\hspace{0pt}n`$ and $`w\hspace{0pt}o\hspace{0pt}m\hspace{0pt}a\hspace{0pt}n`$.

In the context of AL, to the best of our knowledge, there are no such models capable of capturing high-level relation between words and entities. adelani highlighted the difficulty of the evaluation on low-resourced languages, of several architectures that are capable to learn semantic representations from unannotated data, more suitable to high-resourced languages, that have a smorgasbord of tasks and test sets to evaluate on. They conducted also experiments and provided results, comparing word embeddings of two AL (Yoruba and Twi), obtained from curated corpora and a language-dependent processing, using deep learning architectures to learn multilingual representations with high-resource settings.

In general, this lack of research is coupled with the scarcity of the data and the morphological richness of AL. Many studies including orife; ffr, regarding the way to handle the textual data of African languages, have showed the importance of diacritics in the challenging task of creating effective and robust neural machine translation systems, and Natural Language Processing (NLP) tools for AL. Therefore, during the preprocessing of the data used for this study, we made sure to keep the words diacritics, in order to not lose any meaningful information.

We aim to investigate the possibilities of creating proper word embedding models for a better and meaningful representations of AL words and entities; models that could capture exclusive relation between words of the same language. Moreover, the results of our work, could be an effort to creating or improving Named Entity Recognition (NER) models for AL. For each of the languages selected (Fon and Nobiin), we tried the following:

- •
  create a word2vec embedding model, and test its capacity to establish relationships or similarities among words.
- •
  build a Poincaré WE model and use it to predict the types of entities.

To promote reproducibility, and further improvements as well as the use of our findings in NLP tasks on AL. The datasets, and models source code will be open-sourced.

### 4.1 Poincaré Embedding Model for Fon

We implement Fon Poincaré Embedding on a dataset of names (boys, and girls or mixed names), benin cities, body parts, and date components (months of the year, days of the week). $`M\hspace{0pt}i\hspace{0pt}x\hspace{0pt}e\hspace{0pt}d`$ names stand for names that could be attributed to both boys and girls. The dataset consists of a single unique relation among two different entities on each line, and contains 344 data samples. The dataset has been splitted into train (274 samples), validation (25 samples) and test (45 samples). Along the training dataset, we have 82 boy names, 63 girl names, 67 mixed names, 43 benin cities, 12 months of the year and 7 days of the week. The boy and girls names as well as the city names have been scraped respectively from external websites, and from crowd-sourced data through Google Form Surveys. The size of the embedding space is set to $`2`$, chosen for better, understandable visualization and interpretation. We believe that the concept could still be applied to higher dimensions that would however offer less understanding and interpretability. We tried also different constant negative curvatures ($`10`$, $`15`$). All the models have been trained on 2000 epochs. The figures 3, and 4 show different embedding spaces depending of the value of the constant negative curvature. We can notice clearly three different types of entities: $`b\hspace{0pt}o\hspace{0pt}y`$\_$`n\hspace{0pt}a\hspace{0pt}m\hspace{0pt}e`$, $`g\hspace{0pt}i\hspace{0pt}r\hspace{0pt}l`$\_$`n\hspace{0pt}a\hspace{0pt}m\hspace{0pt}e`$ and $`m\hspace{0pt}i\hspace{0pt}x\hspace{0pt}e\hspace{0pt}d`$\_$`n\hspace{0pt}a\hspace{0pt}m\hspace{0pt}e`$. The $`b\hspace{0pt}e\hspace{0pt}n\hspace{0pt}i\hspace{0pt}n`$\_$`c\hspace{0pt}i\hspace{0pt}t\hspace{0pt}y`$ and $`b\hspace{0pt}o\hspace{0pt}d\hspace{0pt}y`$\_$`p\hspace{0pt}a\hspace{0pt}r\hspace{0pt}t`$ entities components are all clustered together and really closed: elements of each group are, based on the graphs practically not distinguishable. Considering the figures 3, and 4 we can see that there are normal connections between $`b\hspace{0pt}o\hspace{0pt}y`$\_$`n\hspace{0pt}a\hspace{0pt}m\hspace{0pt}e`$ and $`m\hspace{0pt}i\hspace{0pt}x\hspace{0pt}e\hspace{0pt}d`$\_$`n\hspace{0pt}a\hspace{0pt}m\hspace{0pt}e`$, as well as between $`g\hspace{0pt}i\hspace{0pt}r\hspace{0pt}l`$\_$`n\hspace{0pt}a\hspace{0pt}m\hspace{0pt}e`$ and $`m\hspace{0pt}i\hspace{0pt}x\hspace{0pt}e\hspace{0pt}d`$\_$`n\hspace{0pt}a\hspace{0pt}m\hspace{0pt}e`$. However, on each figure, there exists an $`i\hspace{0pt}n\hspace{0pt}c\hspace{0pt}o\hspace{0pt}r\hspace{0pt}r\hspace{0pt}e\hspace{0pt}c\hspace{0pt}t`$ or $`n\hspace{0pt}o\hspace{0pt}t`$ $`n\hspace{0pt}o\hspace{0pt}r\hspace{0pt}m\hspace{0pt}a\hspace{0pt}l`$ connection between $`b\hspace{0pt}o\hspace{0pt}y`$\_$`n\hspace{0pt}a\hspace{0pt}m\hspace{0pt}e`$ and $`g\hspace{0pt}i\hspace{0pt}r\hspace{0pt}l`$\_$`n\hspace{0pt}a\hspace{0pt}m\hspace{0pt}e`$ because both are not supposed to tie together. We conclude that the constant negative curvature does not impact how groups intersect together but instead, impacts on the distance between elements among and across groups.

### 4.3 Transfer Learning of Poincaré Embedding Models

## 5 An Approach to Linguistic and Social Interpretations

We noticed a low $`R\hspace{0pt}e\hspace{0pt}c\hspace{0pt}a\hspace{0pt}l\hspace{0pt}l`$ of $`g\hspace{0pt}i\hspace{0pt}r\hspace{0pt}l`$\_$`n\hspace{0pt}a\hspace{0pt}m\hspace{0pt}e`$ for our Poincaré models. As those results could infer some disparities, we tried to look for some linguistic, social interpretations. We found a plausible explanation could be that indigenous girl names share a lot similarities with indigenous boy names (mainly derivated from them), like the names ahoví (prince) and ahossí (princesse), sharing the same root.

![Refer to caption](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.07762/assets/biasedNobinVEC.jpeg)

Figure 7: Visualization of Biased in one of Nobiin Word2Vec Embedding Models

WEs models can illustrate semantic and syntactic relationships between words, but they are not without flaws. The Figure 7 shows one of our models clustering words of housekeeping activities: (floor-sweeping: tôyi), (washing: shûkki), (utensils: dâddi), (cooking: okke) close to word mother (anên). We mitigated this bias by fine-tuning the maximum distance between the current and predicted words within a sentence, in the model of section 3.2.2, which clustered words $`m\hspace{0pt}o\hspace{0pt}t\hspace{0pt}h\hspace{0pt}e\hspace{0pt}r`$ with father (abô), sister (annessi), brother (annenga), and housekeeping activities words with the word house (nôg).

word2vec_bias, also demonstrated in their study how WEs reinforced gender stereotypes at an alarming rate. They tested how Google would translate sentences from Turkish, which uses gender neutral pronouns, to English. Mostly, when a sentence contained descriptors stereotypically attributed to women (cook, teacher, nurse), the Turkish gender-neutral pronoun $`o`$ was translated to $`s\hspace{0pt}h\hspace{0pt}e`$. Conversely, sentences with terms such as hard working, lawyer, and $`e\hspace{0pt}n\hspace{0pt}g\hspace{0pt}i\hspace{0pt}n\hspace{0pt}e\hspace{0pt}e\hspace{0pt}r`$ saw the pronoun translated as $`h\hspace{0pt}e`$. Our point is not to blame Google but instead to emphasize that algorithms are based on a corpus of human words containing billions of data points. So WEs are merely reflecting already existing biases in societies. As our African open source WE models evolve, working on reducing disparities and inequalities would be a great future work pathway.

## 6 Conclusion and Future Work

In this paper we showed the possibilities and importance of building proper word embedding models for African Languages words and entities. We created Word2Vec and Poincaré Embedding models, for Fon and Nobiin, and showed that they can successfully represent similarities and relationships among those words and entities. We also presented promising transfer learning results, from the models accross the languages. The models we proposed can be further improved, since some of the relationships have not been well generalized. Nevertheless, we believe that our findings are headrooms to developing better words embedding models, which could highly ease downstream NLP tasks and challenges on African Languages.
