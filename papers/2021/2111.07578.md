# Introduction

Neural network-based single-channel source separation has made significant advances in the last years. Starting with the seminal papers on deep clustering and <span acronym-label="PIT" acronym-form="singular+short">PIT</span> , improvements have been achieved by combining the two in a multi-objective training criterion , or replacing the <span acronym-label="STFT" acronym-form="singular+short">STFT</span> with a learnable encoder and decoder . Employing convolutional mask estimation network architectures or accounting for short- and longer-term correlations in the signal with recurrent network layers and combining them with a transformer architecture further elevated the performance. Overall, this has led to an improvement in <span acronym-label="siSDR" acronym-form="singular+short">siSDR</span> from roughly 10 dB to more than 20 dB on the standard <span acronym-label="WSJ0-2mix" acronym-form="singular+short">WSJ0-2mix</span> data set , which consists of artificial mixtures of nonreverberant speech.[^1]

However, an anechoic environment is a rather unrealistic assumption for speech separation as in a real-world scenario, the superposition of the speech of two or more speakers typically occurs in a distant microphone setting. A distant microphone naturally captures a reverberated signal. A practically much more relevant setting is thus the separation of mixtures of reverberated speech.

Source separation of noisy and reverberant mixtures is much harder. In particular, reverberation has been considered more challenging than noise . This comes to no surprise because the key assumptions underlying monaural mask-based source separation, namely the sparsity and orthogonality of speech representations in the <span acronym-label="STFT" acronym-form="singular+short">STFT</span> domain, tend to break down under reverberation.

WHAMR! and SMS-WSJ are two widely used data sets for research on source separation for reverberant mixtures. Both contain artificially reverberated utterances from the WSJ corpus. While WHAMR! additionally contains environmental noise, SMS-WSJ consists of 6-channel microphone array data and allows for performance comparison w.r.t.<span acronym-label="WER" acronym-form="singular+short">WER</span> as it is accompanied by a Kaldi recipe . Source separation performance on WHAMR! is in the range of 2 db–8 db output <span acronym-label="siSDR" acronym-form="singular+short">siSDR</span> [^2], while the performance on SMS-WSJ is in the range of 5 db–6 db <span acronym-label="siSDR" acronym-form="singular+short">siSDR</span> for single-channel input and single-stage processing , which is much worse than the performance on clean, anechoic mixtures. In this contribution, we employ SMS-WSJ for our experiments because we wish to assess the performance of the separation system not only by the signal-related evaluation metric <span acronym-label="SDR" acronym-form="singular+short">SDR</span> but also by <span acronym-label="WER" acronym-form="singular+short">WER</span>, given that the SMS-WSJ Kaldi recipe allows us to compare the <span acronym-label="WER" acronym-form="singular+short">WER</span> performance across different publications.

This paper is not about suggesting a new algorithm for reverberant source separation. We rather aim to explore, in a systematic way, which of the recent innovations that proved useful for the separation of anechoic mixtures are also beneficial in the reverberant case, in order to propose some guidelines on how to adjust a separation system to reverberated input.

As our outset, we take the SepFormer architecture, which achieves state-of-the-art performance both on <span acronym-label="WSJ0-2mix" acronym-form="singular+short">WSJ0-2mix</span> and WHAMR! , and the traditional <span acronym-label="PIT-BLSTM" acronym-form="singular+short">PIT-BLSTM</span> source separation model from . Here, we modify and optimize the <span acronym-label="PIT-BLSTM" acronym-form="singular+short">PIT-BLSTM</span> to detect which differences between both models aside from the separator lead to a better separation performance. Then, we modify the SepFormer w.r.t. loss function, encoder/decoder architecture and resolution to mitigate the performance degradation between the anechoic and reverberant scenario. Indeed, we are able to improve the performance w.r.t.<span acronym-label="WER" acronym-form="singular+short">WER</span> by 7 percentage points compared to the vanilla SepFormer implementation. Nevertheless, the final result turns out to be hardly superior to that of the optimized <span acronym-label="PIT-BLSTM" acronym-form="singular+short">PIT-BLSTM</span>, calling into question the importance of some of the innovations of recent years for the realistic case of reverberant speech separation.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. In <a href="#sec:separation_pipeline" data-reference-type="ref+label" data-reference="sec:separation_pipeline">2</a> the <span acronym-label="PIT-BLSTM" acronym-form="singular+short">PIT-BLSTM</span> and the SepFormer are briefly introduced as two realizations of an abstracted pipeline for mask-based source separation. <a href="#sec:assumptions" data-reference-type="ref+Label" data-reference="sec:assumptions">3</a> discusses design choices in light of the requirements of a reverberated input. In <a href="#sec:evaluation" data-reference-type="ref+Label" data-reference="sec:evaluation">4</a> the SepFormer is optimized for performance on reverberant data and compared to the PIT model in <a href="#sec:comparison" data-reference-type="ref+label" data-reference="sec:comparison">5</a>. The paper concludes with a short discussion in <a href="#sec:conc" data-reference-type="ref+label" data-reference="sec:conc">6</a>.

# Mask-based source separation

Mask-based systems for single-channel source separation can be abstracted to the same general processing pipeline that is depicted in <a href="#fig:separation_pipeline" data-reference-type="ref+label" data-reference="fig:separation_pipeline">1</a>.

<figure id="fig:separation_pipeline">

<figcaption>Block diagram of mask-based source separation </figcaption>
</figure>

First, the observed time-domain signal $`y(\ell)`$ is transformed into a latent space (e.g., the <span acronym-label="STFT" acronym-form="singular+short">STFT</span> domain or a learned representation). In this latent space, the encoded mixture $`y(t,f)`$ with time index $`t`$ and latent feature index $`f`$ is used as the input of the neural separation module, which estimates a mask $`m_k(t,f)`$ for the reconstruction of each active speaker $`k`$ in the observation. Then, the estimated signal $`\hat{x}_k(t,f)`$ of each speaker is obtained by masking the mixture with the estimated masks
``` math
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:mask_multiplication}
    \hat{x}_k(t,f) = y(t,f)m_k(t,f).
\end{align}
```
The reconstructed signals $`\hat{x}_k(t,f)`$ are then transformed back into the time-domain in the decoder.

Both the <span acronym-label="PIT-BLSTM" acronym-form="singular+short">PIT-BLSTM</span> approach to monaural source separation and the SepFormer (the latter providing state-of-the-art results on <span acronym-label="WSJ0-2mix" acronym-form="singular+short">WSJ0-2mix</span>) use a mask-based separation. By comparing these two models, which, in principle, share the same overall structure of <a href="#fig:separation_pipeline" data-reference-type="ref+label" data-reference="fig:separation_pipeline">1</a>, we investigate if modifications that were found to be useful in the anechoic scenario can be transferred to the reverberant case.

# Source separation under reverberation

Mask-based source separation relies on the sparsity and orthogonality of the sources in the the domain where the masks are computed. In case of the <span acronym-label="STFT" acronym-form="singular+short">STFT</span> domain, this means that a time-frequency bin $`(t,f)`$ of a mixture $`y(t,f)`$ can be approximated by the contribution of the dominant source $`i(t,f)`$

``` math
\begin{align}
    % \begin{split}
    y(t,f) &= \sum_{k=1}^K s_k(t,f)h_k(t,f) \nonumber \\%[-0.em]
            &\approx s_{i(t,f)}(t,f)h_{i(t,f)}(t,f)
            \label{eq_orth_mtfa}
    % \end{split}
\end{align}
```
where $`s_k(t,f)`$ and $`h_k(t,f)`$ are the STFT representations of the $`k`$-th source signal and the <span acronym-label="RIR" acronym-form="singular+short">RIR</span> from the $`k`$-th source to the microphone, respectively. Further, $`i(t,f)\in\{1,\ldots K\}`$ indicates which of the $`K`$ sources dominates in bin $`(t,f)`$.

Note that Eq. <a href="#eq_orth_mtfa" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq_orth_mtfa">[eq_orth_mtfa]</a> makes the additional assumption that the convolution of the source signal $`s(\ell)`$ with the <span acronym-label="RIR" acronym-form="singular+short">RIR</span> $`h(\ell)`$ corresponds to a multiplication of their respective STFT transforms. This so-called <span acronym-label="MTFA" acronym-form="singular+short">MTFA</span>, however, only holds true if the temporal extent of $`h(\ell)`$ is smaller than the STFT analysis window . When the window length is decreased, this assumption becomes more and more questionable, and the <span acronym-label="CTFA" acronym-form="singular+short">CTFA</span> would be more appropriate. Obviously, this challenges mask-based source reconstruction according to Eq. <a href="#eq:mask_multiplication" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:mask_multiplication">[eq:mask_multiplication]</a>, and the complications are the more pronounced the smaller the STFT analysis window is.

When switching from a fixed STFT encoder to a learnable encoder, the overall structure of the system, see <a href="#fig:separation_pipeline" data-reference-type="ref+Label" data-reference="fig:separation_pipeline">1</a>, stays the same. Therefore, it can be assumed that similar issues arise with the learnable encoder. In the following we will thus study the influence of the encoder/decoder and their temporal resolution on the separation performance.

# Evaluation

## Database and Baseline Results

In order to assess which effect a specific component of a separation module has both on nonreverberant and reverberant data, it is important to run the experiments on a corpus that differs only in this respect. We employ the SMS-WSJ data set for our analysis, which easily allows us to generate both anechoic and reverberant two-speaker mixtures.

For the anechoic scenario, the reverberation time $`T_{60}`$ is reduced from 0.2 s–0.5 s to zero while keeping an otherwise identical data simulation. Dynamic mixing is employed in training: each example during training consists of randomly drawn utterances from WSJ database and only the RIRs are reused to provide a dramatically increased number of examples, which has been proven to improve the system’s performance . To show the competitiveness of the used models, we also provide baseline results on the <span acronym-label="WSJ0-2mix" acronym-form="singular+short">WSJ0-2mix</span> database.

The <span acronym-label="PIT-BLSTM" acronym-form="singular+short">PIT-BLSTM</span> model consists of 3 BLSTM layers with 600 units each, followed by 2 fully connected layers. The encoder and decoder are set to the <span acronym-label="STFT" acronym-form="singular+short">STFT</span> and inverse <span acronym-label="STFT" acronym-form="singular+short">STFT</span> with a window size of 512, a frame advance of 128 and an embedding dimension (number of frequency bins) of 257 at 8 kHz sampling rate. The output of the STFT encoder is the concatenated real and imaginary part of the spectrum as in .

The SepFormer uses the same parameters as proposed in with a window size of 16, a frame advance of 8 and a latent dimension of 256, aside from reducing the number of intra- and inter-Transformer layers to 4, each. This modification yields an about 1 db lower <span acronym-label="SDR" acronym-form="singular+short">SDR</span> on WSJ0-2mix, but significantly reduces the number of parameters, see entry “SepFormer (small)” in <a href="#tab:sdr_ref_wsj2mix" data-reference-type="ref+label" data-reference="tab:sdr_ref_wsj2mix">[tab:sdr_ref_wsj2mix]</a>. Thus, for all following experiments this “small” configuration is employed due to computational limitations. Note, that the memory footprint of the small SepFormer still is 16 times larger than the <span acronym-label="PIT-BLSTM" acronym-form="singular+short">PIT-BLSTM</span>, so that a complexity comparison purely based on the parameters is not fair. The learnable encoder is a single CNN layer with 256 channels, i.e. the latent size, followed by a ReLU, and the decoder has only one CNN layer as in .

Both architectures use the Adam optimizer and the early reverberated signals as target as proposed in . The SepFormer is trained with a soft-thresholded time-domain <span acronym-label="SDR" acronym-form="singular+short">SDR</span> loss
``` math
\begin{align}
    % \mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{th-SDR}}(x, \hat{x}) = -10\log_{10} \frac{\lVert x\rVert^2}{\lVert \hat{x}-x\rVert^2 + \tau\lVert x \rVert^2}\\
    % \mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{th-SDR}} = -10\log_{10} \min_{\pi} \frac{\sum\limits_\ell x_k(\ell)^2}{\sum\limits_\ell \left(\hat{x}_{\pi(k)}(\ell) - x_k(\ell)\right)^2 + \tau \sum\limits_\ell x_k(\ell)^2} \\
    \mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{th-SDR}} = 
    %\min_{\pi} 
    10\log_{10} \frac{1}{K} \sum_k \left( \frac{\sum\limits_\ell \left\lvert\hat{x}_{k}(\ell) - x_k(\ell)\right\rvert^2}{\sum\limits_\ell \left\lvert x_k(\ell)\right\rvert^2} + \tau\right),
    \label{eq:th_sdr}
\end{align}
```
where $`\tau=10^{-\mathrm{SDRmax}/10}`$ and $`\mathrm{SDRmax} =  \text{\SI{20}{\decibel}}`$. This loss decreases the contribution of well separated examples to the gradient, encouraging the model to focus more on enhancing examples with a low <span acronym-label="SDR" acronym-form="singular+short">SDR</span> than those that already show a good separation. The Baseline PIT-BLSTM is trained with a frequency-domain <span acronym-label="SDR" acronym-form="singular+short">SDR</span> loss. The models are evaluated w.r.t.<span acronym-label="SDR" acronym-form="singular+short">SDR</span>, PESQ , and <span acronym-label="WER" acronym-form="singular+short">WER</span>. We use the <span acronym-label="SDR" acronym-form="singular+short">SDR</span> metric proposed in , as it allows an evaluation against the anechoic speech source. The PESQ values also are given w.r.t. the speech source, and the <span acronym-label="WER" acronym-form="singular+short">WER</span> results on SMS-WSJ are determined with the acoustic model from .

<a href="#tab:sdr_ref_wsj2mix" data-reference-type="ref+Label" data-reference="tab:sdr_ref_wsj2mix">[tab:sdr_ref_wsj2mix]</a> and <a href="#tab:sdr_ref_smswsj" data-reference-type="ref+label" data-reference="tab:sdr_ref_smswsj">[tab:sdr_ref_smswsj]</a> display the results of the baseline systems on WSJ0-2mix and SMS-WSJ, respectively. It can be seen that both systems degrade under the presence of reverberation. However, the separation performance of the SepFormer degrades by more than 10 db in terms of <span acronym-label="SDR" acronym-form="singular+short">SDR</span> and almost 30 percentage points regarding the <span acronym-label="WER" acronym-form="singular+short">WER</span>. We wish to find out which components of the SepFormer make it become so sensitive to reverberation.

## PIT-BLSTM optimization

First, we optimized the performance of the <span acronym-label="PIT-BLSTM" acronym-form="singular+short">PIT-BLSTM</span> on reverberant input data. To do so, we switched the training objective from the frequency-domain loss to the thesholded time-domain loss described in <a href="#eq:th_sdr" data-reference-type="ref+label" data-reference="eq:th_sdr">[eq:th_sdr]</a>. In this way, even though the <span acronym-label="PIT-BLSTM" acronym-form="singular+short">PIT-BLSTM</span> uses the magnitude spectrum for the mask estimation, the phase has an influence on the computed loss. In addition, we added white Gaussian noise at an SNR of 25 db to the separated audio files before they were input to the speech recognizer. This is to mask artefacts that were introduced during the source separation. Next to an improved <span acronym-label="WER" acronym-form="singular+short">WER</span> we also observed a higher correlation between the signal-level metric <span acronym-label="SDR" acronym-form="singular+short">SDR</span> and the <span acronym-label="WER" acronym-form="singular+short">WER</span>, rendering the <span acronym-label="SDR" acronym-form="singular+short">SDR</span> a better predictor of the ASR performance. As shown in <a href="#tab:reference_sep" data-reference-type="ref+label" data-reference="tab:reference_sep">[tab:reference_sep]</a>, by introducing the latter modifications the performance of the <span acronym-label="PIT-BLSTM" acronym-form="singular+short">PIT-BLSTM</span> is significantly improved both in terms of <span acronym-label="SDR" acronym-form="singular+short">SDR</span> and <span acronym-label="WER" acronym-form="singular+short">WER</span>. Even more so, these modifications work well both with and without reverberation and lead to a reduction in <span acronym-label="WER" acronym-form="singular+short">WER</span> of more than 20 percentage points for both scenarios.

## SepFormer optimization

The above changes to the <span acronym-label="PIT-BLSTM" acronym-form="singular+short">PIT-BLSTM</span> system also lead to improvements of the Sepformer, see <a href="#tab:reference_sep" data-reference-type="ref+label" data-reference="tab:reference_sep">[tab:reference_sep]</a>. Therefore, the Gaussian noise is added in all following evaluations. However, it is striking that the Sepformer is no longer superior to the <span acronym-label="PIT-BLSTM" acronym-form="singular+short">PIT-BLSTM</span> system for reverberant data. Therefore, we gradually exchanged the Sepformer’s components with those of the <span acronym-label="PIT-BLSTM" acronym-form="singular+short">PIT-BLSTM</span> system to investigate the cause of this performance loss and what the best configuration is for reverberant input.

### Encoder/decoder choice

There is a large mismatch between the window size and the frame advance of standard PIT-BLSTM and SepFormer systems. To verify whether the violation of the <span acronym-label="MTFA" acronym-form="singular+short">MTFA</span> caused by the small window size of the SepFormer contributes to the system deterioration under reverberation, we evaluated the SepFormer for multiple encoder/decoder configurations. As opposed to other works , we only increase the window size while maintaining small shift sizes in order to retain a high temporal resolution. <a href="#tab:encoder_sizes_anechoic_and_reverberant" data-reference-type="ref+Label" data-reference="tab:encoder_sizes_anechoic_and_reverberant">[tab:encoder_sizes_anechoic_and_reverberant]</a> shows the expected behavior for the SepFormer in anechoic conditions: reducing the frame shift leads to an improvement in <span acronym-label="SDR" acronym-form="singular+short">SDR</span> and <span acronym-label="WER" acronym-form="singular+short">WER</span>. The recommended analysis window size and shift of 16 and 8 samples (i.e. 2 ms and 1 ms) , respectively, provides the best results for anechoic data. Furthermore, the learnable encoder proves superior to the <span acronym-label="STFT" acronym-form="singular+short">STFT</span> encoder.

Conversely, for the reverberant scenario, while the STFT encoder in <a href="#tab:encoder_sizes_anechoic_and_reverberant" data-reference-type="ref+label" data-reference="tab:encoder_sizes_anechoic_and_reverberant">[tab:encoder_sizes_anechoic_and_reverberant]</a> is significantly worse than a learnable encoder for a small window size and shift, it begins to be on par or even outperforms the learnable encoder for an increased window size of 32 ms. This validates our assumption that the violation of the <span acronym-label="MTFA" acronym-form="singular+short">MTFA</span> contributes to the poor model performance under reverberation. Interestingly, the overall best results of the SepFormer are achieved with the STFT.

Our assumptions are further supported by the <span acronym-label="WDO" acronym-form="singular+short">WDO</span> score which measures the orthogonality of the single-speaker utterances in the latent space. Following on the results from <a href="#tab:encoder_sizes_anechoic_and_reverberant" data-reference-type="ref+label" data-reference="tab:encoder_sizes_anechoic_and_reverberant">[tab:encoder_sizes_anechoic_and_reverberant]</a> it becomes apparent that the baseline SepFormer learns a highly orthogonal space for anechoic data. However, by switching to reverberant data, the WDO decreases by 5 percentage points. This decrease is mitigated by a larger encoder window size. The same is true for the STFT encoder, where the regularizing effect of a larger window size is even more pronounced. This indicates that the learnable encoder is able to compensate the effects to some degree, but that choosing a large enough window size is mandatory to stabilize the performance under reverberation.

### Data representation for the mask estimator

A significant difference between <span acronym-label="PIT-BLSTM" acronym-form="singular+short">PIT-BLSTM</span> and SepFormer is that the PIT model estimates the masks based on the magnitude spectrogram only, whereas the SepFormer mask estimator has access to the complete signal, i.e., both magnitude and phase in case of the STFT representation.

To compare both networks with the same input representation, the effect of only using the magnitude as input for the mask estimator in the SepFormer is evaluated. The SepFormer trained with concatenated real and imaginary parts estimates separate masks for the real and imaginary parts of the observation, respectively. When only using the magnitude for the mask estimation, the estimated masks are applied both on the real and imaginary parts. <a href="#tab:phase_info" data-reference-type="ref+Label" data-reference="tab:phase_info">[tab:phase_info]</a> shows that the availability of the phase information is not helpful for the SepFormer in the reverberant scenario. Even more so, omitting the phase information leads to a better system performance.

This can have two reasons. Firstly, only using the magnitude spectrogram results in a larger window of 512 samples to keep the size of the separator identical, increasing the temporal context of each frame even further. Secondly, has shown that the phase becomes less informative while the magnitude becomes more informative for increasing frame sizes. The configurations trained with both the phase and magnitude information learn a trade-off between phase and magnitude reconstruction. However, the large window sizes that were shown to be necessary in <a href="#tab:encoder_sizes_anechoic_and_reverberant" data-reference-type="ref+label" data-reference="tab:encoder_sizes_anechoic_and_reverberant">[tab:encoder_sizes_anechoic_and_reverberant]</a> for the reverberant scenario result in an uninformative phase representation. Therefore, omitting this information only slightly deteriorates the system performance for a small frame shift. However, by further increasing the frame shift the magnitude spectrogram becomes more informative. Therefore, using the magnitude allows increasing the frame shift from 16 to 128 samples, reducing the computational effort by almost a factor of 8 compared to the best configuration in <a href="#tab:encoder_sizes_anechoic_and_reverberant" data-reference-type="ref+label" data-reference="tab:encoder_sizes_anechoic_and_reverberant">[tab:encoder_sizes_anechoic_and_reverberant]</a> while simultaneously improving both signal-level metrics and <span acronym-label="WER" acronym-form="singular+short">WER</span>.

# Conclusions

In this paper, we investigated the impact of reverberation on the various design choices for the SepFormer source separation system that is considered state-of-the-art for anechoic mixtures. We showed that it is mandatory to choose a large enough encoder window size for reverberant data. Also, we demonstrated that the STFT likewise is a valid choice as encoder and decoder. Here, it becomes apparent that the phase information no longer is helpful for the separation and only using the magnitude information provides superior results while reducing the computational complexity significantly.

Despite several modifications which greatly improved the performance of the SepFormer on reverberated mixtures, it was in the end hardly superior to a <span acronym-label="PIT-BLSTM" acronym-form="singular+short">PIT-BLSTM</span> separation system, which was optimized with only rather straightforward modifications, such as loss computation in time domain. At least for a single-stage approach, our experiments indicate that jointly focusing on phase and magnitude reconstruction leads to subpar results compared to solely focusing on magnitude reconstruction under reverberation. This raises the issue of whether the improvements that have been appraised for the separation of anechoic mixtures, such as learnable encoder and phase reconstruction, are futile for the more realistic case of reverberant source separation.

We therefore argue that research on source separation should primarily focus on the practically more relevant case of reverberant input, rather than on the anechoic scenario. Since jointly tackling both reverberation and overlapped speech appears to be a challenging task, an alternative solution is to apply an explicit dereverberation component and/or employ multi-stage processing, as in .
