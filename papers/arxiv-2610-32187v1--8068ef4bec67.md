###### Abstract

Unified streaming speech language models aim to support speech
recognition and synthesis within a single model, enabling real-time
human–computer interaction. However, jointly supporting these two
directions of speech–text conversion while preserving task-specific
performance remains challenging. We propose Uni-Stream, a unified
streaming speech language model built on compact pretrained speech
models. Uni-Stream processes interleaved text and speech in a streaming
format and achieves performance comparable to task-specific models
through two-stage training. We also develop a data annotation pipeline
for streaming training and will publicly release the generated
transcriptions and word-level timestamps. We further examine
Uni-Stream’s representations across tasks and modalities and find that
speech and text residence in distinct latent feature space even when
trained in a unified model architecture, while representations within
each modality become more task-dependent in later layers.

## 1 Introduction

Unified streaming speech language models integrate automatic speech
recognition (ASR) and text-to-speech (TTS) synthesis within a single
model, facilitating real-time human–computer interaction and low-cost
model deployment applications. However, jointly training a model on both
tasks may potentially degrade performance relative to task-specific
models in performing ASR or TTS. A central challenge is therefore to
combine recognition and synthesis in a streaming setting with low
latency while retaining the capabilities of each task-specific model.

Prior work has pursued unification and streaming largely separately.
Unified speech language models perform recognition and synthesis with a
shared backbone (Yan et al., 2025; Guan et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026;
Cai et al., 2026), but most operate offline and require complete speech
or text inputs. Streaming recognition and synthesis systems process
their input incrementally, either by interleaving text and speech in one
sequence (Du et al., 2024; Bai et al., 2025; Tu et al., 2026) or by
delaying one stream relative to the other (Défossez et al., 2024;
Zeghidour et al., 2025), with a separate model trained for each task.
Chen et al. (2021b) developed a unified streaming model for TTS and ASR
based on a neural transducer, but their evaluation was limited to
single-speaker data. To our knowledge, no prior work has demonstrated
both recognition and synthesis with streaming input using a single
shared decoder-only language-model backbone.

In this work, we develop Uni-Stream, a unified streaming speech language
model, through three design choices: streaming speech–text interleaving,
pretrained backbone selection, and two-stage training. For speech–text
interleaving, we compare fixed-ratio and time-aligned formats in TTS.
Time-aligned interleaving yields lower TTS word error rates in our
streaming adaptation experiments, motivating its use in the unified
model. To build on pretrained capabilities for both ASR and TTS, we
select the backbone from the VibeVoice family (Peng et al., 2025; Peng
et al., 2026), whose TTS and ASR variants use similar architectures,
especially for the identical speech tokenizer. We first adapt separate
TTS and ASR models to our streaming formats, then initialize Uni-Stream
from the adapted TTS model and jointly train it on both tasks. We
formulate task-specific distillation objectives to preserve synthesis
capabilities and facilitate recognition learning, and examine their
contributions through training ablations. Our evaluation shows that the
unified model achieves performance comparable to the task-specific
models on both TTS and ASR. To support training in the time-aligned
format, we develop a transcript voting and word-level time-alignment
pipeline, retaining approximately 40,000 hours of Emilia-EN for training
after filtering. We will release voted transcriptions for Emilia-EN and
word-level timestamps for Emilia-EN, GigaSpeech, and LibriSpeech to
support reproducibility and further research.

Beyond task performance, we ask how the unified model represents speech
and text. Does learning both speech-to-text and text-to-speech mappings
bring their internal representations closer together? To investigate
this question, we probe representations across tasks, modalities, and
layers. We find that speech and text representations remain distinct
across layers despite joint training in both directions. Across tasks,
representations are similar in early layers but diverge in later layers.

Our contributions are threefold:

- •
  We develop a unified streaming speech language model for both ASR and
  TTS with a set of shared weights, achieving performance comparable to
  task-specific streaming models on both tasks.
- •
  We show through representation probing that speech and text remain
  distinct, while TTS and ASR representations are similar in lower
  layers and diverge in upper layers.
- •
  We transcribe and have time-aligned segmentation for 40,000 hours of
  English data, facilitating the future time-aligned streaming modeling
  research.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Speech Language Models

Language-model-based speech recognition systems generate transcripts
from encoded speech (Ma et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2026a; Peng et al.,
2026). Language-model-based speech synthesis systems generate speech
conditioned on text (Chen et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024; Du et al.,
2024; Peng et al., 2025). Unified models perform both tasks with a
single language model, operating on discrete speech tokens (Cai et al., 2026) or on continuous representations (Guan et al., 2025; Yan et al.,
2025; Li et al., 2026). Balancing the two capabilities remains delicate:
recognition and synthesis place competing demands on speech
representations (Yan et al., 2025), and starting the generation module
from a synthesis-trained checkpoint improves synthesis but can reduce
recognition accuracy (Li et al., 2026). The results reported for these
models, some with progressive output, do not establish that both tasks
run on streaming input.

Streaming means processing the input as it arrives, with bounded
lookahead, not only emitting output progressively. Serial interleaving
places text and speech in one sequence: recognition alternates audio
chunks with incremental transcripts (Tu et al., 2026), and synthesis
alternates text and speech at a fixed ratio (Du et al., 2024) or at word
boundaries with text lookahead (Bai et al., 2025); our time-aligned
format follows the latter. Delayed-stream models instead keep text and
audio in parallel and use separately trained recognition and synthesis
models with opposite delay directions (Défossez et al., 2024; Zeghidour
et al., 2025). Full-duplex dialogue models share a backbone for
listening and speaking, with an emphasis on conversational interaction
(Défossez et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025). An early transducer,
Speech-T, was trained jointly for synthesis and recognition with
streaming in both directions on a single-speaker corpus (Chen et al.,
2021b). We study how to extend a pretrained speech synthesis model to
both tasks with streaming input while retaining the quality of our
separately adapted streaming models.

### 2.2 Speech–Text Representations in Speech Language Models

Speech shares linguistic content with text while carrying additional
information about prosody, speaker identity, and acoustic context.
Understanding how these shared and modality-specific attributes are
represented is important to joint speech–text modeling. Evidence from
multimodal language models suggests that non-textual inputs can acquire
language-aligned representations in intermediate or later layers (Wu et
al., 2025; Neo et al., 2025; Nikankin et al., 2026). However, studies of
cross-model alignment caution that semantic overlap and functional
compatibility do not necessarily imply a common representational
structure (Koepke et al., 2026; Smith et al., 2025). The relationship
between cross-modal alignment and effective information use remains an
open question.

In speech language models, this question spans both tokenization and
language modeling. Codec probing reveals that phonetic,
lexical-semantic, and accent information are encoded unevenly across
representations, highlighting the importance of tokenizer design (Shi et
al., 2026b; Wang et al., 2026). Yet retaining information does not
ensure its effective use: acoustic attributes can remain recoverable
from LM hidden states even when the output head fails to express them in
task predictions (Jo et al., 2026). These findings motivate examining
both what speech representations preserve and how the LM accesses that
information.

## 3 Uni-Stream: Unified Streaming Speech Language Model

### 3.1 Overview

We propose Uni-Stream, a unified streaming speech language model that
performs text-to-speech synthesis (TTS) and automatic speech recognition
(ASR) with shared weights. To this end, we design the model from three
key aspects: streaming speech–text interleaving data format, pretrained
backbone selection, and two-stage training.

As shown in Figure 1, each input sequence begins with a task token
specifying TTS or ASR, followed by interleaved text and speech chunks.
We build on the VibeVoice model family (Peng et al., 2025; Peng et al.,
2026; Tu et al., 2026) for its synthesis and recognition capabilities.
Our two-stage training procedure first adapts separate TTS and ASR
models to our streaming formats, then initializes Uni-Stream from the
adapted TTS model and jointly trains it on both tasks. We introduce
synthesis anchoring and recognition distillation into unified training
objectives and evaluate their contributions through ablations.

### 3.2 Streaming Speech-Text Interleaving

Streaming ASR interleaves incoming speech chunks with incremental
transcriptions. For streaming TTS, we consider fixed-ratio interleaving
Du et al. (2024) and time-aligned interleaving Bai et al. (2025). We use
$`S_{i}`$ and $`T_{i}`$ to denote speech and text chunks. Throughout the
paper, $`m`$ denotes the speech-frame step size, $`n`$ the text step
size (tokens or words, depending on the format), and $`\ell`$ the
lookahead (speech frames for ASR and words for time-aligned TTS).

#### ASR

Given speech frames, we advance by $`m`$ frames per step with $`\ell`$
frames of lookahead. Each $`T_{i}`$ contains the words whose end times
fall in the $`i`$-th $`m`$-frame interval. The ASR sequence is

```math
[S_{1},T_{1},S_{2},T_{2},\ldots]. \tag{1}
```

Before end-of-utterance truncation,

```math
\displaystyle S_{1} \displaystyle=s_{1:m+\ell}, \tag{2}
```

```math
\displaystyle S_{i} \displaystyle=s_{(i-1)m+\ell+1:im+\ell},\quad i>1.
```

The first step consumes $`m+\ell`$ frames; each subsequent step adds
$`m`$ frames without repeating lookahead frames. When the audio ends,
the model emits the remaining text.

#### TTS

Given a text sequence, we alternate text chunks with generated speech
chunks. After the voice prompt, the TTS sequence is

```math
[T_{1},S_{1},T_{2},S_{2},\ldots]. \tag{3}
```

_Fixed-ratio._ Each step pairs $`n`$ text tokens with $`m`$ speech
frames. With $`t_{j}`$ denoting a text token, the chunks are

```math
\displaystyle T_{i} \displaystyle=t_{(i-1)n+1:in}, \tag{4}
```

```math
\displaystyle S_{i} \displaystyle=s_{(i-1)m+1:im}.
```

Chunk sizes are fixed independently of word timing. Once the text
reaches the end, speech generation continues until the utterance is
complete.

_Time-aligned._ Here, text chunks advance by $`n`$ words rather than
tokens, with $`\ell`$ additional words as lookahead. With $`w_{j}`$
denoting a word, the text window is

```math
T_{i}=w_{(i-1)n+1:in+\ell}. \tag{5}
```

$`S_{i}`$ is the corresponding speech chunk for the first $`n`$ words of
$`T_{i}`$. The remaining lookahead words provide context and reappear in
the next window. Alignment determines where each speech chunk ends
during training; the model predicts these boundaries at inference.
Appendix A.3 details alignment and boundary handling.

### 3.3 Unified Streaming Backbone

In this work, we combine representations from the TTS and ASR variants
of the VibeVoice family to form the speech input to our unified model.
Specifically, both VibeVoice-TTS (Peng et al., 2025) and
VibeVoice-ASR (Peng et al., 2026) use acoustic and semantic tokenizers
that encode 24 kHz speech into 7.5 latent frames per second. These low
frame-rate representations have been shown to effectively capture
acoustic and semantic information.

Using these combined speech representations, VibeVoice-ASR
autoregressively generates text during inference, while
VibeVoice-ASR-Streaming (Tu et al., 2026) extends it to streaming
recognition. VibeVoice-TTS uses next-token diffusion, combining an
autoregressive backbone with a diffusion head (Peng et al., 2025).
During inference, the backbone encodes the input text and preceding
speech into a hidden state that conditions the diffusion head to denoise
the next acoustic latent. To support both recognition and synthesis
within a single model, we adopt the VibeVoice-TTS backbone with separate
text and speech output heads for ASR and synthesis, respectively.

### 3.4 Two-Stage Training

Stage I adapts separate TTS and ASR models to the streaming formats.
These specialists provide task-specific performance references and
distillation targets for Stage II, which jointly trains Uni-Stream on
synthesis and recognition.

#### Stage I: Task-specific Streaming Adaptation.

We first fine-tune VibeVoice-TTS and VibeVoice-ASR-Streaming
independently to adapt them to our streaming interleaving formats while
limiting deviations from their pretrained capabilities. For TTS, we
train two separate models for each interleaving format: fixed-ratio and
time-aligned, using diffusion loss $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diff}}`$ on
target speech latents and cross-entropy
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{TTS\text{-}CE}}`$ at frame-continuation and
speech-ending positions. For ASR, we compute the cross-entropy loss
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ASR\text{-}CE}}`$ over transcript tokens,
text-chunk terminators, and the end-of-sequence token.

#### Stage II: Unified Synthesis and Recognition.

Let $`\theta_{\mathrm{T}}`$ and $`\theta_{\mathrm{A}}`$ denote the
adapted Stage I TTS and ASR specialists. We initialize Uni-Stream
$`\theta`$ from $`\theta_{\mathrm{T}}`$ and train on both tasks with the
supervised objective:

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diff}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{TTS\text{-}CE}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ASR\text{-}CE}}. \tag{6}
```

Each term is averaged over its target frames or token positions within
the corresponding task. Joint training can degrade the TTS capabilities
inherited from $`\theta_{\mathrm{T}}`$ as the model learns ASR. We
therefore formulate a synthesis anchor to preserve inherited TTS
capability, together with recognition distillation to facilitate ASR
learning. Both specialists, $`\theta_{\mathrm{T}}`$ and
$`\theta_{\mathrm{A}}`$, are frozen in the teacher forcing training.

#### Synthesis anchor.

The synthesis anchor works as the TTS teacher for providing acoustic
predictions and stopping decisions. Teacher model
$`\theta_{\mathrm{T}}`$ and student model $`\theta`$ receive the same
TTS sequence, but compute their hidden states independently.

For acoustic distillation, we match diffusion velocities at the noisy
latents and timesteps used for supervised training:

```math
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{vel}} \displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}|\,K\,d}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\big\|\Delta_{j}^{k}\big\|_{2}^{2}, \tag{7}
```

```math
\displaystyle\Delta_{j}^{k} \displaystyle=v_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{j}^{k},\tau_{j}^{k},\mathbf{h}^{\theta}_{j})
```

```math
\displaystyle-v_{\theta_{\mathrm{T}}}(\mathbf{z}_{j}^{k},\tau_{j}^{k},\mathbf{h}^{\theta_{\mathrm{T}}}_{j}),
```

where $`\mathcal{F}`$ indexes target speech frames, $`K`$ is the number
of noise samples per frame, and $`d`$ is the latent dimension. The head
$`v_{\theta}`$ predicts velocity from noisy latent
$`\mathbf{z}_{j}^{k}`$ at diffusion timestep $`\tau_{j}^{k}`$,
conditioned on hidden state $`\mathbf{h}_{j}^{\theta}`$ preceding frame
$`s_{j}`$.

For termination distillation, we match the two-token softmax
distribution $`q_{\theta}`$ over \<\|speech_pad\|\> (another frame) and
\<\|speech_end\|\> (end of a time-aligned chunk or a fixed-ratio
utterance). Over their supervised positions $`\mathcal{P}`$, we minimize

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stop}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{KL}\big(q_{\theta_{\mathrm{T}}}(\cdot\mid p)\,\big\|\,q_{\theta}(\cdot\mid p)\big). \tag{8}
```

#### Recognition distillation.

To facilitate ASR learning, we define a distillation loss that matches
the ASR teacher’s next-token predictions, supplementing the supervised
cross-entropy $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ASR\text{-}CE}}`$. At each
position, the teacher $`\theta_{\mathrm{A}}`$ and student $`\theta`$
receive the same speech context and reference text prefix and use
identical chunk and lookahead settings. Let $`r_{\theta}(\cdot\mid p)`$
denote the full-vocabulary next-token distribution and $`\mathcal{A}`$
the positions supervised by $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ASR\text{-}CE}}`$. We
minimize:

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ASR\text{-}KD}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{A}}\mathrm{KL}\big(r_{\theta_{\mathrm{A}}}(\cdot\mid p)\,\big\|\,r_{\theta}(\cdot\mid p)\big). \tag{9}
```

Therefore, the Stage II objective can be written as

```math
\displaystyle\mathcal{L} \displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{vel}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{vel}} \tag{10}
```

```math
\displaystyle+\lambda_{\mathrm{stop}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stop}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{ASR}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ASR\text{-}KD}},
```

where enabled distillation terms have unit weight. Section 4.3 compares
training with neither teacher, the TTS teacher alone, and both teachers.
The synthesis anchor applies to TTS samples and recognition distillation
to ASR samples. Only the unified model is used at inference.

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

#### Data preparation.

We use the English subset of Emilia (Emilia-EN) (He et al., 2024) for
both TTS and ASR, supplemented with GigaSpeech XL (Chen et al., 2021a)
and LibriSpeech train-960 (Panayotov et al., 2015) for ASR. To construct
streaming training sequences, our pipeline produces transcripts with
word-level timestamps. For Emilia-EN, considering its transcription is
generated by WhisperX Bain et al. (2023) and not necessary to be
perfect, we combine the original transcripts with two ASR hypotheses
through majority voting to refine the transcription, then merge
word-boundary estimates from three alignment sources. We filter
Emilia-EN utterances by transcript agreement, alignment quality, and
chunk-length constraints, retaining approximately 40k hours for training
with development and test speakers held out. For GigaSpeech and
LibriSpeech, we normalize the provided transcripts and obtain word
timestamps with Qwen3-ForcedAligner (Shi et al., 2026a). Word-end times
determine speech-segment boundaries for time-aligned TTS and
text-emission chunks for streaming ASR. Appendix A details annotation,
filtering, and sequence construction.

#### Training.

Stage I adapts VibeVoice-1.5B TTS and VibeVoice-ASR-Streaming-1.5B to
our streaming TTS and ASR formats, respectively, on a 10k hours of
speech subset from the full training set. Stage II jointly trains TTS
and ASR for 300k steps, using the full training set with 40k hours of
Emilia for TTS and a mixture of 28k hours of Emilia, 10k hours of
GigaSpeech, and two passes over the 960-hour LibriSpeech training set
for ASR, approximately balancing the two tasks by speech duration. Both
stages use AdamW with a peak learning rate of $`10^{-5}`$ and cosine
decay, keeping the speech tokenizers frozen.

#### Streaming settings.

Following Section 3.2, ASR uses $`m=4`$ speech frames per step with
$`\ell=3`$ lookahead frames. Time-aligned TTS uses $`n=2`$ words per
step with $`\ell=2`$ lookahead words, while fixed-ratio TTS pairs
$`n=5`$ text tokens with $`m=6`$ speech frames, following VibeVoice’s
streaming TTS implementation.¹¹ 1
[https://github.com/microsoft/VibeVoice/blob/main/vibevoice/modular/modeling_vibevoice_streaming_inference.py](https://github.com/microsoft/VibeVoice/blob/main/vibevoice/modular/modeling_vibevoice_streaming_inference.py)
TTS inference uses 10 diffusion steps with text and voice CFG scales of
3.0 and 4.0, respectively. ASR uses greedy decoding.

#### Evaluation and baselines.

We evaluate TTS on Seed-TTS-Eval English (Anastassiou et al., 2024),
reporting word error rate (WER) computed with Whisper-large-v3 (Radford
et al., 2023), speaker similarity (SIM) with WavLM Chen et al. (2022),
and UTMOS (Saeki et al., 2022). We evaluate ASR using corpus-level WER
on LibriSpeech test-clean and test-other. Streaming metrics include TTS
first-packet latency, ASR mean word-emission delay (excluding
computation), and real-time factor (RTF) for both tasks.

To our knowledge, no prior unified speech language model supports both
streaming TTS and ASR, we therefore compare with offline unified models,
HoliTok-Unite (Li et al., 2026) and Ming-UniAudio (Yan et al., 2025),
and with single-task streaming models: Nemotron Speech Streaming
(NVIDIA, 2026), VibeVoice-ASR-Streaming-7B (Tu et al., 2026), and Kyutai
DSM-ASR (Zeghidour et al., 2025) for ASR, and Kyutai DSM-TTS (Zeghidour
et al., 2025) and VibeVoice-Realtime-0.5B (Microsoft, 2025) for TTS.

Training and evaluation details are provided in Appendix B.

#### ASR Adaptation

Shown in Table 1, for ASR model, VibeVoice-ASR-Streaming-1.5B Tu et al.

effectively keeps a comparable WER on test-clean at 3.56%, while
increasing WER on test-other by 1.1%.

#### TTS Adaptation

After adaptation on approximately 10k hours of speech, both interleaving
formats improve speaker similarity over the reported VibeVoice-1.5B
baseline (Table 2). Time-aligned TTS has substantially lower WER than
fixed-ratio TTS (3.54% vs. 7.67%), with a small reduction in speaker
similarity and slightly higher first-packet latency. Therefore, we use
time-aligned TTS format for the following Uni-Stream experiments.

#### Analysis of the TTS Interleaving Format

We inspect generated speech to understand the WER gap between the
fixed-ratio and time-aligned formats. Of the 39 fixed-ratio outputs with
WER above 50%, 29 complete the target text but continue speaking, which
points to stopping behavior as a major source of errors. The formats
provide different stopping supervision: time-aligned training ends each
segment after its assigned words, whereas fixed-ratio training provides
an utterance-level stop signal, often long after the final text chunk.
These observations suggest that local stopping supervision contributes
to the lower WER of time-aligned TTS. Appendix B.4 provides additional
stopping statistics and confidence intervals.

$`\theta_{\mathrm{T}}`$ and jointly fine-tune it on TTS and ASR. We
compare three training configurations of Uni-Stream to examine how
synthesis anchoring and additional recognition distillation affect early
learning and final performance.

Using the objective in Eq. 10, the three configurations set
$`(\lambda_{\mathrm{vel}},\lambda_{\mathrm{stop}},\lambda_{\mathrm{ASR}})`$
to $`(0,0,0)`$ for SFT only, $`(1,1,0)`$ for the TTS teacher, and
$`(1,1,1)`$ for both teachers. All three share the same initialization
and training mixture.

#### Learning ASR while retaining TTS.

Table 3 reports results at 40k and 300k steps, demonstrating model
performance at an early training point and after one epoch full-size
training.

For TTS, distillation from the TTS teacher improves synthesis quality
over supervised fine-tuning alone at both checkpoints. At 40k steps,
adding the TTS teacher reduces TTS WER from 5.03% to 4.31%, increases
speaker similarity from 0.688 to 0.702, and raises UTMOS from 3.980 to
3.995. Adding the ASR teacher further reduces TTS WER and improves UTMOS
at this stage, with a slight decrease in speaker similarity. At 300k
steps, the configuration with only the TTS teacher achieves the best TTS
point estimates across all three metrics, although its speaker
similarity is nearly identical to that of the other configurations. Its
TTS WER is 3.20%, compared with 4.35% for SFT only and 3.40% for the
configuration with both teachers. These results support the
effectiveness of synthesis anchoring in retaining TTS capabilities
during joint training.

For ASR, the additional ASR teacher improves early recognition
performance. At 40k steps, the configuration with both teachers achieves
WERs of 6.13% and 15.65% on test-clean and test-other, respectively,
outperforming both SFT only and the configuration with only the TTS
teacher. At 300k steps, however, the three configurations achieve
similar recognition performance, with WERs ranging from 3.63% to 3.68%
on test-clean and from 10.56% to 10.74% on test-other. The ASR teacher
helps the model learn recognition early in training, but this advantage
largely diminishes with continued training.

These ablations show that synthesis anchoring supports TTS quality at
both checkpoints, while additional ASR distillation benefits early
recognition learning without a statistically significant improvement in
final ASR or TTS metrics at 300k steps (Appendix B.3). Subsequent
evaluations use Uni-Stream trained with the TTS teacher alone.

#### Streaming efficiency.

Joint training also preserves the streaming responsiveness established
in Stage I. Uni-Stream maintains a mean ASR word-emission delay of
0.66 s on both test sets and a median TTS first-packet latency of
129 ms, comparable to the specialists’ 0.66–0.67 s and 130 ms,
respectively. The ASR and TTS RTFs are 0.16 and 0.64, respectively,
enabling faster-than-real-time inference under our evaluation setup.
These results show that Uni-Stream supports both tasks within a single
model while maintaining streaming responsiveness comparable to that of
the Stage I specialists.

### 4.4 Uni-Stream Representation Analysis

Does joint training bring speech and text representations closer in the
unified model, and how does it change representations across tasks? We
probe Uni-Stream and its initialization $`\theta_{\mathrm{T}}`$ on ASR
and time-aligned TTS sequences, investigating the cross-modality and
cross-task text and speech distribution before and after unification
training. We use 64 Emilia development utterances from 26 speakers under
teacher forcing.

At each layer, we average hidden states over each word’s text tokens or
aligned speech frames. For each comparison, we center the two
task–modality groups separately and compute same-word cosine similarity
minus similarity to words from other utterances, averaging within and
then across utterances. This measures content-matched alignment after
removing group offsets. Bands show 95% paired speaker-bootstrap
confidence intervals.

Figure 2 reveals two patterns: a persistent modality gap and increasing
task specificity with depth. In panels (a, b), speech–text similarity
remains low for both ASR and TTS and does not increase after joint
training. Learning both directions of speech–text conversion therefore
does not bring matched speech and text representations closer under this
measure.

Panels (c, d) compare TTS and ASR representations within each modality.
Cross-task similarity is high in early layers (1–9), averaging 0.78 for
text and 0.96 for speech, but falls to 0.19 and 0.43 in upper layers
(20–28). To distinguish this depth-dependent pattern from changes
associated with joint training, we compare Uni-Stream with its
initialization at the same layers. Early-layer scores remain close to
the initialization, whereas upper-layer scores are lower by 0.42 for
text and 0.24 for speech. These results suggest that joint training
preserves similar early word representations across tasks while making
later representations more task-specific, even after removing average
group offsets.

### 4.5 Comparison among SLM Baselines

Table 4 compares Uni-Stream with offline unified models and
task-specific streaming systems. Baseline results are taken from the
corresponding papers, technical reports, or model cards. Among the
listed models, Uni-Stream uniquely supports streaming input for both
recognition and synthesis with a set of shared weights. Compared with
HoliTok-Unite, Uni-Stream achieves lower WER on both tasks while
supporting streaming operation, although it uses a larger backbone.
Ming-UniAudio achieves lower ASR and TTS WER, showing that unification
can support stronger task performance at a larger model scale. These
comparisons provide context rather than controlled evaluations, given
differences in architecture and training data.

Task-specific streaming systems still achieve better ASR performance,
with the recognition gap particularly pronounced on LibriSpeech
test-other. For TTS, Uni-Stream achieves speaker similarity comparable
to Kyutai DSM-TTS, but its higher WER indicates room to improve
intelligibility. The main result is therefore the combination of unified
and streaming capabilities while retaining performance close to the
Stage I specialists. The remaining gap to dedicated systems motivates
further improvements in recognition robustness and synthesis accuracy.

Uni-Stream acquires streaming TTS and ASR capabilities by adapting a
pretrained speech model with approximately 40k hours of training data
per task. Table 4 describe Stage II adaptation, not total training data,
since VibeVoice’s pretraining data are not reported. The results support
a practical route toward unified speech interfaces: extending pretrained
capabilities to both streaming directions within one model. Future work
will focus on narrowing the quality gap to specialized systems.

## 5 Conclusion

We presented Uni-Stream, which unifies streaming speech recognition and
synthesis in a shared decoder-only language model. With time-aligned
speech–text interleaving and two-stage training, Uni-Stream achieves
performance comparable to the adapted task-specific models on both
tasks. Our training ablations show that synthesis anchoring helps
preserve TTS quality, while additional recognition distillation improves
early ASR performance without a statistically significant final gain. We
will make the accompanying transcriptions and word-level timestamp
annotations public to support reproducibility and further research.
Representation probing reveals a persistent modality gap: speech and
text remain distinct under our similarity measure, while representations
within each modality become more task-dependent in later layers. These
findings show that unified streaming capabilities can coexist with
distinct modality and task representations. Future work will focus on
further improving recognition and synthesis quality.

## Limitations

While Uni-Stream presents the potential of a unified streaming speech
language model for speech synthesis and recognition, several limitations
and future directions remain for improving model performance,
experimenting with more training configurations, and extending the model
to support other languages.

#### Recognition and synthesis quality.

Although Uni-Stream performs comparably to the adapted task-specific
models, the strongest specialized systems still achieve better results
on LibriSpeech test-other and intelligibility in synthesized speech.
Future work will investigate whether stronger pretrained backbones and
different joint training strategies can improve results on these tasks
while maintaining unified streaming capabilities.

#### Streaming configuration search.

Computational constraints limit our exploration of streaming and
training configurations. We select chunk sizes, lookahead windows, and
the text-to-speech ratio for fixed-ratio interleaving empirically or
following prior work, which balances system latency and task-specific
performance without an exhaustive search. In the future, a broader
evaluation of these choices would help characterize the trade-offs
between task performance, system latency, and training cost.

#### English-only evaluation.

Our data preparation, training, and evaluation are limited to English.
The effectiveness of the proposed streaming formats and joint training
approach in other languages remains untested. Future work will extend
the data pipeline and model training to multilingual settings.

## Ethics Statement

#### Data and licensing.

We train on publicly available speech corpora (Emilia-EN, GigaSpeech XL,
and LibriSpeech train-960) and evaluate on LibriSpeech and
Seed-TTS-Eval, using each corpus under its license and terms of use. The
transcriptions and timestamps described in Appendix A are produced
automatically, without human annotation. We will publicly release these
derived transcriptions and annotations under the terms of the original
licenses; the release will not include audio. For Emilia, this is CC
BY-NC 4.0, which restricts use to non-commercial purposes. Emilia and
GigaSpeech consist of in-the-wild recordings that contain the voices of
real people and we use them only for research.

#### Potential misuse.

Uni-Stream performs zero-shot voice cloning: given a few seconds of a
speaker’s voice, it can synthesize arbitrary text in that voice. This
capability could be misused for impersonation, fraud, or disinformation,
and streaming synthesis makes real-time misuse, such as in phone calls,
easier. Streaming ASR could likewise facilitate unauthorized
transcription of private conversations. If we release model weights, we
will restrict them to research use and require that voice prompts be
used only with the speaker’s consent. We also encourage the use of
synthetic speech detection and audio watermarking alongside such
systems.

#### Evaluation.

All evaluations use automatic metrics; no human subjects or crowd
workers were involved.

## Appendix A Data Preparation Details

### A.1 Emilia-EN Annotation

The Emilia-EN annotations pair voted transcripts with merged word
boundaries. For GigaSpeech XL and LibriSpeech train-960, timestamps
correspond to the normalized provided transcripts. The annotation
release described in the main text contains these derived texts and
timestamps, without audio.

#### Transcript voting.

We combine the original Emilia transcript with hypotheses from
CrisperWhisper 2.0 (Wagner et al., 2026) and Nemotron-Streaming-ASR-0.6B
(Noroozi et al., 2024; Rekesh et al., 2023). Before voting, we normalize
comparison units so that numbers, abbreviations, and filler variants can
match equivalent spoken forms. We jointly align the three sequences with
a beam-pruned edit alignment (beam width 32) and apply ROVER-style
column-wise voting (Fiscus, 1997). Words supported by at least two
hypotheses are retained; singleton insertions are removed. Other columns
retain the entry with the most support and are marked disputed;
utterances are rejected if disputed columns exceed 5% of
output-producing columns. We prefer the Emilia surface form when it
matches the selected entry, and use Emilia to break ties. Finally, we
restore fillers detected only by CrisperWhisper and attach its
timestamps wherever its words match the voted transcript.

#### Word-boundary merging.

We combine CrisperWhisper timestamps with forced alignments of the voted
transcript from MMS-FA (Pratap et al., 2024) and
Qwen3-ForcedAligner-0.6B (Shi et al., 2026a). Available predictions are
averaged with weights proportional to inverse squared boundary-error
scales.

With three predictions, an outlier is identified when the other two
agree within 80 ms and its value differs from their mean by more than
80 ms. Rejection is shared across each word-end/next-word-start pair:
the aligner with the largest qualifying deviation on either boundary is
removed from both. Using the same surviving aligners and weights on both
boundaries preserves their order when the inputs are monotone.

#### Filtering.

We require monotone word spans, including a start no later than the end
of each word. The MMS-FA mean word score must be at least 0.30, and at
most 20% of words may have scores below 0.20; utterances without MMS
scores do not pass this gate. We also apply the sequence-length limits
below and split Emilia by speaker, keeping training and evaluation
speakers disjoint.

### A.2 ASR-only Corpora

GigaSpeech XL and LibriSpeech train-960 use their provided transcripts
without voting. We normalize capitalization while preserving spelled-out
numbers; for GigaSpeech, we also convert punctuation tags and discard
segments with non-speech tags. Qwen3-ForcedAligner-0.6B supplies one
span per word, and we retain successful alignments with monotone
boundaries. We then apply the ASR chunk-length limit below.

### A.3 Word Boundaries and Sequence Construction

For the streaming formats in Section 3.2, both tasks map word-end times
to latent frames using

```math
b_{k}=\left\lfloor\frac{\lfloor\hat{t}_{k,e}f_{s}\rfloor}{h}\right\rfloor, \tag{11}
```

where $`\hat{t}_{k,e}`$ is word $`k`$’s end time, $`f_{s}=24{,}000`$ Hz,
and the tokenizer hop is $`h=3{,}200`$ samples.

#### Time-aligned TTS.

For $`W`$ words and $`N`$ frames, we form nondecreasing cuts from
$`b_{k}`$, clipped to $`[0,N]`$, with the first cut at zero and the last
at $`N`$. Each word receives the frames between consecutive cuts, so
every frame is assigned once and the last word includes the remaining
audio tail. The speech for each segment consists of the frames assigned
to its new words; lookahead words supply text context only. We reject
utterances with any segment exceeding 32 frames ($`\approx`$4.3 s),
matching the inference limit.

#### ASR.

We clip end-frame indices to $`[0,N-1]`$, enforce nondecreasing order,
and assign word $`k`$ to zero-based chunk $`\lfloor b_{k}/m\rfloor`$. At
the end of an utterance, remaining words are emitted with the available
audio. We reject utterances with more than 32 text tokens in any
emission chunk.

## Appendix B Experimental Details

### B.1 Training Configuration

#### Optimization.

We train the backbone, modality connectors, and output heads while
keeping both speech tokenizers frozen, following the initialization and
data budgets in Section 4.1. AdamW uses
$`(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})=(0.9,0.99)`$, $`\epsilon=10^{-8}`$, weight decay
0.01, and gradient-norm clipping at 1.0. Linear warm-up precedes cosine
decay; the optimizer and schedule are reset at the start of Stage II.
Batches group examples of similar lengths under the per-GPU token
budgets in Table 6, including padding.

#### TTS conditioning.

Each training example uses a voice prompt from another utterance by the
same speaker, with requested duration sampled between 3 and 10 s. Text
conditioning and voice prompts are independently dropped with
probabilities 0.2 and 0.1. The diffusion objective uses four noise
samples per target frame. The supervised and distillation objectives and
their weights follow Sections 3.4 and 4.3.

### B.2 Evaluation Protocol

#### Quality metrics.

TTS evaluation uses all 1,088 Seed-TTS-Eval English utterances with
voice prompts capped at 10 s. TTS WER is averaged over utterances after
lowercasing and punctuation removal; ASR WER is pooled over each
LibriSpeech test set after Whisper English normalization. SIM and UTMOS
are averaged over utterances, with UTMOS computed using the UTMOS22
strong predictor. TTS stopping decisions are greedy, and ASR speech
inputs use posterior-mean latents; other decoding settings are given in
Section 4.1.

#### Latency and throughput.

TTS first-packet latency runs from the generation call to the first
decoded audio packet, including prompt encoding and prefix processing,
on an H100 GPU. We report the median over 100 utterances at batch size
one after warm-up. ASR word-emission delay is the consumed audio
duration at commitment minus the reference word-end time for correctly
recognized words; reference boundaries come from Qwen3-ForcedAligner.
This delay excludes computation time. RTF is averaged over utterances
and divides inference wall-clock time by generated audio duration for
TTS or input audio duration for ASR; ASR RTF is measured on test-clean.
