# Generating Realistic, Protocol-Compliant Maritime Radio Dialogues using Self-Instruct and Low-Rank Adaptation

Gürsel Akdeniz Emin Cagatay Nakilcioglu

###### Abstract

Very high frequency (VHF) radio miscommunication remains a major safety risk in maritime operations, with human factors accounting for over 58% of recorded incidents in Europe between 2014 and 2023. Despite decades of operational use, VHF radio communications are still prone to noise, interference, linguistic variability, and the absence of real-time transcription, making procedural errors both frequent and difficult to correct. Developing artificial intelligence-assisted systems to support real-time communication and decision-making requires a considerable amount of high-quality maritime data, yet operational, regulatory, and privacy constraints render such datasets scarce.

This study introduces a compliance-aware Self-Instruct methodology for generating realistic maritime radio dialogues that conform to the International Maritime Organization’s Standard Marine Communication Phrases (SMCP). Our approach integrates a 26-filter verification pipeline directly into the iterative generation loop to enforce entity information accuracy, hallucination detection, SMCP-compliance, logical consistency, and linguistic diversity. We employ Low-Rank Adaptation for parameter-efficient fine-tuning, reducing computational overhead during training and enabling efficient deployment of the resulting models on resource-constrained maritime systems.

To assess dataset quality, we introduce a four-metric evaluation framework combining automated and expert assessments: Format Accuracy, Information Accuracy, Uniqueness, and Logical Coherence. Experiments using publicly available vessel, coastal and Automatic Identification System (AIS) datasets demonstrate that the approach produces synthetically diverse, procedurally compliant, and operationally realistic dialogues. Although downstream applications such as automatic speech recognition and natural language processing are reserved for future work, the released code, datasets, and verification tools provide a reproducible foundation for artificial intelligence-assisted maritime safety and other safety-critical domains.

## 1 Introduction

The maritime industry underpins global commerce, facilitating over 80% of international trade with volumes reaching 12.292 million tons in 2023 and projected to grow at an average annual rate of 2.4% between 2025 and 2029 \[23, 24\]. However, this growth is accompanied by persistent safety challenges. Between 2014 and 2023, European authorities recorded 26,595 marine incidents resulting in 7,604 injuries and 650 fatalities, with collisions and flooding as the predominant incident types \[2\]. Critically, human factors accounted for 58.4% of these incidents as the primary cause, far outweighing system or equipment failures at 25.9% \[2\]. This disparity underscores that human-centered interventions, supported by intelligent decision-making systems, are essential for maritime safety.

VHF Radio communication remains the primary medium for ship-to-ship and ship-to-shore coordination, yet miscommunication during procedural tasks has emerged as a leading safety threat. Despite decades of use, VHF radio is still vulnerable to multiple degradation factors, including adverse weather conditions, intrinsic radio noise and static, vessel-generated interference, and linguistic challenges, including accents, dialects, and multilingual speakers \[9, 3\]. Moreover, radio communications are often not transcribed or logged in real time, forcing operators to rely on memory during high-stress situations. These vulnerabilities make procedural communication one of the most failure-prone components of maritime operations.

AI-assisted radio communication systems offer a promising avenue to mitigate these risks. Such systems combine multiple complementary capabilities: (1) Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) for real-time transcription of noisy VHF communications; (2) Procedural Guidance Systems that support operators in formulating SMCP-compliant communications by suggesting standardized phrases aligned with the current operational context; and (3) Communication Monitoring Assistance that alerts operators to potential procedural deviations, enabling them to self-correct before critical communications are sent. These capabilities are designed not to replace operator judgment, but to augment human expertise by reducing cognitive load, compensating for the high-stress conditions under which maritime personnel operate, and providing decision support for procedurally complex scenarios. Effective implementation and deployment of such human-in-the-loop systems requires substantial volumes of realistic maritime communication data that strictly adheres to the International Maritime Organization’s (IMO) Standard Marine Communication Phrases (SMCP), a standardized vocabulary of approximately 900 phrases designed to transcend linguistic and cultural barriers while ensuring unambiguous communication during emergencies \[15\].

Advancing AI-enabled maritime safety interventions requires high-quality, domain-specific maritime communication datasets for training and validation. However, maritime radio communication datasets are rarely public and extremely difficult to acquire due to operational sensitivity, regulatory constraints, and the episodic nature of maritime voyages \[19, 20 for the maritime domain in the field of vhf communication"), 7\]. This data scarcity fundamentally limits the development of downstream maritime AI applications and explains why prior maritime AI research has relied on limited, self-curated datasets or simulation-based approaches.

This scarcity motivates exploring synthetic data generation. Although rule-based template approaches can produce procedurally compliant dialogues, they lack contextual fluency, semantic diversity, and scenario coverage necessary for realistic AI training. Conversely, Large Language Models (LLMs) offer scalable, contextually authentic synthetic dialogue generation, enabling the creation of diverse maritime scenarios that reflect operational challenges. LLMs have demonstrated notable versatility across specialized domains \[22, 16, 8, 13, 28, 10, 12, 11\]. In the maritime context, LLMs can be adapted to generate large-scale synthetic radio dialogues for direct use or conversion to audio via text-to-speech (TTS) for ASR training.

However, generating useful synthetic maritime data presents two key challenges. The first is compliance: general-purpose LLMs often fail to adhere to the strict regulatory and procedural standards defined by SMCP. While SMCP’s standardized 900-phrase vocabulary ensures that communications are unambiguous and universally understood, general-purpose LLMs have no intrinsic knowledge of these constraints and frequently generate plausible-sounding but non-compliant utterances. The second is realism: LLMs frequently generate text that, while superficially plausible, lacks the operational authenticity, contextual coherence, domain-specific conventions, and real-world entities (vessel names, geographical coordinates, incident types etc.) necessary for realistic maritime scenarios. Training downstream AI systems on non-compliant or unrealistic synthetic data risks producing systems that fail during real-world emergencies, creating false confidence in operators and potentially exacerbating rather than mitigating human-factors incidents.

Several studies have explored LLM adaptation in specialized domains. Afzal et al. examined LLM adaptation for text summarization in science, health, and government contexts \[1\], while Wan et al. applied retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) to the Chinese legal domain \[25\]. More recently, Nguyen et al. trained LLaMA 3.1 models (8B and 70B) with maritime-specific data to develop domain-enriched maritime LLMs \[21\]. However, while RAG-based approaches excel in integrating static external knowledge, they typically do not embed compliance verification directly into the learning process, since their architectures decouple retrieval from model optimization \[17\]. For maritime radio communications, such decoupling risks generating radio chatters that are plausible in language but fail to meet strict SMCP compliance or reflect real operational scenarios.

Iterative self-improvement methods like Self-Instruct \[26\] are more suitable for bootstrapping domain knowledge through iterative generation and filtering without extensive manual annotation. By embedding regulatory compliance verification into the adaptation loop, compliance can become an intrinsic learned property rather than a post-hoc filtering step. To our knowledge, this dual requirement (compliance as an embedded constraint within iterative fine-tuning and operationally authentic realism) has been largely unexplored in the maritime AI literature.

We propose a compliance-aware Self-Instruct methodology that embeds regulatory verification into the iterative fine-tuning process. Unlike standard Self-Instruct, which relies on generic heuristic filtering, our approach integrates a novel 26-filter verification pipeline directly into the generation loop. This pipeline operates across multiple verification stages (entity information accuracy, hallucination detection, SMCP compliance, logical consistency and uniqueness) ensuring that only compliant, operationally authentic dialogues contribute to model training. The compliance constraints are thereby learned as intrinsic properties of the adapted model, rather than applied as external post-hoc filters. We combine this with parameter-efficient LoRA fine-tuning to enable deployment on resource-constrained maritime systems, which often operate in environments with limited computational capacity. Finally, we introduce a four-metric evaluation framework combining automated assessment (Format Accuracy, Information Accuracy, Uniqueness) and expert judgment (Logical Coherence), enabling rigorous quantification of synthetic data quality in terms of regulatory adherence, information fidelity, diversity, and real-world contextual realism.

Our validation strategy incorporates three components: (1) Public data sources including public AIS datasets as well as GeoNames and GSHHG databases to verify information accuracy and entity realism; (2) Expert maritime linguistic evaluation by domain specialists to assess logical coherence, procedural authenticity, and compliance with operational norms; and (3) Automated SMCP compliance verification against the official IMO SMCP handbook. All datasets rely exclusively on publicly available maritime sources. The complete dataset composition and preprocessing pipeline are detailed in Section 2.1.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

- 1.  Compliance-aware Self-Instruct methodology with embedded 26-filter verification, generalizable to other regulatory domains (legal, medical, aviation).
- 2.  A high-quality synthetic maritime dataset containing logically coherent, SMCP-compliant distress calls with real vessel identifiers (IMO & MMSI) and geographical data from public sources.
- 3.  A four-metric evaluation framework assessing synthetic data quality in terms of regulatory adherence, information fidelity, diversity, and operational realism.
- 4.  Open-source code, synthetic datasets, comprehensive filter specifications, and evaluation results enabling reproducibility and downstream applications.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 details the compliance-aware Self-Instruct procedure, the 26-filter verification pipeline, and LoRA fine-tuning strategy. Section 3 presents quantitative and qualitative results comparing the base LLM, fine-tuned model, and evaluation metrics across the synthetic dataset. Section 4 discusses the limitations of this work and outlines potential directions for future research. Finally, Section 5 provides a general conclusion and discusses the broader implications of this work. ## 2 Methodology

In this section, we detail our complete methodology, beginning with the open-source datasets and the base LLM used in the synthetic data generation process. We then provide a detailed explanation of our adaptation of the Self-Instruct method \[26\] to the domain of maritime distress calls and outline the subsequent LLM fine-tuning procedure. Finally, we introduce the evaluation framework that consists of four metrics designed to assess the Format Accuracy, Information Accuracy, Uniqueness, and Logical Coherency of the generated calls. These metrics are crucial for quantifying the effectiveness of our approach and are used to conduct a comparative performance analysis between the base and the fine-tuned models.

### 2.1 Datasets

To support the generation of maritime radio communication that incorporates real vessel and geographical data, we utilize four public datasets.

- 1.  Global Self-consistent, Hierarchical, High-Resolution Geography(GSHHG) Database \[27\]: This dataset provides shoreline data. The full-resolution L1 data is used in the scope of this work.
- 2.  Geonames \[14\]: GeoNames is a comprehensive geographical database containing over 25 million entries, including names, latitudes, longitudes, feature codes and country codes of geographical entities.
- 3.  Marine Cadastre \[4\]: This dataset contains AIS data from the US Coast Guard starting from 2009.
- 4.  Danish Maritime Authority AIS Dataset\[6\]: This dataset contains AIS data from the vicinity of Denmark, starting from the year 2006.

Vessel information, including names, call signs, vessel types and MMSI numbers, was extracted by combining AIS data from Marine Cadastre (from 01.01.2023 to 30.06.2024) and the Danish Maritime Authority AIS Dataset (data from 26.07.2024 to 14.02.2025). To prevent the over-representation of certain vessel types, we removed 5000 pleasure craft and 5000 sailing vessels from the Marine Cadastre, and ensured vessel type consistency during the combination process. Entries without a vessel name were removed, special characters were cleaned, and vessel types classified as ”UNKNOWN” or ”Other” were mapped to ”Motor Vessel.” The final vessel dataset contains 58855 vessels. We extracted names, latitudes, longitudes, feature codes, and country codes from Geonames, while also removing parentheses and brackets.

### 2.2 LLM used in the study

Llama 3.1 8B base model was selected for this study, as it provides a suitable balance between performance and model size \[10\].

### 2.3 Self Instruct for Maritime Distress Call Training Dataset Generation

Wang et al. developed the Self-Instruct method to create diverse datasets for fine-tuning large language models \[26\]. This approach uses an iterative bootstrapping process, where a model is prompted to generate synthetic instructions and corresponding input-output pairs from a small set of manually created seed instances of instructions and input-output pairs. The generated data is then filtered to remove low-quality or similar instances before being used to fine-tune the original model. This process is repeated until a sufficiently large and diverse fine-tuning dataset is created. The resulting model, finetuned with this self-generated data, shows a significant improvement in its ability to follow instructions.

We adapted the Self-Instruct method to generate a training dataset for maritime distress calls. The overall process is illustrated in Figure 1. We began by manually creating a seed instances, $`S`$, consisting of 10 seed instances for each 10 distress categories from the SMCP. Each instance $`S({I,X,C})`$ comprises an instruction $`I`$, a context $`X`$, and a maritime distress call chatter $`C`$. The instructions correspond to the SMCP distress call categories, which are detailed in Table 13. To ensure diversity, seed instances include 10 unique contexts and 10 unique chatters per category, for a total of 100 chatters. An example of a seed instance is provided in Table 14. The phrases that form the basis of these seed instances were derived from SMCP chapter A1. We intentionally omitted the category ”Abandoning Vessel” as it contains a single phrase. However, to promote diversity, we also incorporated suitable phrases from various categories into other categories.

Finally, the synthetic instances undergo multi-stage filtering to ensure dataset quality and realism. The filtering process validates instances across four dimensions: (1) entity information accuracy for verifying vessel identity, geographic coordinates, and vessel classifications; (2) hallucination detection for identifying and rejecting fabricated maritime identifiers such as MMSI numbers or call signs; (3) SMCP compliance confirming adherence to established maritime communication standards for structure, sequencing, and formatting; and (4) logical consistency for eliminating unrealistic patterns, duplications, and incoherent sequences. Additionally, we employ a Rouge-L ²²2Rouge-L is a token-level evaluation metric that captures word-order-aware similarity through longest common subsequence matching, making it effective for detecting near-duplicate texts. score threshold of 0.7 to assess the (5) uniqueness of the instance and prevent redundancy \[18\]. Only synthetic instances that successfully pass all 26 constituent filters are retained for the training dataset. A detailed taxonomy and explanation of each filter are provided in D.

The entire training dataset augmentation process can be represented with the following function:

```math
S^{\prime}=F(M(S_{1},S_{2},S_{3},S_{4},S_{5},G(\theta),\alpha),\beta),
```

where $`\{S_{1},...,S_{5}\}`$ are the 5 instances randomly sampled from the instance pool $`S`$. $`G(\theta)`$ represents the context generation function, parameterized by $`\theta`$. $`M(S_{n},G,\alpha)`$ is the LLM function parameterized by $`\alpha`$, which takes 5 examples from the instance pool $`S`$ and the context generated by the function $`G`$. $`F`$ is a filtering function, parameterized by $`\beta`$, that returns the synthetic instance generated by $`M`$ if it passes all filters applied by $`F`$, otherwise it returns null. We run this pipeline for each disaster category until 500 synthetic instances are generated per disaster category.

### 2.5 Evaluation of the Distress Calls Generated by the Fine-tuned Adapters

We propose an evaluation framework to quantify synthetic data quality across four key dimensions: Format Accuracy, Information Accuracy, Uniqueness, and Logical Coherence. While the first three metrics are assessed via automated scoring across the entire dataset of 100 dialogues per category, Logical Coherence requires manual annotation from a maritime domain expert. This qualitative assessment is performed on a randomly selected 10-dialogue subset per adapter to ensure reliable human-in-the-loop validation.

Format Accuracy and Information Accuracy are calculated as a weighted average of coefficients, assigned to specific filters within the Self-Instruct adaptation pipeline. Tables 3 and 4 detail the filters and coefficients for these metrics. Uniqueness is quantified using the highest Rouge-L score for each synthetic instance, while Logical Coherence scores (Table 5) are derived from domain expert ratings.

The formula of the Format Accuracy and Information Accuracy are as follows:

```math
Evaluation\;Score=\frac{F_{i}(S)\cdot k_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}M\cdot k_{i}}
```

where $`n`$ is the number of filters considered for the evaluation criteria, $`k_{i}`$ is the coefficient assigned to filter $`i`$, $`M`$ is a binary mask with value 0 if the value in the context related to the filter $`F_{i}`$ is null, and 1 otherwise, $`F_{i}`$ is the filtering function that returns 1 if the synthetic instance $`S`$ passes the filter $`i`$, and 0 otherwise.

For the Uniqueness criterion, formula is as follows:

```math
Uniqueness=\begin{cases}1-(Rouge-L(C,C^{\prime}))&\text{if }Rouge-L(C,C^{\prime})\leq 0.7\\
0&\text{if }Rouge-L(C,C^{\prime})>0.7\\
\end{cases}
```

where $`C`$ is the distress call in the synthetic instance $`S`$, $`C^{\prime}`$ is the most similar call to $`C`$ within the synthetic training dataset used to train the adapter. $`Rouge-L`$ is the function that calculates the Rouge-L score between $`C,C^{\prime}`$.

Logical Coherence is evaluated using the criteria and coefficients in Table 5. Logical Coherence evaluation formula is below:

```math
Logical\;Coherence\;Score=L_{i}(C)
```

where $`L`$ is a function that assigns a Logical Coherence score between 1 and 5 to the distress call $`C`$ in the synthetic instance $`S`$. Examples of Logical Coherence evaluation are in F. Rest of the evaluations can be found in the provided GitHub repository.

## 3 Results

This chapter presents the performance evaluation of the LLM in two configurations: a vanilla model and LoRA adapters. First, we establish a baseline by assessing the vanilla model’s output quality. We then demonstrate how the fine-tuned LoRA adapters, trained with our Self-Instruct adaptation, significantly outperform the baseline, validating our framework’s ability to generate reliable and compliant maritime distress calls.

### 3.1 Performance of the Vanilla LLM

Table 9 presents the average evaluation metrics for the vanilla LLM when prompted only with the context and it provides a baseline for comparison with the performance of the LoRA adapters, as shown in Table 10, which details the percentage of valid chatters generated during the Self-Instruct Adaptation pipeline.

Table 9 demonstrates that the vanilla model is unable to generate any valid chatters. Logical Coherence scores are consistently low, around 0.1, across all categories. The average scores are 0.613 for Format Accuracy, 0.740 for Information Accuracy, and 0.455 for Uniqueness. An example of a chatter generated by the vanilla model with a context-only prompt is provided in Table 18.

In contrast, Table 10 shows that the vanilla model is capable of generating valid chatters when provided with instructions and five example instances from the instance pool. In this setting, the model successfully generated 5,000 valid chatters out of 14,938 total attempts, resulting in a 33.47% success rate. The highest success rate in the ”Disabled, Adrift” category with a rate of 52.91% and the lowest in ”Fire, Explosion” with a rate of 24.41%. Despite using the synthetic context, the generated chatters often contain repetitive or irrelevant statements. In a safety-critical domain governed by SMCP, an unadapted general-purpose LLM is therefore insufficient for generating reliable, protocol-compliant maritime communication.

### 3.2 Performance of the LoRA Adapters

Table 11 displays the average evaluation metrics for the trained LoRA adapters, which are named after their respective categories. All adapters achieved Format Accuracy and Information Accuracy scores exceeding 90%. Uniqueness scores for all adapters were above 0.4, and Logical Coherence scores were higher than 0.7. The ”Grounding” adapter had the lowest Logical Coherence score at 0.7, while the highest was 0.9 in the ”Collision” category. Overall, 87% of the synthetic distress calls generated passed all filters. The ”Disabled, Adrift” adapter produced the highest percentage of valid chatters at 93%, while ”List, Danger of Capsizing” produced the lowest at 81%. An example of a valid chatter generated by the ”Fire, Explosion” LoRA adapter, along with its context, is shown in Table 19. GitHub repository contains all the chatters generated by all the trained adapters.

Table 12 shows the number of unique chatters created by each LoRA adapter. The success rate of the LoRA adapters is more than double that of the vanilla model when prompted with valid examples. This improvement is not limited to the number of valid chatters, but extends to all proposed evaluation metrics. Our results show that applying the Self-Instruct adaptation with Llama 3.1 8B increased the average Format Accuracy, Information Accuracy, and Logical Coherence by 60%, 28%, and 698%, respectively. This demonstrates that our Self-Instruct adaptation enables the LLM to effectively incorporate crucial information from the context and generate disaster calls that adheres more closely to the SMCP format. The significant increase in average Logical Coherence confirms that our methodology allows the LLM to generate substantially more logical chatters. Additionally, our method produced a slight increase in average Uniqueness.

Overall, the LoRA adapters outperform both the vanilla model prompted with only instructions and the vanilla model prompted with instructions and valid examples. These gains reflect stronger adherence to SMCP, better use of contextual information, and substantially improved internal consistency, all essential for dependable data generation in safety-critical maritime operations. This was achieved by training on a dataset of only 500 chatters per distress category, generated from just 10 seed instances, and training only about 8% of the total model parameters. This work demonstrates that the combination of the Self-Instruct method for maritime distress call generation and LoRA effectively overcomes the typical bottlenecks of high GPU resource and large labeled dataset requirements, enabling fine-tuning LLMs with minimal manual effort and low computational resources.

## 4 Limitations and Future Work

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. As with all LLM applications, the quality and realism of generated distress calls are influenced by the inherent biases within the LLM’s pretraining corpus. Additionally, the contextual fidelity of the generated chatters is limited by the scope of the datasets, namely GSHHS, Marine Cadastre, AIS Dataset and GeoNames datasets, which do not include operational descriptors such as Coast Guard station locations, vessel capacities, or cargo characteristics. Consequently, this limitation necessitated simplifying assumptions about incident distributions across water bodies, i.e. this work assumed that most incident types could occur on any body of water except for groundings. The initial human-created seed prompts also introduce a degree of subjectivity and potential biases that may influence the model’s learned representations.

Moreover, the present study experimented only with the LLaMA 3.1 8B architecture. While this model balances performance and computational efficiency, larger or multimodal variants may provide more coherent, context-aware generations. Although the 26-filter verification pipeline significantly reduces factual and procedural errors, edge-case inconsistencies and residual hallucinations persist. Finally, the scope of this research was restricted to distress calls/communications between vessels and Coast Guard stations, excluding broader operational exchanges such as navigational coordination or pilotage, and the manual evaluation of Logical Coherence score may be subject to the assessor’s domain knowledge and interpretation.

Future work will pursue several research and development directions to address these limitations. First, contextual expansion will include additional communication types (e.g., ship-to-ship, pilotage, and port operations) and regional variations, using dynamic maritime datasets to increase situational diversity. Another key direction is converting the generated dialogues into synthetic speech via TTS systems, forming multimodal datasets that assist ASR training under variable acoustic conditions. Hybrid adaptation strategies combining real and synthetic data will be investigated to further improve transferability and robustness. Finally, the textual data will serve as a foundation for developing domain-specific language models for ASR post-correction, anomaly detection, and semantic parsing. Collectively, these extensions will promote a more realistic, interoperable, and operationally deployable AI-assisted tools for safety-critical maritime operations.

## 5 Conclusion

This study introduced a compliance-aware Self-Instruct methodology for synthetic maritime communication generation, addressing the shortage of regulated domain data. By integrating a 26-filter verification pipeline that enforces SMCP adherence, semantic accuracy, and contextual plausibility, and by adopting LoRA-based parameter-efficient fine-tuning, the framework produces communications that reflect both procedural and linguistic realism. The accompanying multi-metric evaluation in terms of Format Accuracy, Information Accuracy, Uniqueness, and Logical Coherence, provides a transparent, reproducible basis for assessing dataset quality and model behavior.

In addition to synthetically generated datasets, this work also contributes a validated methodology for domain-constrained LLM adaptation. The generated dialogues can be paired with TTS models to create scalable, compliant audio-text corpora, offering a sustainable alternative to costly, privacy-restricted real-world data collection. Such resources are invaluable for training robust ASR systems, enhancing real-time communication monitoring, and supporting decision-making on ship bridges, or in Vessel Traffic Service (VTS) centers. Beyond the maritime domain, the compliance-aware generation and evaluation framework offers a transferable blueprint for other safety-critical contexts, such as aviation or industrial control.

By releasing the dataset, codebase, and verification tools, this study reinforces the principles of transparency, reproducibility, and responsible AI deployment. Future work will continue refining multimodal validation, extending contextual diversity, and integrating human-in-the-loop evaluation to bridge the gap between synthetic realism and operational reliability.

## Appendix A Context Generation

The generated context includes the following information:

- 1.  Vessel name: Name of the vessel that reports the distress. Must be stated in the distress call.
- 2.  Vessel MMSI: 9 digit unique MMSI number of the vessel. It can be randomly null.
- 3.  Vessel call sign: Unique call sign of the vessel. It can be randomly null.
- 4.  Vessel type: Type of the vessel. It can be randomly null or ”Motor Vessel”.
- 5.  Vessel coordinates: Vessel coordinates in degree-minutes format. Minutes or decimals of minutes can be randomly omitted. They are ensured to be on a sea or ocean with the usage of GSHHG dataset. For the case of ”Grounding”, coordinates are at most 1 nautical mile away from the nearest land.
- 6.  Closest place name: Name of the closest landmark from Geonames dataset to the vessel position.
- 7.  Distance to the closest place: Distance between the closest landmark and the vessel position in nautical miles.
- 8.  Closest place country: Country of the nearest landmark to the vessel position.
- 9.  Compass direction: Orientation of the vessel position relative to the closest place.
- 10. Nearest port: Name of the closest port from the Geonames dataset to the vessel position.
- 11. Distance to the nearest port: Distance between the vessel position and the closest port to it in nautical miles.
- 12. Nearest harbor: Name of the closest harbor from the Geonames dataset to the vessel position. It is null if harbor is further than 200 nautical miles to vessel position.
- 13. Distance to the nearest harbor: Distance between the vessel position and the closest harbor to it in nautical miles. It is null if the nearest harbor is null.
- 14. Digit by digit: A randomly assigned boolean value. Numbers can be occasionally expressed as digits in maritime radio calls. For example, the number 123 can be expressed as either ”one two three” or ”one hundred twenty-three”. If the value is true, numbers in the synthetic chatter are to be expressed as digits.
- 15. Can have cargo: A boolean value based on the vessel type. If it is true, vessel should not mention carrying any cargo or cargo hold in the chatter. True for vessel types ”Passenger Vessel”, ”Cargo Vessel” and ”Tanker”.
- 16. Closest water body: Name of the water body the vessel is located on. It can be a sea, ocean, cove, etc. It is null if the water body coordinates are 200 nautical miles to vessel position.
- 17. Distance to the closest water body: Distance between the vessel and the coordinate of the closest water body to it. It is null if the closest water body is null.
- 18. Collided vessel name: Name of the vessel that the vessel has collided with. Only present in the disaster category ”Collision”. It can be randomly null. In that case, collision happens with a non-vessel object.
- 19. Collided vessel type: Type of the vessel, with which the vessel has collided. Only present in the disaster category ”Collision”. It is null if ”Collided vessel name” is null.

## Appendix D Filtering

To ensure that the synthetic radio chatter is realistic and complying with the guidelines in the SMCP handbook, a set of filters is applied to the synthetic instance generated in the instance generation step(Section 2.3 covers this step). Filters and their functions are explained below:

- 1.  Parentheses: This filter checks if LLM generates any phrases in parentheses. Distress calls should not have any phrases in parentheses.
- 2.  Bracket: This filter checks if LLM generates any phrases in brackets. Distress calls should not have any phrases in brackets.
- 3.  Incomplete: This filter checks if the output of the LLM ends with a dot. If it does not end with a dot, it means that the distress call is incomplete. Distress call should not be incomplete.
- 4.  Wrong Category: This filter checks if the LLM output is a distress call in the disaster category specified in the instance generation prompt. For this purpose, this filter checks the existence of certain keywords in the synthetic chatter. The keywords searched for each category are specified in the table 16. In the category of Undesignated Distress, distress call passes this filter only if it does not contain any of the keywords from other categories. Vessel name, closest place name, nearest port and harbor from the context are excluded from this check in the call, since these information can have phrases from the keywords.

- 11. Collided Vessel Type: This filter checks if the collided vessel type from the context is used in the distress call. It should be used in the distress call if it is not null in the context. This filter is only applied in the collision category.
- 12. Hallucinated MMSI: This filter checks if any MMSI number was hallucinated by the LLM if vessel MMSI is null in the context. LLM should not generate any MMSI number if it is null in the context.
- 13. Hallucinated Call Sign: This filter checks if any call sign was hallucinated by the LLM if vessel call sign is null in the context. LLM should not generate any call sign if it is null in the context.
- 14. Unknown Information: LLM generates sometimes phrases, such as ”MMSI unknown” or ”call sign None” when the vessel states its information, even if these information are null in the context. MMSI and call sign should not be mentioned in the chatter if they are null in the context. This filter checks the existence of the phrases ”mmsi” and ”call sign” in the distress call if they are null in the context.
- 15. Mayday: Distress calls should start with the phrase ”Mayday, mayday, mayday”. This filter checks if this is true for the synthetic distress call.
- 16. Vessel Name Not After Mayday: This filter checks if the vessel name is stated in the sentence after the phrase ”Mayday, mayday, mayday”, since it should be stated after the mayday according to SMCP handbook.
- 17. Duplicate Sentences: This filter checks if a sentence longer than three words is occurs multiple times in the distress calls, since this is not realistic in the case of Coast Guard responding to the call immediately, which is the scope of this study.
- 18. Cargo Logic: This filter checks the existence of the word ”cargo” in the distress call. If ”can have cargo” is null in the context, the call should not contain the word ”cargo”.
- 19. Hallucinated Vessel Type: This filter checks if a vessel type other than the vessel type in the context was hallucinated in the distress call. In addition, this filter checks if the vessel type is expressed with the phrases ”We are a \[vessel type\]” or ”I am a \[vessel type\]”, since these phrases are not realistic.
- 20. Both Port and Harbor: If both the nearest port and harbor are not null in the context, LLM uses sometimes both of them in the distress call. This filter is applied if both port and harbor are used in the distress call. If both are present in the distress call, this filter rejects the instance. This filter is implemented under the assumption that using both the nearest port and harbor names is not a common practice.
- 21. No Coast Guard: As mentioned in the definition of the filter ”Duplicate Sentences”, Coast Guard responds to the distress call immediately. This filter checks if the Coast Guard responds to the distress call immediately in the synthetic chatter.
- 22. Digit by Digit: SMCP handbook states that numbers should be expressed in digits in maritime distress calls. However, this is not always the case in practice. If the ”digit_by_digit” value is true in the context, all the numbers in the call should be stated in digits. This filter checks the existence of any numbers which are not digits in the distress call.
- 23. Wrong Distance to the Closest Place: Stating the closest place is not mandatory according to the SMCP. However, if it is used and the distance of the vessel to this place is stated in the distress call, stated distance should match the distance in the context. This filter checks if this is true.
- 24. Wrong Distance to the Nearest Port: Usage of the nearest port in the distress call is optional. However, if it is used and the distance of the vessel to the nearest port is stated in the distress call, stated distance should match the distance in the context. This filter checks if this is true only if the nearest port is not null in the context.
- 25. Wrong Distance to the Nearest Harbor: Usage of the nearest harbor in the distress call is optional. However, if it is used and the distance of the vessel to this place is stated in the distress call, stated distance should match the distance in the context. This filter checks if this is true only if the nearest harbor is not null in the context.
- 26. Uniqueness: Even though 5 chatters are provided in the instance generation prompt to contribute to the diversity and prompt is constructed in a way to promote creativity, LLM tends to generate distress calls that are similar to at least one of the example distress calls. This filter makes sure that the Rouge-L Score \[18\] between the generated chatter and the chatters in the instance pool are below the threshold of 0.7. Rouge-L score is defined as:

  ```math
  P=\frac{L}{C},
  ```

  ```math
  R=\frac{L}{R},
  ```

  ```math
  Rouge-L=\frac{2\cdot P\cdot R}{P+R}
  ```

  where $`R`$ is the reference text, $`C`$ is the text to be compared and $`L`$ is the length of the longest sequence of common words between $`R`$ and $`C`$. The value of Rouge-L score is between 0 and 1. 1 means identical texts and 0 means that texts do not have any common word sequence.

Rest of the information from the context is not checked, since the usage of the rest of the context in the distress call is optional. Compass direction from the context (see A) is included in the evaluation of information accuracy described in section 2.5.

Synthetic distress calls that pass all these filters are added to the instance pool and these distress calls are considered to be valid distress calls. An example synthetic instance that was rejected in the filtering step is shown in table 17.
