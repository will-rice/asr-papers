# Introduction

<span acronym-label="AM" acronym-form="plural+short">AMs</span> for <span acronym-label="ASR" acronym-form="singular+short">ASR</span> traditionally make use of hand-designed feature extraction methods like log Mel filterbank features or Gammatone features . These techniques are motivated by insights into the properties of the human auditory system. However, their inherent information loss can lead to suboptimal performance due to the fixed design.

This issue can be addressed by neural . Since their parameters are learned during training, these can adapt to the need of <span acronym-label="AM" acronym-form="plural+short">AMs</span> and the loss of particularly helpful information may be avoided. Several works have been proposed in this direction, often using convolutional <span acronym-label="NN" acronym-form="plural+short">NNs</span> but also applying parametrized functions and other architectures .

Recently, the framework has received large attention. It is mainly known for its contrastive loss, which allows to pre-train a model on unlabeled audio data. While the context network with multiple blocks constitutes the major part of the architecture, the model also uses a convolutional feature encoder. This raises the question of how much this feature encoder contributes to the performance and whether it can be helpful for supervised <span acronym-label="ASR" acronym-form="singular+short">ASR</span> as well. Feature encoder and feature extractor are both common terms in the literature referring to the same part of the model and for consistency, we only use the term <span acronym-label="FE" acronym-form="singular+full">FE</span> here.

In this work, we show new insights into the which is not yet closely studied in the literature. Unlike previous work, we demonstrate that it can be used as a replacement for traditional feature extraction methods for a <span acronym-label="CTC" acronym-form="singular+short">CTC</span> model similar to <span acronym-label="SC" acronym-form="singular+short">SC</span> features. The results show that both are competitive on the LibriSpeech benchmark. We analyze the learned filters and show the differences between the characteristics of both neural . Finally, we demonstrate that the learned bandpass filters of the <span acronym-label="SC" acronym-form="singular+short">SC</span> encode the valuable information for the <span acronym-label="ASR" acronym-form="singular+short">ASR</span> system while the remaining wideband filters are largely ignored.

# Related work

This paper takes up the line of research that applies learnable feature extraction front-ends for <span acronym-label="ASR" acronym-form="singular+short">ASR</span> . In particular, different neural are compared to traditional feature extraction methods. We choose to investigate the supervised convolutional from , which was also studied in . Furthermore, the feature extractor of is used as a learnable front-end for supervised <span acronym-label="ASR" acronym-form="singular+short">ASR</span> training.

The framework has been established in a series of related papers . Besides modifications of the self-supervised training criteria – moving from the future time step prediction to masked time step prediction – and incorporating quantization modules, also the architecture has been revised. Instead of a fully convolutional architecture , a large stack of blocks is introduced . Also, the itself has been adjusted, where 5 layers, a group normalization with a single group and a nonlinearity are replaced by 7 layers with group normalization in the first layer and a GELU activation function . Additionally, layer normalization and a linear projection are added after the convolutional layers.

While most works analyzing the model focus on the part which operates at a higher level of abstraction , some papers study the as well. The authors of feed synthesized sine waves to a pre-trained model and observe that enough temporal detail is obtained and information about fundamental frequencies is represented in the model’s output. In particular, they claim the frequencies are distributed linearly over the Hertz scale, unlike perceptually motivated scales like the Mel scale or the Greenwood function for Gammatone features. Also, the learned representations of the are highly correlated with Mel spectrogram features, where the correlation is at a high plateau for layers 3 to 7 . This observation is supported by further experimental evidence .

Different settings for the are studied in , showing that the required computation can be significantly reduced while maintaining similar performance. In addition, a deeper variant with additional point-wise convolutional layers is proposed which outperforms its wider counterpart using higher inner dimensions. However, unlike our work this is always done in the whole framework including pre-training and fine-tuning and no insights into the learned representations are given.

### Supervised Convolutional Features

As an alternative comparable method for neural feature extraction, we use the <span acronym-label="SC" acronym-form="singular+short">SC</span> features . Similarly, a convolutional filterbank with learnable parameters is applied to the waveform. As in the case of Gammatones, a temporal integration is performed over each channel. However, in this case, multiple filters are used for temporal integration allowing for multi-resolutional processing. Additionally, these filters are learned during training. By default, we set the size of the first filterbank to 160, its stride to 10 and the number of channels to 150. The second convolutional filter applies 5 temporal integration filters with a size of 40 and strides of 16 each. Since the output of all 5 filters is stacked, we have a resulting feature dimension of 750. ## Acoustic Model

The <span acronym-label="AM" acronym-form="singular+short">AM</span> mainly consists of 3 VGG blocks for downsampling and 12 blocks. Its configuration mostly follows and . The VGG downsampling uses three 3x3-convolutional layers with 32, 64 and 64 channels, respectively. In total, a subsampling factor of 4 in the time dimension is achieved by the strided convolutions so that the operates on frames with a shift of 40 ms. The feature dimension is reduced by a factor of 2 due to max pooling and then multiplied by the number of channels in the last convolution resulting in a total increase by factor 32 in our case. After a linear projection to a 512-dimensional representation and dropout of 0.1, the 12 blocks with an inner dimension of 512, swapped convolution and multi-headed self-attention modules and 8 heads are applied.

## Dissecting the wav2vec 2.0 Feature Extractor

It is striking that the uses two orders of magnitude more parameters than the <span acronym-label="SC" acronym-form="singular+short">SC</span> architecture. To understand where uses them and how much they contribute to the <span acronym-label="FE" acronym-form="singular+short">FE</span>’s performance, we run it with different configurations. The results for different widths and depths are shown in . The kernel sizes and strides were chosen as (10, 3, 3, 3, 3, 2), (5, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2) for 6 layers, (10, 6, 3, 3, 3), (5, 4, 2, 2, 2) for 5 layers, (10, 6, 6, 3), (5, 4, 4, 2) for 4 layers, (20, 6, 6), (10, 4, 4) for 3 layers and (32, 20), (16, 10) for 2 layers. The results show that decreasing the inner dimension deteriorates the performance, however, a larger dimension does not improve over the 512-dim baseline. In contrast, no major effect can be observed regarding the number of layers. We also tried a variant with only one layer using kernel size 320 and stride 160 which did not converge. Removing the final projection with about 394k parameters has no significant impact.

The proposed in uses increasing inner dimensions and we use this approach in the last three lines of . The first layer uses a dimension of 64/128 and it is doubled after each layer with an odd index. Additionally, we add point-wise convolutional layers after all but the first layer using the same inner dimension as the preceding layer for the last row of the table. It can be observed that these configurations perform similarly to the 6-layer 512-dim baseline while reducing the training time by about 21%, 19% and 14%, respectively. ## Frequency Response of Learned Filters

To analyze the learned filters of the neural , we first plot the frequency response of the filters in the first layer which operates on the raw waveform. As the order of learned filters in a convolutional layer is arbitrary, we sort the filters by the peak value of the frequency response and by the upper and lower 3 db cutoff frequencies as second and third criteria. The plots are depicted in  (a)-(d), which share the same color range.

The well-known distribution of the Gammatone filters is given in  (a) as a reference.  (b) shows the frequency response of the learned <span acronym-label="SC" acronym-form="singular+short">SC</span> filters. In line with , bandpass filters with a non-linear distribution of center frequencies are learned. This confirms, that these filters are also preferred by more advanced neural back-ends like the self-attention-based which are superior in the exploitation of context information compared to simpler neural back-ends e.g. in . The distribution resembles the Gammatone filterbank and with higher center frequencies, the passband is also wider. However, the stopband attenuation is clearly weaker and there are also several filters with no clear passband and a rather uncharacteristic frequency response.

The distribution for the ’s first layer in  (c) does not entail this degree of resemblance. Again, bandpass filters are learned, however, they have a wider passband, their center frequencies are distributed rather linearly and the stopband attenuation is weak. In addition, about 14% of all filters have their peak at 0 kHz and another 10% at 8 kHz with varying bandwidths. Several filters exist with multiple passbands. Interestingly, the frequency response of the pre-trained in  (d) is very similar to  (c). This indicates that the properties learned in unsupervised pre-training and supervised <span acronym-label="CTC" acronym-form="singular+short">CTC</span> training are similar.

 (e) and (f) show the complete network’s response to single synthesized sine waves of different frequencies, similar to the approach in . Unlike suggested in , the distribution of center frequencies is not as linear anymore. Moreover, the ratio of peak to mean values is much lower than for the <span acronym-label="SC" acronym-form="singular+short">SC</span> features.

## Filter Masking

The observed uncharacteristic wideband filters from raise the question of whether their output is particularly helpful for the model in addition to the bandpass filters or if they are just not trained properly and ignored by the system. To answer this question, we masked a portion of the 150 filters in the <span acronym-label="SC" acronym-form="singular+short">SC</span> features’ first layer. The filter kernels are sorted by the peak-to-average ratio of their frequency response and the $`N`$ filters with the highest (sharp) and lowest (soft) ratio are replaced by zeros during recognition. Since there is no bias, the masked filters have a constant output of 0. In , we can observe that masking the first 20 soft filters has no effect on the <span acronym-label="WER" acronym-form="singular+short">WER</span> (absolute difference \<0.1%) and only after masking 50, so a third of all filters, the <span acronym-label="WER" acronym-form="singular+short">WER</span> degrades significantly (7.7%). For the sharp filters, a clear impairment can be observed already when masking only 5 filters. This demonstrates that the wideband filters, unlike the sharp ones, do not contain valuable information for the <span acronym-label="ASR" acronym-form="singular+short">ASR</span> system and are rather the result of a suboptimal training process. Future work should address this observation to allow better exploitation of the ’s potential.

# Conclusions

In this work, we utilize the as a replacement for traditional feature extraction methods in a <span acronym-label="CTC" acronym-form="singular+short">CTC</span> <span acronym-label="ASR" acronym-form="singular+short">ASR</span> model on LibriSpeech. It shows competitive performance and outperforms an alternative neural , namely the <span acronym-label="SC" acronym-form="singular+short">SC</span> features, slightly. We demonstrate that its width is more responsible for its capabilities than its depth and show that pre-training the only is not beneficial. Finally, we analyze the learned filters and highlight the differences between both neural . In particular, it is shown that the <span acronym-label="SC" acronym-form="singular+short">SC</span> ’s learned bandpass filters encode the valuable information for the <span acronym-label="ASR" acronym-form="singular+short">ASR</span> system while the remaining wideband filter information is largely ignored.
