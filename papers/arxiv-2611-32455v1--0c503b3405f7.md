# Phoneme-identity depth profiles do not reliably predict LoRA placement for dysarthric ASR

###### Abstract

Parameter-efficient adaptation of a speech foundation model to
dysarthric speech requires a rarely justified choice: which encoder
layers to adapt. A recently proposed strategy is to probe the encoder,
find where phonetic information becomes most decodable, and put the
adapters there. We test that inference across two ASR models (Qwen3-ASR,
Whisper-small) and two dysarthric corpora (Mandarin CDSD, English
UASpeech), holding recipe and seed discipline constant throughout, and
split and gate constant within each corpus.

The inference fails. Measured under one pipeline rather than inherited
from published figures, all four phoneme-error curves minimise between
83% and 92% of relative depth, each model’s minimising layer moving no
further between corpora than between probe seeds. The best adaptation
band is not similarly constant. None of three natural mappings from the
curve to a band — steepest improvement, lowest band-mean error, or knee
location — recovers the best tested configuration. We do not claim no
statistic of the curve could work, only that these three do not.

Placement still matters, but is model- and corpus-specific. On CDSD the
models disagree — Qwen3-ASR prefers its upper third (29.88% CER,
three-seed mean), Whisper-small all-block LoRA (53.56%) — while on
UASpeech neither favours the upper band. Retraining the CDSD sweep at
three seeds leaves upper-versus-all resolved ($`-`$1.31 pp, CI
\[$`-`$2.16, $`-`$0.25\]) but not upper-versus-middle ($`-`$0.83 pp, CI
\[$`-`$2.06, +1.86\]) — a gap of that size is within what a training
seed moves. The two models’ preferences differ as a directly tested
interaction ($`-`$4.71 pp, CI \[$`-`$6.17, $`-`$2.89\]). We designate
this the primary claim by its role — it is what our first contribution
asserts and the question the paper sets out to answer — and we record
that the designation was made after an earlier version nominated a
different contrast (§6.1.1). It resolves under micro CER, under
speaker-equal weighting and under an exact paired test alike (unadjusted
exact p = 0.031); Qwen3-ASR’s own preference holds under micro-averaging
only. A budget-matched control finds no statistically resolved benefit
from additional capacity for either placement, but does not establish
that capacity is irrelevant: at matched budget the placement contrast
itself is not resolvable at this sample size, and the resolved result is
the equal-rank one. On Qwen3-ASR × UASpeech, adapting near the input is
reliably worse than not adapting at all (+9.90 pp CER). We therefore
recommend choosing the band by a short adaptation sweep on the model and
corpus in hand, not by reading it off a probe.

## 1. Introduction

Automatic speech recognition has become usable for typical speech and
remains unusable for many people with dysarthria. The dominant remedy is
adaptation: take a large pre-trained model and fine-tune it, in whole or
in part, on whatever disordered speech is available. Because that data
is scarce and because per-speaker adaptation is increasingly the
deployment model, parameter-efficient methods — low-rank adapters (LoRA;
\[8\]) in particular — have become common. The dysarthric-ASR literature
bears this out: Jiang et al. \[11\] fine-tune Whisper with LoRA before
adding speaker prompts for Chinese disordered speech; Tan et al. \[26\]
use curriculum-scheduled AdaLoRA for low-resource dysarthric
recognition; Wagner et al. \[27\] report AdaLoRA outperforming both full
fine-tuning and standard LoRA for per-speaker personalisation; and Hu et
al. \[9\] route between mixture-of-experts speaker adapters on UASpeech.
What none of them settles is placement. Hu et al. \[9\] is the closest
to our question and still orthogonal to it: it selects _which expert_
adapts a given speaker, not _where in the encoder_ the adaptation sits.
Each inherits or searches an adapter configuration without measuring
where the relevant information sits, which makes a design question
unavoidable: where in the encoder should the adapter go?

The question invites an appeal to mechanism: if one can measure where in
an encoder’s depth the phonetic information relevant to the target
domain is formed, one ought to be able to place adapters there. Muthu et
al. \[18\] made exactly this argument for dysarthric ASR. Probing a
12-block Whisper-small encoder on Mandarin dysarthric speech, they found
that phoneme error rate drops steeply through the early-to-middle layers
and plateaus after layer 6, inferred that “these layers should be the
most productive targets for dysarthric adaptation”, and confirmed it: a
single adapter at layer 7 and the 5–8 band both land close to
full-encoder adaptation, while their upper band 9–12 is the weaker
contiguous group. (Their v2 reports 68.64 and 66.21 CER against 64.35
for all blocks, i.e. 6.67% and 2.89% relative. Their v1 abstract gave
3.5% and 2.48%, which did not follow from its own table; the v2 abstract
corrects this. We cite v2 throughout — it also revised the unadapted
baseline from 105.08 to 89.98 CER, which matters for §5.) That is a
clean result, and it invites a reading we think is unsafe: _dysarthric
adaptation belongs mid-encoder_.

The confound is that a layer index means different things in different
encoders. Depth, time resolution, training objective and what sits
downstream all vary across ASR model families. An encoder feeding a
Whisper-style text decoder is under different pressure at its top than
an encoder feeding a projector into a large language model. If the depth
at which phonetic information matures differs, the band where adaptation
is productive should differ with it — and layer-placement advice
inherited from the literature would be actively misleading.

That was our hypothesis, and the second half of it turned out to be
wrong. Placement advice _is_ model-specific on the Mandarin corpus:
swapping the encoder while holding everything else fixed changes which
band wins. It is not model-specific everywhere — running the same
contrast in all four cells of a model × corpus design shows the two
models agreeing on English isolated words (§6.8, §6.9), so the
difference is a property of one corpus rather than of the two
architectures. But the proposed mechanism — differing maturation depth —
does not survive measurement in either case. Measured under our own
pipeline rather than cited, Whisper-small’s phoneme-error minimum lands
at 83% of relative depth rather than the 50% the published curve
suggests, essentially where Qwen3-ASR’s sits, so the depth profile
cannot be what selects the band.

We test this along two orthogonal axes. First, hold the data constant
and change the encoder. We use the same corpus as Muthu et al. — the
Chinese Dysarthria Speech Database (CDSD; \[28\]) — and probe the
24-block audio encoder of Qwen3-ASR-1.7B \[25\], whose decoder is a
language model rather than a Whisper-style text decoder. Differences in
the depth profile are therefore attributable to the encoder rather than
to corpus, language or task — though not to any single architectural
factor, since the two systems differ simultaneously in depth, frame
stride, pre-training data and objective, scale and decoder type. Second,
hold the encoder constant and change the corpus. We repeat the identical
adaptation sweep on UASpeech \[12\], which differs from CDSD on every
axis available to us: English rather than Mandarin, 449 isolated words
rather than read news sentences, and clinical severity labels rather
than CER-derived pseudo-labels. Both depth probes are run on both
corpora (§5.5, §5.6), so the second axis tests the mechanism proposed to
explain the ordering and not only the prediction’s outcome — and finds
that the probe curves barely move while the placement outcome does.

Two things this paper is not. It is not a claim that one foundation
model is better than another for dysarthric speech: that comparison is
not run here, and the one published head-to-head we are aware of \[10\]
reaches the opposite conclusion for adaptability, on a single speaker
(§2). Nor is it a claim that probing-before-adapting is a new idea —
Pasad et al. \[21\], Beheshti et al. \[2\] and Muthu et al. \[18\] all
state some form of it. Our contribution is narrower: a controlled
demonstration that a LoRA placement result obtained on one dysarthric
ASR model cannot safely be transferred to another, and evidence that
three natural phoneme-PER heuristics fail to explain the placement
difference we observe.

        under our own pipeline rather than cited, Whisper-small’s
        phoneme-error minimum sits at 83% of relative depth and Qwen3-ASR’s

        _Adapt where PER improves most steeply_ is contradicted on
        Qwen3-ASR, where the steepest band is the worst target; _adapt where

        We withdraw that rule on the strength of our own measurement.
        Repeating both probes on UASpeech (§5.6) closes the alternative
        explanation that the curve is corpus-dependent: all four minima fall

        proportional bands identically, and each model’s argmin moves no
        further between corpora than between probe seeds within one.
3.  3.  A second-corpus replication that separates what generalises from
        what does not. Two contrasts hold with intervals excluding zero on
        _both_ corpora — upper outperforms lower, and middle outperforms
        lower. A third, upper over middle, resolves on UASpeech but not on
        CDSD once training seeds are resampled alongside speakers (§6.1.1),
        while pointing the same way in every run; we report it as
        directional. No contrast is resolved in opposite directions on the
        two corpora. upper-versus-all is the one that does not carry:
        UASpeech resolves it in neither direction, so it and the
        parameter-matched 2×2 built on it are reported as CDSD-only. The
        second corpus also settles a question the first could not: on CDSD
        no adaptation-versus-baseline contrast reached significance, whereas
        on UASpeech all four do, establishing that adaptation helps (up to
        $`-`$9.20 pp CER) and that on this model and corpus lower-band
        adaptation is reliably worse than not adapting at all (+9.90 pp
        CER). Three matched seeds per band put seed variance at 0.12–0.38
        pp, well inside the speaker-sampling uncertainty.
4.  4.  Two methodological results about layer probing itself, which explain
        how the discarded claim arose. Probe-readout sensitivity to training
        volume is depth-dependent and architecture-specific (at a fixed
        epoch budget, so volume and optimiser steps move together — §5.5):
        increasing probe training data 5.4× (6,200 → 33,617 utterances)
        improves Whisper-small’s deepest block by 9.15 pp, 1.87× its own
        stack mean, against 2.25 pp and 0.74× for Qwen3-ASR’s. A low-volume
        probe therefore manufactures an apparent late-stack difference
        between the two architectures — 6.56 against 1.85 pp of post-minimum
        rise — that shrinks to 2.07 against 1.82 at matched volume. And we
        cannot reproduce the mid-stack plateau reported for Whisper-small:
        probe configuration matches the prior work item by item, and the
        discrepancy survives both padding treatments and matched training
        volume. We can no longer rule out unadapted baseline competence as a
        contributor: the prior work’s v2 reports 89.98 CER against our
        corrected 77.57 (§5). We report the discrepancy as unexplained, with
        competence among the live candidates.
5.  5.  An architectural precondition for the method, reported as a finding
        rather than a gap. The Qwen3-ASR audio encoder emits one frame per
        ~77 ms against Whisper-small’s 20 ms, and against this corpus’s
        forced alignments (median phone 80 ms) 49.4% of phones are shorter
        than a single encoder frame, versus 1.3% at 20 ms. We therefore do
        not compare frame-level boundary F1 across the two encoders: that
        would confound representational information with quantisation
        imposed by the output grid. We do not claim the representation
        cannot encode sub-frame timing; what is limited is where a framewise
        probe can place a boundary.
6.  6.  A documented failure mode of shallow adaptation. Damage from
        adapting near the input is concentrated on acoustically atypical
        speakers, and the errors are consistent with language-prior takeover
        — a mechanistic hypothesis rather than a demonstrated causal
        pathway, since the decoder is frozen and adaptation changes its
        conditioning representation rather than any language-model weights:
        the adapted model emits fluent, corpus-typical text unrelated to the
        audio while the unadapted model transcribes correctly. We also
        report a negative result that bounds how far this generalises.

 

## 2. Related work

### 2.1 Layer-wise analysis of speech representations

Pasad et al. \[20\] established layer-wise analysis of self-supervised
speech encoders, showing an autoencoder-like behaviour in wav2vec 2.0 in
which middle layers carry the most linguistic content while top layers
move back toward the input. Their follow-up \[21\] states two things
this paper tests in a new setting: that layer-wise properties “evolve
across layers differently depending on the model”, and that layer-wise
analysis “provide\[s\] reliable guidance to choose layers of interest
for downstream tasks”. What has not been tested is whether they hold for
the choice of _adaptation_ depth in a supervised ASR foundation encoder
under pathological speech. Ashihara et al. \[1\] perform the analogous
cross-model-family comparison for self-supervised speech and speaker
models, and are the methodological precedent for holding data constant
while varying the encoder.

Two recent studies probe layer-wise structure on atypical speech. Yue et
al. \[33\] probe Whisper-Medium for dysarthria detection and severity
assessment, locating the most informative representations around layers
13–15 and finding that fine-tuning induces only modest layer-wise
change; Mojarad and Tang \[17\] probe wav2vec 2.0 and Whisper for
consonant-cluster reduction in African American English. Neither
connects the probe to adapter placement.

Mechanistic-interpretability work on ASR converges on the same region of
the model from a different direction. Glazer et al. \[4\] find
contextual bias emerging _within the encoder_ and overriding acoustic
evidence — closely related to the failure mode we document in §7.2;
Pluth et al. \[22\] report that causal steering of Whisper encoder
features “is more reliable for higher-level features than lower-level
ones”; and Ma et al. \[15\] describe a “delayed specialisation” in a
LoRA-adapted Whisper. All three are consistent with the direction of our
adaptation result and are not, individually, evidence for it.

### 2.2 Layer-wise probing tied to adapter placement

Muthu et al. \[18\] is the direct antecedent of this paper and the work
we contradict in part. They probe a Whisper-small encoder on CDSD under
three transcript-matched conditions, report a task-dependent hierarchy
across depth, and use it to motivate mid-band adapter placement, which
their sweep over single layers and the bands 1–4 / 5–8 / 9–12 / all then
confirms. Their setting is a broken-baseline regime: unadapted
Whisper-small reaches 89.98% CER on dysarthric CDSD and 64.35% after
full-encoder LoRA (their v2; v1 reported 105.08 and 68.85 respectively).
We reuse their probe designs, adapter configuration and band structure
unchanged, so that what differs between the two studies is the encoder
and not the protocol — and we re-ran their architecture rather than only
citing it: both the depth probe (§5.5) and the four-band sweep (§6.6,
§6.8) were repeated on Whisper-small under our own corpus, split, gate,
recipe and scoring, which converts the paper’s central comparison from
cross-paper to internally controlled. Their probe result does not
reproduce under our pipeline, for reasons we have been unable to
identify (§5.5), while their band result partially does.

### 2.3 Where to adapt, outside dysarthria — an established and contested question

The general proposition that the middle is not automatically the right
place to adapt is not novel, and the literature does not agree with
itself.

- •
  A U-shape. DAMA \[30\] studies depth-aware adaptation for multilingual
  ASR in low-resource languages and reports that early _and_ late layers
  are language-specific and need adaptation while intermediate layers
  retain shared structure, reaching an 80% reduction in trainable
  parameters at equal or better accuracy across 18 languages.
- •
  Layer-selective LoRA in ASR already exists. Han et al. \[6\] apply
  LoRA to an automatically selected subset of layers of a large ASR
  model for low-resource multilingual adaptation, establishing the
  general move — do not adapt every layer — in ASR specifically. It does
  not address which depth suits _pathological_ speech, nor tie the
  choice to a measured phonetic-maturity profile, but it removes
  “selective placement in ASR” from anything we can claim as novel.
- •
  Probing-guided selection. Beheshti et al. \[2\] rank layers with cheap
  probes _before any task-specific model is trained_, reporting that
  informative layers “cluster in depth zones rather than at uniquely
  optimal positions”, with “backbone-specific selections rather than a
  fixed layer recipe”. They select layers to _read from_ rather than to
  _adapt_, but this is the closest existing statement of the principle
  we test.
- •
  The contrary result. Lin et al. \[14\] searched layer-wise PEFT
  placement in self-supervised speech encoders and found it did not beat
  uniform insertion into all layers. Our finding that upper beats all on
  CDSD × Qwen3-ASR — at one third the parameter count (§6.1) — is a
  counterexample in a different model family, and the disagreement is
  itself evidence for this paper’s thesis.
- •
  Transfer from other modalities. Ogawa et al. \[19\] select LoRA layers
  by CKA similarity and halve trainable parameters at parity across
  encoder-only, decoder-only and multimodal models; Xu et al. \[31\]
  propose a theory-driven “Layer Card” diagnostic for placement in large
  language models.
- •
  Learning the layer choice instead of reading it off a probe.
  Gumbel-BEARD \[29\] replaces manual layer search with an end-to-end
  hard Gumbel-Softmax selector over Whisper encoder layers, trained
  against a BEST-RQ self-supervised objective, and reports that the
  selected depth varies by domain. It is the closest contemporary
  alternative to this paper’s practical recommendation: where we argue
  for a short sweep because a phoneme probe does not transfer, that work
  argues for learning the choice from the adaptation objective itself.
  Both share the premise the paper defends — that the useful depth is a
  property of the model and the task, not something readable from a
  generic diagnostic.
- •
  Encoder depth and LoRA in ASR. Kolluri et al. \[13\] prune Whisper
  encoder layers and combine pruning with LoRA, finding that LoRA partly
  compensates for removed encoder capacity via language-model priors,
  less well in low-resource settings — a close relative of the failure
  mode in §7.2.
- •
  Steering the encoder rather than the decoder. SALSA \[32\] reports
  that steering a speech encoder, “particularly the later layers, is
  more effective than steering the LLM backbone”.

### 2.4 Foundation-model choice for dysarthric speech

Huber et al. \[10\] compare Whisper-large-v3 against Qwen3-ASR-1.7B as
bases for dysarthric adaptation and conclude in favour of Whisper,
reporting 9.7% WER with all available adaptation data against 14–16% for
Qwen3-ASR fine-tuning and LoRA, and noting that LoRA underperformed full
fine-tuning; their stated hypothesis is that dysarthric speech is too
far from the base distribution for low-rank capacity to absorb. That
study is n = 1 speaker, with 92 h of read speech plus 8.8 h of
in-the-wild corrections from a single user, and it measures adaptability
under generous per-speaker data rather than representational structure
or adapter placement.

We record it because a reader will encounter it, and because it cuts
both ways. It is a reason not to phrase any part of this work as a claim
about which base model is better — we make no such claim. But it does
not undercut what we do claim: our between-band contrasts are internal
to one encoder and hold whatever the encoder’s absolute standing, and
our own result that no adapted variant differs significantly from the
unadapted model on the CDSD test set (§6.1) is if anything consistent
with their scepticism.

### 2.5 Corpus and tooling

CDSD \[28\] is the Mandarin dysarthric corpus used here and by Muthu et
al. Alignments are produced with the Montreal Forced Aligner \[16\]; the
phoneme-identity probe is a CTC probe \[5\]; the adapters are LoRA
\[8\].

 

## 3. Encoder geometry: a precondition, and a finding

Before any probe is defined, the encoder’s time resolution has to be
measured against the phenomenon being probed. This step is normally
silent, and on this architecture it decides which probes are admissible.

The Qwen3-ASR technical report \[25\] specifies 8× downsampling of
128-dimensional filterbank features, a nominal token rate of 12.5 Hz.
Measured directly across five CDSD utterances spanning 0.40–9.40 s, the
released encoder emits 12.5–13.2 frames per second, i.e. ~77 ms per
frame; we use the measured value throughout, and the nominal 80 ms would
make every figure below slightly more extreme. Whisper-small, the
encoder probed by Muthu et al., runs at 20 ms \[23\], so the Qwen3-ASR
encoder is roughly 4× coarser in time, and 2× deeper (24 blocks against 12) with d_model 1024 against 768. The encoder returns 25 hidden states:
index 0 is the post-convolutional encoder input and 1–24 are block
outputs, matching the layer-0-is-input convention of the prior work.
Hidden state 0 is already post-convolution, so no earlier tap exists at
a finer resolution.

Against the corpus’s forced alignments (21,251 phones, 2,640 utterances,
44 speakers):

Table 1. Phone-duration distribution in CDSD, and its relation to
encoder frame period.

| Statistic       | ms              |
| --------------- | --------------- |
| p5 / p10 / p25  | 30 / 30 / 30    |
| median          | 80              |
| p75 / p90 / p95 | 140 / 230 / 300 |
| mean            | 110.4           |

| Frame period                | Phones \< 1 frame | Phones \< 2 frames | Mean frames per phone |
| --------------------------- | ----------------- | ------------------ | --------------------- |
| 20 ms (Whisper-small)       | 1.3%              | 28.6%              | 5.52                  |
| 77 ms (Qwen3-ASR, measured) | 49.4%             | 77.8%              | 1.43                  |

Consequence. Frame-level phoneme-boundary detection — “is frame _t_ a
boundary?” — is not a measurement that can be made on this encoder. Half
of all phones are shorter than the sampling interval, so any F1 obtained
would characterise the frame grid, not the representation. This is a
different statement from the prior work’s finding that boundary
information stays weak at every layer for dysarthric speech; that
finding is about a representation, and we cannot test it here. Nor is
the figure sensitive to the 77-versus-80 ms question, since the corpus’s
median phone duration is itself 80 ms.

We report the constraint because it is the clearest instance of the
paper’s general point: a probing protocol is not architecture-portable,
and its low-level probes can silently stop being well-posed when the
encoder changes. Anyone porting a layer-probing study to a new encoder
should measure frame period against the target phenomenon’s duration
distribution first. This matters beyond methodology: dysarthria distorts
sub-phonemic timing, so an encoder at this resolution is structurally
unable to represent some of the phenomena clinicians care about. The
remaining probes are unaffected: phoneme identity via a CTC probe is
feasible (99.9% of utterances satisfy T $`\geq`$ L, median T/L = 3.47),
utterance-level difficulty needs no time resolution, and the
cross-condition and adaptation analyses are pooled.

 

## 4. Experimental setup

### 4.1 Corpus and integrity gate

The probing experiments of §5.1–§5.5 and the adaptation sweep of
§6.1–§6.4 use CDSD. The replication sweeps of §6.5 and §6.8 and the
replicated depth probes of §5.6 use UASpeech \[12\] under an otherwise
identical recipe; its corpus, split and scoring details are given in
§6.5, and the two protocol departures forced on the UASpeech probes in
§5.6.

CDSD \[28\]: 44 dysarthric Mandarin speakers reading sentence material
drawn from news text. The corpus ships two subset directories: the 1h
subset holds 55,851 utterances from all 44 speakers (35.06 h), the 10h
subset 92,730 utterances from 8 speakers (64.32 h). 6,103 (speaker,
utterance-id) pairs name one file in each subset, and in every one of
those cases the two files carry different transcripts. The prior work
reports ~55,850 utterances, which identifies the 1h subset, and we use
it for the same reason it is the right choice independently: it retains
all 44 speakers. On corpus size, the two subsets sum to 99.38 h against
the 133 h reported in the corpus publication; we have no account of the
difference, and all results here derive from the 1h subset only.

Utterance identifiers are keyed speaker/subset/utt_id throughout, and
every retained row’s alignment transcript is verified against its
manifest transcript, with mismatches dropped. Forced alignments were
produced with the Montreal Forced Aligner \[16\]; utterances whose phone
tier contains no real phones are excluded, as are those with no TextGrid
at all. Of the 55,851 utterances in the 1h subset, 8,164 (14.6%) are
removed — 7,720 for having no real phones and 444 for a missing TextGrid
— leaving 47,687.

What the gate removes, and whether it makes the evaluation easier.
Forced-alignment failure is not random in pathological speech, so an
integrity gate can quietly select an easier evaluation population. We
measured this rather than assuming it away.

| Stratum             | Excluded       | Rate                               |
| ------------------- | -------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| mild                | 323 / 3,211    | 10.1%                              |
| moderate            | 3,256 / 22,778 | 14.3%                              |
| severe              | 4,585 / 29,862 | 15.4%                              |
| kept utterances     | —              | mean duration 2.37 s (median 2.06) |
| excluded utterances | —              | mean duration 1.59 s (median 1.28) |

There is a severity gradient and it runs in the direction the concern
predicts — 10.1% of mild material is removed against 15.4% of severe —
so the retained set is modestly easier than the raw subset. The gradient
spans 5.3 pp across the full severity range, which we judge small
relative to the effects in §6 but do not dismiss. The dominant factor is
duration, not severity: excluded utterances are on average 0.78 s
shorter than retained ones, and 94.6% of exclusions are “no real
phones”, the signature of very short or effectively empty recordings.
Per-speaker exclusion rates range from 3.5% to 32.5%.

Because the gate is applied identically to every band and to the
unadapted baseline, it does not _differentially assign_ utterances by
condition, so it cannot manufacture a between-band difference out of
nothing. It does, however, change the population the contrasts are
estimated on, and could alter their magnitude if placement effects are
heterogeneous in the characteristic being selected on — which is not a
hypothetical concern here, since §7 argues that shallow adaptation harms
acoustically atypical speakers specifically, and the gate removes short
and somewhat more severe utterances at a higher rate. What the gate
certainly affects is the absolute CER level and any comparison against
work using a different gate — including the cross-paper Whisper-small
comparison, where the antecedent applied no equivalent filter. LoRA
training and CER scoring do not themselves require alignments, so
re-running the key CDSD conditions on the full valid-audio subset would
settle this; we have not done it, and flag it as an open sensitivity
check (§9).

Splits are speaker-disjoint: 31 train / 7 validation / 6 test speakers,
and the same partition is used for the probes and for the adaptation
sweep so that the two are commensurable. The probe dataset is 8,800
utterances (200 per speaker); the adaptation manifest is 47,687
utterances (33,617 train / 8,682 validation / 5,388 test), 14.6% smaller
than the prior work’s because it inherits the gate.

Tone units are derived from the transcript, not from the alignments. The
alignment phone labels retain tone letters, but T1 (high level) does not
occur once in 43,137 phone tokens — a data defect rather than a fact
about Mandarin, since T1 is roughly a fifth of Mandarin syllables.
Text-derived pinyin over the same transcripts gives the expected
distribution (T1 18.3% / T2 19.3% / T3 19.4% / T4 33.2% / neutral 9.9%),
so we follow the prior work exactly: pinyin initial/final/tone units
from text (tong2 → {t, ong, T2}), with alignments used only for segment
timing.

### 4.2 Model and layer indexing

The probed encoder is the AuT audio tower of Qwen3-ASR-1.7B \[25\], read
from the released Apache-2.0 checkpoint: 24 transformer blocks, d_model
1024, 16 attention heads, FFN width 4096, projected to 2048 for the
language-model decoder. Following the prior work, probe layer 0 is the
encoder input and layers 1–24 are block outputs; adaptation bands are
named by block index, 1-based, so upper = blocks 17–24, and probe tap 17
corresponds to block 17.

Loading this checkpoint under a current transformers release requires an
explicit key remapping and a sub-config rebuild; the naive path yields
zero key overlap and a randomly initialised model behind a warning
(§10).

### 4.3 Probes

We follow the prior work’s probe designs unchanged, so that the
comparison is about the encoder rather than about probe capacity.

- •
  Probe B — phoneme identity. A CTC probe \[5\]: layer normalisation →
  128-dim bottleneck → residual temporal convolution → linear
  classifier; dropout 0.3, blank bias 0.0, CTC loss, Adam lr 1e-3, batch
  256, 10 epochs, gradient clip 1.0; trained independently per layer on
  frame-level states. Metric is phoneme error rate, tone-sensitive and
  tone-agnostic, with model selection on tone-sensitive PER. Training
  uses 6,200 utterances (200 × 31 train speakers) and evaluation the
  1,200 utterances of the 6 unseen test speakers, so absolute PER is not
  comparable to the prior work’s ~39,000 utterances; only the shape
  across depth is. The same probe is run on UASpeech in §5.6 over
  ARPAbet phones from a pronunciation dictionary.
- •
  Probe C — base-model difficulty prediction. Named for what it
  measures: the labels are derived from the probed model’s own CER, so
  the probe asks where in its encoder this model’s eventual recognition
  failure becomes recoverable by the trained CTC readout — which carries
  a bottleneck and a temporal convolution and is therefore not a linear
  probe — not where an independently defined notion of difficulty or
  clinical severity lives. A linear softmax classifier over mean-pooled
  encoder states (cross-entropy, Adam lr 1e-3, batch 4096, 10 epochs),
  with speaker-disjoint 5-fold cross-validation — a random fold split
  would let the probe memorise speaker identity. Metric is macro-F1 over
  three difficulty tiers; chance is 0.333. Two deviations must be
  stated. First, difficulty labels come from decoding with the base
  model itself: having no stronger _uncontaminated_ Mandarin decoder, we
  define difficulty as “hard for a general-purpose ASR model”. Second,
  the tier construction is forced: the CER distribution has two large
  mass points (2,267 utterances at exactly 0.0 and 2,853 at exactly
  1.0), so plain percentile thresholds collapse the tiers to 2,943 /
  5,503 / 354. Binning at the natural breakpoints (high = CER $`\geq`$
  1.0, low = CER \< 0.2) gives 2,813 / 2,780 / 3,207 with well-separated
  tier means (0.024 / 0.531 / 1.061).
- •
  Probe A — phoneme boundary. Not run: at this output stride the
  frame-level formulation is not comparable to the 20 ms case without
  redefining the task (§3). This bounds what the _probe_ can measure at
  frame resolution; it is not a claim that sub-frame timing is absent
  from the representation, which a pooled or tolerance-based formulation
  could still test.

### 4.4 Cross-condition divergence, and an explicit design weakness

The prior work compares three transcript-matched conditions per
utterance: (d) original dysarthric speech, (r) speaker-conditioned
zero-shot TTS resynthesis of the same transcript, and (u) unconditioned
TTS. We reproduce the conditions but not the per-utterance matching. Our
synthetic pools were generated for severity-matched augmentation, which
has no reason to preserve who-said-what: of the 13,627
dysarthric-conditioned synthesis rows built on CDSD speakers, 93.0% use
a text that exists somewhere in CDSD but only 3.0% (411 rows) use a text
the conditioning speaker actually said. That is a pairing problem, not a
volume problem.

We therefore run a matched-distribution design — 37 speakers with
dysarthric-conditioned synthesis for (r), 39 healthy-conditioned
speakers for (u), all 44 real CDSD speakers for (d), with per-speaker
mean pooling over ~400 utterances each — validated against the 411
strictly paired rows as an exact-match consistency check. This is weaker
than a per-utterance match, which removes lexical confounds by
construction rather than in expectation. Divergence is cosine distance
(1 $`-`$ cos) between condition means at each layer. A second weakness:
condition (u) is conditioned on healthy control speakers from a
different corpus, so the d–u contrast mixes a speaker-set difference
with a condition difference. d–r is the clean contrast.

### 4.5 Adaptation sweep

LoRA \[8\] is applied to the encoder self-attention projections q_proj,
k_proj, v_proj, out_proj of the selected blocks, with everything else
frozen, including the entire decoder — the prior work’s configuration,
unchanged: r = 64, $`\alpha`$ = 128, dropout 0.1, AdamW at lr 3e-5,
effective batch 128, 6 epochs, cosine schedule, weight decay 0.03.
Adaptation starts from the unadapted base model; starting from an
existing Mandarin dysarthric fine-tune would have measured nothing.
Throughout, all means all-block LoRA — low-rank adapters on every
encoder block — not full-weight fine-tuning, and no experiment in this
paper updates base weights directly.

### 4.6 Evaluation and statistics

Adapters are merged and decoded through the model’s own packaged
inference path (§10) on the 5,388 held-out utterances of the 6 unseen
test speakers. Mandarin has no orthographic word boundaries, so the
scorer tokenises by character and the edit rate over those tokens IS the
character error rate; we therefore report CER only for Mandarin and do
not label a character-tokenised edit rate “WER.” Because six test
speakers is a small N, we report per-speaker CER, micro- and
macro-averages, paired speaker bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (B =
10,000) on every contrast, and a leave-one-speaker-out sensitivity
table. Every CDSD adaptation configuration was trained at three seeds
(42/43/44) and all CDSD intervals are speaker × seed (§6.1.1); the CDSD
_probe_ curves of §5.1 remain single-run (§9).

The curve’s knee — the depth beyond which PER stops improving — sits at
layer 21, inside the upper band. Muthu et al. report PER plateauing
after their layer 6 of 12, i.e. at 50% of relative depth; here it
continues to improve to $`\approx`$85–88%.

That contrast is between our measurement and their published figure, and
it does not survive measuring both ourselves. §5.5 places
Whisper-small’s minimum, under this paper’s own pipeline, at layer 10 of
12 — 83% of relative depth, not 50% — while at matched probe-training
volume the Qwen3-ASR minimum resolves to a flat plateau across layers
20–23 (a spread of 0.27 pp). Both probes reach their minima late in the
stack, so the apparent depth-profile difference in the published
comparison is not a property of the two architectures. Because the
argmin layer sits inside the noise band of a single-seed estimate, only
the _band_ is claimable in either architecture.

The prediction this licensed was documented before the adaptation sweep
ran. The prior work justifies mid-band placement by mid-encoder phoneme
maturation; on Qwen3-ASR that premise is false, so the productive band
should be the upper one rather than the proportional 9–16 analogue. §6
is that prediction’s test, and it is confirmed on Qwen3-ASR — but its
_rationale_ does not survive, since Whisper-small’s own knee also sits
in its upper band, where the same reasoning is wrong (§6.6).

A secondary observation, with a caveat that limits it. A Mandarin
dysarthric fine-tune of the same base model tracks base to within ~1 pp
for layers 0–12 and then diverges steadily (layer 16: 41.07 vs 37.80;
layer 20: 34.39 vs 25.67) — fine-tuning reshaped the top third of the
encoder and left the lower two thirds untouched. That fine-tune had,
however, seen 81.9% of the probe utterances and all six test speakers
during its own training. The layer localisation is informative; the
magnitude is not interpretable as generalisation. All headline claims
rest on the base model, which has never seen this corpus.

Tone. The tone-sensitive minus tone-agnostic gap is negative for layers
0–4 ($`-`$2.93 pp at layer 0), crosses over at layer 5, peaks at +3.5 to
+3.8 pp over layers 10–16, and narrows at the top. From layer 5 upward
this reproduces the prior work’s finding that requiring tone costs
accuracy. The early-layer inversion is a normalisation artefact: at
75–78% PER the probe is near chance, and stripping tone tokens shortens
the reference by roughly a third, so a similar error count is divided by
a smaller denominator.

All four peak at layer 11 of 24 (the r–u pair peaks at 15) and then fall
by 2.1–3.7× toward the output. Muthu et al. report divergence rising
with depth, and that rise is the evidence for their headline claim that
disordered speech affects high-level representations more than low-level
acoustic ones. On this encoder that claim does not hold as stated:
divergence is maximal at $`\approx`$46% of relative depth and the top
third of the encoder pulls the three conditions back together. The three
distributional analyses reproduce the primary subset’s shape and its
layer-11 peak, which is the condition under which the
matched-distribution design is admissible. Magnitudes are small (cosine
distance $`\leq`$ 0.12) and no confidence intervals are computed for any
curve in this table — a real limitation of this section (§9). r–u
divergence is an order of magnitude smaller than d–r or d–u at every
layer (peak 0.0116), so the two synthetic conditions resemble each other
far more than either resembles real dysarthric speech.

Joint reading with §5.1. PER keeps improving into the upper layers while
cross-condition divergence falls there, so the upper third of this
encoder abstracts away speaker identity and synthesis artefacts while
continuing to sharpen linguistic content — what one would expect of
layers feeding a projector into a language-model decoder, and which a
12-block encoder with half as many layers either lacks or never reaches.

### 5.3 Recognition difficulty is deepest-encoded — this one reproduces

Figure 1, lower panel, plots the difficulty probe across all 25 taps
with per-fold error bars. Macro-F1 rises monotonically with depth, from
0.421 at layer 0 to a maximum of 0.646 at layer 24 (chance = 0.333,

final layer), so the signal is a property of the architecture’s depth
profile rather than of adaptation.

### 5.5 The Whisper-small profile, measured here rather than cited

Everything above compares our Qwen3-ASR measurement against
Whisper-small results reported elsewhere. We therefore ran the
Whisper-small probe ourselves, on the same CDSD manifest, speaker split,
integrity gate, probe architecture, optimiser (Adam, lr 1e-3, batch 256,
10 epochs, gradient clipping 1.0, dropout 0.3), unit inventory and
model-selection criterion. Whisper-small exposes 13 taps (embedding
output plus 12 blocks), the analogue of Qwen3-ASR’s 25.

The minimum is at layer 10 of 12 — 83% of relative depth — and there is
no plateau after layer 6. Figure 2 plots both architectures against
_relative_ depth on one axis, which is the clearest statement of this
paper’s negative result.

We checked four explanations for the disagreement with the published
curve, and none accounts for it.

1.  1.  _Probe configuration._ Matched item by item against the prior work’s
        description: probe architecture, optimiser, batch size, epochs, unit
        inventory and 70/15/15 speaker-disjoint splits.
2.  2.  _Baseline competence — no longer ruled out, and the reason is
        instructive._ An earlier version of this section reported that our
        unadapted baseline agreed with the prior work’s to 1.5 pp. That
        comparison used the figures in v1 of their preprint, which we read
        on 13 August 2026. Their v2 of 15 August revised the results tables,
        and the baseline moved from 105.08 to 89.98 CER (all-block LoRA
        likewise from 68.85 to 64.35). We cite v2 throughout, and against it
        the agreement does not hold: our corrected baseline of 77.57 is 12.4
        pp better than theirs. We therefore cannot rule baseline competence
        out as a contributor to the probe-curve disagreement, and no longer
        claim to. It joins the list of live candidates rather than the list
        of eliminated ones.

    Two observations keep this informative rather than merely deflating.
    First, their revision runs in the same direction and for plausibly
    the same reason as ours: an unadapted Whisper baseline above 100%
    CER was corrected downward by ~15 pp, exactly as ours fell from
    103.57 to 77.57 once repetition control was applied (§6.6.1). Two
    groups independently retracting an inflated Whisper baseline on the
    same corpus is better evidence that the artefact is real and general
    than either correction alone. Second, this check never bore on the
    probe result anyway: the probe is a CTC readout trained on encoder
    activations and never runs the autoregressive decoder, so no probe
    result in this paper depends on the decode configuration — of ours
    or of theirs.

3.  3.  _Padding._ Whisper pads every input to a fixed 30 s / 1500-frame
        window, and the prior work truncates to 1500 frames where we slice
        to the ~133 real frames per utterance. A matched-pair extraction
        wrote both a padded and a sliced shard set over the _same_ 1,188
        utterances and the _same_ 7 taps, verified numerically so that
        padding was the only variable. Both arms minimise at layer 10.
4.  4.  _Probe training volume._ The prior work trains on ~39,000
        utterances; our first run used 6,200. At 33,617 the curve shifts
        down ~4.9 pp on average and the location of the late minimum is
        unchanged, though the magnitude of the post-minimum rise changes
        substantially (Table 7).

The layer-10 minimum therefore holds across four configurations spanning
a 28× range in sample size and opposite padding treatments (8,800
sliced; 1,188 sliced; 1,188 padded; 33,617 sliced). We report the
discrepancy as unexplained.

An unexpected result about padding belongs in the record. Padding did
not dilute the probe; it helped it substantially (87.89 → 53.54 PER at
the minimum in the matched-pair experiment). Whisper’s encoder does not
honour attention masks and attends across the whole 30 s window, so the
padded positions carry contextualised, smeared copies of the speech,
giving the CTC probe hundreds of informative positions to align ~15
units against instead of ~133. On this reading Whisper’s padded frames
are an information channel rather than noise — a genuine asymmetry with
the masked Qwen3-ASR arm (§9). We offer that as a hypothesis: the
experiment establishes that padding changes the probe’s readout
substantially and in which direction, not that the proposed
information-flow mechanism is the reason.

Probe-readout sensitivity to training volume is depth-dependent, and the
dependence differs by architecture. This is the result that explains how
the discarded depth-profile claim arose.

A confound we must state before interpreting it. Both volumes are
trained for the same 10 epochs, so the 33,617-utterance condition
receives roughly 5.4× as many optimiser updates as well as 5.4× as many
unique utterances. This experiment therefore identifies sensitivity to
training volume under a fixed-epoch recipe, not sensitivity to the
number of unique utterances at matched optimisation. A deeper
representation may need more optimiser steps for this CTC readout rather
than more independent data, and the small condition may be under-trained
rather than data-starved. Separating the two needs a step-matched
control — resampling the 6,200-utterance set to the larger step count,
and training the large set at the smaller step count — at a few
representative depths. We have not run it, so we describe this result as
volume sensitivity throughout and avoid “data efficiency” as a mechanism
claim (§9).

The consequence is cautionary. Report the probe curve at more than one
training volume, or do not compare late-stack shape across
architectures.

The two minima differ in shape, and this is measured rather than
asserted. Both probes were re-run at matched volume under three seeds
(0/1/2), with per-utterance error counts retained so that the paired
speaker × seed bootstrap used for the adaptation contrasts (B = 10,000,
contrasts formed within a seed) applies to the curves.

On Whisper-small, L10 $`-`$ L6 = $`-`$7.67 \[$`-`$11.92, $`-`$3.98\],
L11 $`-`$ L10 = +1.83 \[+0.49, +3.54\] and L12 $`-`$ L10 = +1.55
\[+0.07, +3.11\] — all three resolved. On Qwen3-ASR, L22 $`-`$ L16 =
$`-`$6.20 \[$`-`$9.34, $`-`$2.75\] and L22 $`-`$ L20 = $`-`$0.64
\[$`-`$1.29, $`-`$0.02\] resolve, but the terminal contrast L24 $`-`$
L22 = $`-`$0.15 \[$`-`$2.63, +2.43\] spans zero. Whisper-small rises
significantly past its minimum; Qwen3-ASR does not — its late stack is
flat in a way Whisper-small’s is not.

Judged against the raw per-layer seed standard deviations (mean 1.43 pp
on Whisper-small), the +1.83 pp step would look like noise. It is not:
pairing within a seed cancels that seed’s overall difficulty, which is
why the paired interval excludes zero where a marginal comparison would
not. Each claim also sits at the most precisely estimated point on its
curve (seed SD 0.128 pp at Whisper-small’s layer 10 against 2.75 pp at
its layer 2), while the speaker-bootstrap intervals on individual layers
are wide — restating on the probe curves what §6.5 found on the
adaptation sweeps: speaker sampling, not retraining, is the binding
source of uncertainty.

 

### 5.6 Both depth profiles, measured again on the second corpus

Everything in §5.1–§5.5 is a CDSD measurement, and the same fixed pair
of probe curves stands behind a placement _difference_ on CDSD and
placement _agreement_ on UASpeech. Either the phoneme-identity curve is
intrinsically non-predictive of the band, or it is corpus-dependent — in
which case placement might still be readable off a probe measured on the
corpus in hand. The second reading would leave this paper’s negative
result substantially weaker, and nothing in §5.1–§5.5 distinguishes the
two. We therefore ran Probe B on UASpeech — both models, every tap,
three seeds.

Two departures from the CDSD probe protocol bound what this subsection
may be used for.

1.  1.  Targets are canonical dictionary pronunciations, not forced
        alignments. No aligner is installed on the machine that holds
        UASpeech, and UASpeech is not redistributable, so it cannot be moved
        to compute where one is. Probe B is a CTC readout and requires the
        target _sequence_, not time-aligned boundaries, and UASpeech’s
        closed 449-word vocabulary makes a dictionary lookup exact. Targets
        are therefore CMU Pronouncing Dictionary pronunciations \[3\] —
        version 0.7b, 135,166 entries — with stress stripped, over a
        39-phone ARPAbet inventory. This makes the UASpeech probe target the
        canonical phone sequence implied by the word label, not the sequence
        the speaker actually produced. In a pathology study that distinction
        matters, because substitutions, deletions and distortions are part
        of the phenomenon being measured: on UASpeech the probe measures
        decodability of the _canonical_ phonological sequence, and its
        absolute PER is therefore not commensurable with CDSD’s
        alignment-derived targets. Only the shape across depth is compared.
        This also reconciles the two UASpeech test counts: the adaptation
        sweep scores all 3,798 held-out utterances, the probe 3,738 — the
        60-utterance difference is those whose word has no CMUdict
        pronunciation and therefore no phone target, which the probe must
        drop and the ASR sweep does not. Coverage is 11,222 of 11,402
        utterances (98.4%) — 10,942 direct hits and 280 recovered by
        compound decomposition (_backspace_ → _back_ + _space_) — with 180
        utterances across 10 word types dropped for having no pronunciation.
2.  2.  There is no integrity gate. The CDSD probe inherits the alignment
        gate of §4.1, which removes 14.6% of utterances and thereby also
        removes very short and effectively empty recordings. Nothing
        equivalent exists here, because there are no alignments to fail.

Absolute PER is therefore not comparable between the two corpora: the
unit inventories differ, one corpus is gated and the other is not, and
the material is isolated words against read sentences. Only the shape
across depth is comparable, and only shape is used below. Splits are the
same speaker-disjoint 8 train / 2 validation / 5 test partition used in
§6.5, giving 5,979 / 1,505 / 3,738 utterances; probe architecture,
optimiser, model-selection criterion and per-layer independence are
unchanged from §4.3; every tap of both models was trained three times
with seeds 0/1/2.

#### 6.1.1 These are three-seed numbers, and one earlier conclusion does not survive them

An earlier version of this section reported a single training run per
band, with intervals from a speaker-only bootstrap. That estimator
quantifies which six speakers happened to be held out; it is silent
about which random initialisation and batch order the adapter happened
to get. Since the two headline CDSD contrasts were around 1.5 pp, that
omission was load-bearing, and a reviewer was right to say so.

Each band was therefore retrained at seeds 42, 43 and 44 under an
otherwise identical recipe, and every CDSD interval in this paper now
comes from the speaker × seed estimator. One conclusion changed. upper
$`-`$ middle was previously reported as $`-`$1.52 with CI \[$`-`$2.27,
$`-`$0.54\], excluding zero; it is $`-`$0.83 with CI \[$`-`$2.06,
+1.86\], which includes zero. We no longer claim upper beats middle on
CDSD, only that it did so in all three seeds we ran.

The claim the paper’s first contribution rests on is unaffected. upper
$`-`$ all moves from $`-`$1.47 to $`-`$1.31 and stays resolved. The
parameter-matched 2×2 of §6.7 gives the same equal-rank pair as
$`-`$1.26, consistent in sign and magnitude; note that the 2×2’s own
*budget-matched* contrasts do not resolve (§6.7), so it corroborates
this contrast without independently establishing a placement effect. The
seed sensitivities of the two contrasts differ by more than a factor of
three — seed SD 0.16 for upper $`-`$ all against 0.59 for upper $`-`$
middle — so the contrast that has independent corroboration elsewhere in
the paper is also the one that is stable under retraining, and the
contrast that had none is the one that dissolves. We report that as a
caution about single-run band sweeps generally, ours included: a 1.5 pp
difference between two bands is within what a seed can move.

The upper \< all result contradicts Lin et al. \[14\], who concluded
that uniform insertion into all layers was not beaten in self-supervised
speech encoders. Here adapters confined to a third of the blocks beat
insertion into all of them, with a CI excluding zero, stable under
leave-one-out — a second instance of this paper’s thesis that whether
selective placement pays is itself encoder-dependent. This contrast
rests on CDSD alone; UASpeech does not resolve it in either direction,
so it, the 2×2 built on it, and the cross-model comparison of §6.6 are
all single-corpus.

### 6.5 Second corpus: the sweep replicated on English isolated words

on UASpeech \[12\], which differs from CDSD on every one of those axes:

parameter budget, LoRA configuration, integrity gates and decode path

to disturb.

worst of the four.

#### 6.6.1 The unadapted Whisper baseline is decode-sensitive, and we report both settings

Our first measurement of Whisper-small’s unadapted CDSD CER returned
103.57, and being above 100% invites reading it as evidence of
hallucination. That number was produced with greedy search and a flat
200-token generation budget on utterances averaging 2.4 s — ample room
for a repetition loop, and every looped character scores as an
insertion. Re-running every Whisper condition in this paper with
repetition control (no_repeat_ngram_size 3, beam 5) and a
duration-scaled token cap gives the figures reported throughout. A flat
small cap is not the remedy either: CDSD reaches 18.3 s and UASpeech
19.5 s, so a fixed budget tuned on short utterances would trade an
insertion artefact for a deletion one.

The correction is strongly non-uniform, and in the direction the
diagnosis predicts: the unadapted base gains 26.01 pp while the adapted
bands gain between 1.85 and 4.63 pp, because an adapted model already
emits well-formed output and has little looping to suppress. No
placement conclusion in this paper changes, on either corpus; what
changes is the size of every gap involving an unadapted or weakly
adapted condition. Two verdicts do move, and both are reported at their
corrected values: all $`-`$ middle here (was resolved, now spans zero)
and lower $`-`$ base on UASpeech CER (§6.8, was spanning zero, now
resolved). We report this rather than silently restating the numbers
because the same decode default is used widely, and a baseline inflated
this way invites the conclusion that a model is broken when it is partly
the harness that is.

 

### 6.7 Placement or capacity? A parameter-matched 2×2

Every upper versus all contrast reported so far varies two things at
once: upper places adapters in 8 of 24 blocks and all in all 24, but
both use r = 64, so all also carries three times the trainable capacity.
That leaves open the reading that all wins nothing because it is
over-parameterised for 33,617 utterances. This subsection separates the
two factors on CDSD × Qwen3-ASR, the cell in which upper beats all.

Design. Placement × capacity, fully crossed, with rank chosen per cell
to equalise the trainable-parameter count along the capacity axis. Each
adapted block contributes four LoRA modules of r × 2 × 1024 parameters,
so a configuration’s budget is proportional to (blocks × rank):

- •
  High capacity, exact match. 8 blocks × r = 192 and 24 blocks × r = 64
  both give 1,536 rank-blocks and 12,582,912 trainable parameters — an
  exact match, not an approximate one.
- •
  Low capacity, 98.4% match. 8 blocks × r = 64 gives 4,194,304
  parameters; 24 blocks × r = 21 gives 4,128,768. Integer rank makes an
  exact match impossible at 24 blocks.

All four cells were trained at seeds 42, 43 and 44 with $`\alpha`$ = 2r
throughout, with manifest, split, gate, optimiser, schedule, epoch
count, merge procedure and decode path identical to §6.1 and to each
other. Two design points matter and are stated because an earlier
version of this section got the second one wrong.

_Seeds._ An earlier version ran this 2×2 at seed 42 only. Since its
headline margin was about 1.5 pp — inside what a seed moves on this
corpus (§6.1.1) — every cell was retrained at three seeds and every
interval below is a speaker × seed bootstrap.

_Scaling._ An earlier version left LoRA $`\alpha`$ at the default 128
for every rank, so effective scaling $`\alpha`$/r was not matched: 0.667
for upper r=192 against 2.000 for all r=64 in the high-capacity pair,
and 2.000 against 6.095 in the low-capacity one. The arms therefore
differed in three respects at once — where the capacity sits, how
concentrated it is per block, and how strongly each update is scaled.
Here $`\alpha`$ = 2r in all four cells, which removes the scaling term
and leaves placement and per-block rank concentration as the only
differences.

total trainable parameter count is not the explanation for the upper
advantage — upper at 4.19 M is not worse than all at 12.58 M, and added
capacity does not help either placement — but at matched budget the
placement contrast itself is not resolvable at this sample size. The
resolved form of the placement claim is the equal-rank one in §6.1. Both
are CDSD × Qwen3-ASR results, untested in the other three cells (§6.9).

### 6.9.1 Do the two models actually differ? A direct interaction test

§6.6 reports upper $`-`$ all resolving negative on Qwen3-ASR and
positive on Whisper-small, and the paper’s first contribution is that
the two models _differ_. Two separately-resolved intervals of opposite
sign are not a test of that difference, so we test it directly. Both
models were evaluated on the same six held-out speakers, which lets the
difference of differences

> $`\operatorname{DoD}={(\texttt{upper}-\texttt{all})}_{\text{Qwen3-ASR}}-{(\texttt{upper}-\texttt{all})}_{\text{Whisper-small}}`$

be formed inside a single bootstrap replicate: speakers are resampled
jointly, a seed is drawn per model, and speaker difficulty cancels on
both sides.

### 6.10 How much of this depends on the estimator? Micro, macro, and an exact test

Every interval reported so far comes from a percentile bootstrap over
six CDSD or five UASpeech speakers. At that few clusters, percentile
tail coverage is not guaranteed, and the paper’s conclusions should not
rest on one estimator’s behaviour in its least reliable regime. Two
checks were therefore run on every headline contrast.

The first changes the estimand. Our bootstrap pools edit counts across
resampled speakers (micro CER), which weights a speaker by how much
reference text they contribute. The alternative weights speakers equally
(macro CER). Both are defensible: micro is the field-standard ASR
metric, macro is the quantity a claim about _generalising across
speakers_ implicitly refers to.

The second removes the assumption of a distributional _shape_, though
not every assumption. With n $`\leq`$ 6 the paired randomisation
distribution can be enumerated exactly — 2⁶ = 64 sign assignments on
CDSD, 2⁵ = 32 on UASpeech — so we report an exact two-sided sign-flip
p-value alongside the intervals. What it assumes in place of a
parametric family is sign symmetry under the null: that each speaker’s
paired difference is exchangeable with its own negation, which is the
null of no placement effect for that speaker. It is distribution-free,
not assumption-free. All exact p-values reported here are unadjusted,
and no contrast survives Holm correction over the three-hypothesis
family (floor 0.094), which is stated wherever they appear.

Table 26. The three estimators on the paper’s principal contrasts.

| Cell             | Contrast           | Micro                | Macro                | Exact p |
| ---------------- | ------------------ | -------------------- | -------------------- | ------- |
| Whisper × CDSD   | all $`-`$ upper    | $`-`$3.40 resolved   | $`-`$3.63 resolved   | 0.031   |
| Qwen3-ASR × CDSD | upper $`-`$ all    | $`-`$1.31 resolved   | $`-`$0.98 spans zero | 0.156   |
| Qwen3-ASR × CDSD | upper $`-`$ lower  | $`-`$5.64 resolved   | $`-`$5.32 resolved   | 0.031   |
| Qwen3-ASR × CDSD | upper $`-`$ middle | $`-`$0.83 spans zero | $`-`$0.63 spans zero | 0.438   |

Three of these four agree across all estimators. One does not, and it is
the paper’s headline placement contrast. upper $`-`$ all on Qwen3-ASR ×
CDSD resolves under micro CER and does not under speaker-equal
weighting; the exact test agrees with the macro result. The reason is
visible in the per-speaker differences: five of six speakers favour
upper, CDSD_03 favours all by 1.12 pp, and CDSD_03 contributes little
enough reference text that micro-averaging down-weights it.

We therefore state the claim with its dependency attached. Qwen3-ASR
prefers upper over all on CDSD _under the standard micro-averaged
metric_; that preference is not robust to weighting speakers equally,
and with six speakers and one dissenting we do not claim it as
established in the speaker-general sense. What is unaffected is the
_other_ half of the cross-model comparison: Whisper-small’s preference
for all on the same corpus resolves under every estimator, unanimously
across speakers.

A note on multiplicity, because the obvious correction is uninformative
here. Nominating a primary family of three hypotheses and applying Holm
to the exact p-values returns nothing significant — including the
unanimous, 5.6 pp upper $`-`$ lower contrast. That is a structural
artefact rather than a finding: with six speakers the smallest
attainable exact p is 2/2⁶ = 0.031, so Holm over three hypotheses has a
floor of 0.094 and cannot reach 0.05 before any data is seen. We report
the exact p-values and this floor rather than a corrected verdict that
is determined by the design instead of the measurements. The honest
reading is that six speakers is the binding constraint on this study
(§9, Limitation 2), not that the effects are absent.

 

## 7. A failure mode of shallow adaptation

### 7.1 The pattern

Two speakers account for lower’s aggregate damage, and four of six
actually improve slightly under it:

- •
  CDSD_08: 39.42 → 51.42 (upper), 48.90 (middle) → 59.06 (lower),
  three-seed means (Table 11). Every variant makes this speaker worse,
  and lower by far the worst, but the deterioration is not monotonic in
  depth: middle sits below upper here.
- •
  CDSD_38: 16.87 → 13.50 (upper), 15.94 (middle), 14.06 (all) → 25.26
  (lower), three-seed means (Table 11). A _new_ failure: shallow
  adaptation breaks a speaker that deep adaptation handles well.

This suggests that perturbing the blocks closest to the signal disrupts
exactly the low-level representation that acoustically atypical speakers
depend on, whereas the upper blocks can be re-aimed without destroying
the acoustic front end. Stated at the strength the data supports:
susceptibility to shallow adaptation is heterogeneous across speakers,
and the two conspicuous failures here are the acoustically atypical
ones. With two failing speakers, and the most severe of them the sole
occupant of its demographic cell, this is hypothesis generation — the
cohort is far too small to identify which acoustic or demographic
properties predict susceptibility, and we do not claim to have isolated
one.

The last hypothesis is a stock newswire opening formula. CDSD is read
news text, so six epochs of adaptation build a strong prior over news
phrasing; when the adapted acoustic front end fits a speaker poorly, the
decoder falls back on that prior and emits plausible text unrelated to
the audio. This is a qualitatively different degradation from a higher
error rate: confident, fluent and wrong. The mechanism is not peculiar
to this model — Glazer et al. \[4\] locate contextual bias arising
_within_ the ASR encoder and overriding acoustic evidence, and Kolluri
et al. \[13\] find LoRA compensating for reduced encoder capacity by
leaning on language-model priors.

It is a substitution failure, not an over-generation one, and the
distinction is measurable. Because “hallucination” is often diagnosed by
output length, we checked: the ratio \|hyp\|/\|ref\| stays within
0.92–1.03 for every speaker under every band, including lower and
including CDSD_08. (An earlier version of this sentence also reported a
corpus-level insertion rate of 0.000. That figure cannot be
reconstructed from the released aggregates — which carry edit totals
rather than insertion, deletion and substitution counts — and it is not
consistent with a length ratio above 1.0, so it is withdrawn. The
length-ratio bound is the claim the data supports.) The adapted model
emits about the right amount of text; it emits the wrong text. That
distinguishes this failure cleanly from the Whisper decode artefact of
§6.6.1, which _was_ over-generation, and it rules out the trivial
reading in which shallow adaptation simply makes the decoder run on.
What we can state is that altering early encoder representations moves a
frozen decoder toward outputs dominated by its prior; the decoder’s own
weights never change, so no claim is made about the language model
itself. Diagnostics: scripts/language_prior_diagnostics.py.

 

## 8. Discussion

The three tested probe summaries do not predict the placement. This is
the paper’s central negative result, and the qualifier is load-bearing:
what fails is the mapping from _these_ depth statistics to a band, not
probing in general (§9, Limitation 14). The natural and widely-assumed
inference — measure where phonetic information matures in an encoder,
then put the adapters there — has three plausible readings. With both
architectures measured under one pipeline, all three fail.

_Reading 1 — adapt where PER improves most steeply._ This is closest to
the prior work’s own wording (“phoneme recognition improves most steeply
through the middle layers … so these layers should be the most
productive targets”). Applied to Qwen3-ASR it fails badly: the steepest
improvement is in blocks 1–8, which drop 16.97 pp (Table 4), and that
band is catastrophically the _worst_ target — the only one that leaves
the model worse than not adapting at all. The upper band has the
flattest slope and wins.

_Reading 2 — adapt where band-mean PER is lowest._ This is the strongest
of the three, and as a rule for selecting the best of the four available
targets it holds in one cell of four. On CDSD × Qwen3-ASR it works
perfectly: band-mean PER orders upper 35.20 \< middle 48.17 \< lower
64.66, adaptation CER orders upper 29.88 \< middle 30.71 \< lower 35.52,
and upper also beats all. On CDSD × Whisper-small, band-mean PER gives
the _same_ ordering — upper 49.49 \< middle 54.99 \< lower 66.81 — yet
adaptation CER orders all 53.56 \< middle 55.16 \< lower 56.28 \< upper
56.95, so the lowest-PER band is the worst place to adapt. On UASpeech
the rule picks upper for both models — band-mean PER 41.80 and 58.00
(§5.6, Table 8) — and both prefer all (Tables 14 and 22).

A weaker form survives. Restricted to the three equal-budget bands and
ignoring all, the rank correspondence between band-mean PER and
adaptation CER holds for Qwen3-ASR on both corpora, fails for
Whisper-small on CDSD, and is unresolved for Whisper-small on UASpeech
(§6.8). The probe curve therefore retains some purchase on the
_relative_ merit of equal-budget bands within one model, and none on the
question a practitioner actually faces, which includes all-block LoRA
among the options. The band ordering is invariant to probe volume in
both architectures, so neither the survival nor the failure is an
artefact of probe volume.

_Reading 3 — adapt where the knee sits._ The productive band is the one
containing the knee of the PER curve. We state the rule computationally
so it can be applied without discretion: for a curve $`p(\ell)`$ over
layers $`\ell=1,\ldots,L`$, let
$`\ell^{*}=\underset{\ell}{\arg\min}\,p(\ell)`$, and define the knee as
the smallest $`\ell\leq\ell^{*}`$ such that
$`p(\ell)-p(\ell^{*})\leq 0.05\,[p(1)-p(\ell^{*})]`$ — the first depth
reaching within 5% of the total available improvement. The band
containing that layer is the prediction.

Applied to the released curves, the rule gives upper in three cells and
middle in the fourth: Qwen3-ASR × CDSD knee at layer 20, Whisper-small ×
CDSD at layer 10, Qwen3-ASR × UASpeech at layer 18, and Whisper-small ×
UASpeech at layer 8, which sits in that encoder’s middle band 5–8 (Table
8). On CDSD, then, Whisper-small’s knee is at layer 10, not the layer 6
its published curve implies (§5.5), which places it inside the upper
band 9–12 — so the rule predicts upper should win on Whisper-small. It
ranks worst of the four, and all wins with a resolved margin (§6.6).

The threshold is the one free parameter, and two of the four predictions
are not stable in it. The two CDSD cells give upper for every threshold
in 2–10%. The two UASpeech cells move inside that range: Whisper-small ×
UASpeech gives upper at 2% (layer 9) and middle at 5% and 10% (layer 8),
and Qwen3-ASR × UASpeech gives upper at 2% and 5% (layers 19 and 18) and
middle at 10% (layer 16). An earlier version of this paragraph claimed
the predictions were unchanged across 2–10%; recomputing all four from
the released curves shows they are not, which is a further count against
reading the band off a probe rather than sweeping it. The rule was
proposed by us, fitted to our Qwen3-ASR result against the prior work’s
published Whisper-small curve; on the one architecture where it could be
tested prospectively rather than fitted, it is falsified, and we
withdraw it.

How wrong is each rule? Selection regret rather than a win/lose label.
Scoring a heuristic purely on whether it names the empirical argmin
treats two very different errors alike: missing the best band by a
fraction of a point in a cell where the top two are statistically
indistinguishable, and selecting a band that is many points worse. We
therefore report regret,
$`R(h)=\operatorname{CER}[\text{choice of }h]-\operatorname{CER}[\text{best band}]`$,
per cell.

This sharpens the negative result rather than softening it, and it
separates two failures the paper previously scored identically.
_Steepest improvement_ is not merely wrong but catastrophically so — it
never names the best band and costs up to 19.10 pp, because the steepest
part of a PER curve is its early rise and the earliest band is the worst
adaptation target in three of the four cells and second-worst in the
fourth (Whisper-small × CDSD, where upper is last, Table 17). _Lowest
band-mean PER_ names upper everywhere, so it is correct exactly where
upper happens to win and wrong elsewhere, at a mean cost of 2.27 pp;
_knee_ agrees with it in three cells and names middle on Whisper-small ×
UASpeech, at a mean cost of 2.65 pp. A practitioner following the best
of the three heuristics would give up about 2 pp of CER on average and
up to 4 pp, against a four-run sweep that costs a few GPU-hours and
finds the best band directly.

The deeper reason all three fail is that the depth profiles are not in
fact different. All three rules attempt to explain a placement
difference by a maturation-depth difference. But all four probe minima
fall between 83% and 92% of relative depth, none of the curves shows the
mid-stack plateau the published comparison implies, and all four order
the three proportional bands identically (§5.6, Table 9). Two models
whose probe minima both fall in the late encoder prefer different
adaptation bands on CDSD, so whatever selects the band, it is not the
depth at which phoneme identity matures. The completed grid sharpens
this: placement varies across the model × corpus design while the probe
curves do not vary with it, so these particular depth summaries give no
evidence of explaining the variation in the best tested band. We stop
there deliberately. Three mappings failing is not an impossibility
proof: the curves still differ in late-stack rise, curvature, absolute
PER and variance, and we have not tested a learned or non-linear mapping
over those. The claim is that the tested maturation-depth heuristics do
not transfer, not that no statistic of the curve could. The obvious
reply — that a _corpus-matched_ probe might predict UASpeech placement —
is closed by measurement. That route is closed for phoneme-identity PER;
it is not closed for diagnostics this paper does not test (§9,
Limitation 14).

This is a speech-adaptation instance of a distinction the probing
literature has drawn for years, and we claim it as an instance rather
than as a discovery. Hewitt and Liang \[7\] showed that probe accuracy
partly reflects the capacity of the probe rather than the structure of
the representation, and introduced control tasks to separate the two;
Ravichander et al. \[24\] showed directly that a property can be
decodable from a representation without being used by the model’s own
computation. The conceptual gap between _decodability_ and _functional
relevance_ is therefore not new. What is new here is a concrete,
quantified instance of that gap in layer-selective PEFT for dysarthric
ASR: a diagnostic that looks like it should identify where to adapt — it
is measured on the right model, the right corpus and the right unit —
and that nonetheless fails to select the best tested band in three of
four cells. The probing literature predicts that such a failure is
possible; this paper reports what it costs when a placement decision is
made on that basis.

Two failures of control produced the discarded claim, and both are cheap
to avoid. First, a cross-paper comparison stood in for a controlled one:
the Whisper-small half of the original contrast was a published figure,
not a measurement, and once measured under our own pipeline it did not
reproduce (§5.5). A depth comparison across architectures is only as
good as the weaker of its two measurements. Second, a single modest
probe-training volume was treated as sufficient: at 6,200 training
utterances the two architectures appear to differ markedly in late-stack
behaviour, and at 33,617 that difference is 0.25 pp. Report the probe
curve at more than one training volume, or do not compare late-stack
shape across architectures.

What stands, and what the fourth cell bounds. On CDSD, swapping the
model with corpus and protocol held fixed changes which band wins
(§6.6), and on the Qwen3-ASR side a budget-matched control finds no
resolved benefit from additional capacity for either placement — though
at matched budget the placement contrast itself does not resolve at this
sample size, so capacity is not shown to be irrelevant (§6.7).
Inheriting the band from the literature would have selected middle on
Qwen3-ASR — second of the four options at three seeds, and 0.83 pp
behind upper — while the reading closest to the prior work’s own
justification, the steepest-improvement rule, would have selected the
worst band by a wide margin (§8, Table 29). What does not stand is the
generalisation from that single corpus. That removes a second route
rather than restoring the first: a practitioner can read the band off
neither a probe nor another corpus, so it is to be determined by running
the sweep on the model and corpus in hand.

Why the placement difference might arise, given that depth does not
explain it. Two candidates are consistent with the data and neither is
tested here. The first is baseline competence: Whisper-small is much
weaker on CDSD (77.57 CER) than Qwen3-ASR (32.06), and a model with that
much more to repair may need correction spread over all blocks where a
stronger model needs only a top-end adjustment — lower is the clearest
evidence, harming Qwen3-ASR and helping Whisper-small. We put this less
strongly than the uncorrected figures would license: with the decode
correction of 6.6.1 the Whisper baseline is no longer hallucinating at
the corpus level, so the mechanism is “more to fix”, not “must first be
stopped from fabricating”. The fourth cell makes this harder to test:
the competence gap is _wider_ on the corpus where the two models agree
about placement than on the one where they disagree. The second
candidate is what sits downstream: the upper third of the Qwen3-ASR
encoder simultaneously improves phoneme identity and collapses
cross-condition divergence, as one expects of layers projected into a
language-model decoder, as Yegorova et al. \[32\] and Pluth et al.
\[22\] both observe. Two model instances cannot separate depth, frame
rate, pre-training corpus, scale, decoder type and baseline competence.

Relation to DAMA. We do not reproduce the U-shaped adaptability pattern
Xiao et al. \[30\] report for multilingual low-resource adaptation: here
the early band is not a second peak but by a wide margin the worst place
to adapt. This is a contradiction _in our setting_ rather than a
refutation, since DAMA studies language adaptation and we study domain
adaptation. A plausible reconciliation is that language identity is
partly encoded in early acoustic-phonetic layers, so adapting them is
productive for a new language, whereas dysarthria is not the kind of
variation those layers can absorb (§7.1). The transferable statement is
that the U-shape is not universal across adaptation targets.

Practical implications, in decreasing order of confidence.

1.  1.  For low-rank adaptation of this encoder to dysarthric speech, prefer
        the upper third over the lower and middle thirds. That ordering is
        the part that holds across both corpora. Whether upper alone or all
        is better does not transfer: upper wins on CDSD, while on UASpeech
        the point estimate favours all and the interval spans zero (§6.9,
        Table 24) — so state it as corpus-dependent rather than as a
        recommendation for upper-only adaptation. The resolved form of the
        claim is the equal-rank one ($`-`$1.31 pp, CI \[$`-`$2.16,
    $`-`$0.25\]). At matched parameter budgets neither placement
    contrast resolves — $`-`$1.28 pp, CI \[$`-`$2.36, +0.08\] at the
    exact 12,582,912-parameter match and $`-`$0.78 pp, CI \[$`-`$1.55,
        +0.39\] at ~4.2 M — though both point estimates lean the same way,
        and added capacity helps neither placement (§6.7).
2.  2.  Do not assume shallow adaptation is harmless. On Qwen3-ASR ×
        UASpeech, lower is actively worse than not adapting at all (+9.90 pp
        CER, resolved). The effect is model-dependent, not universal: on
        Whisper-small the same band helps, modestly (UASpeech CER $`-`$7.75,
    CI \[$`-`$14.82, $`-`$0.60\]). lower is the weakest of the four
        bands in three of the four cells; the exception is Whisper-small ×
        CDSD, where upper is worse (56.95 against lower’s 56.28, Table 17).
        Whether lower is worse than doing nothing depends on the model.
3.  3.  Determine the band per architecture and per corpus, and by sweeping
        rather than by probing. The first half is not new — Pasad et al.
        \[21\] and Beheshti et al. \[2\] argue the general form of it, and
        it is contested \[14\]. A four-band sweep at fixed parameter budget
        costs roughly four adaptation runs (~3 h each here) and answers the
        question directly; the probing stage is cheaper but does not answer
        it, and running it on the corpus in hand does not rescue it (§5.6).
4.  4.  Treat any published layer recommendation as bound to both the
        architecture and the corpus it was measured on. Following the
        published band would have cost 0.83 pp CER at equal parameter budget
        on Qwen3-ASR — a difference that does not resolve at this sample
        size (§6.1.1) — and on the corpora tested here no probe-derived rule
        selected the best band in more than one of four cells (§8, Table
        29).

What this does not say. It does not say that layer-wise probing is
uninformative — §5.2, §5.3 and §5.5 all report probe results we stand
behind, and the data-efficiency asymmetry is itself a probing result.
The negative claim is specific: the probe curve does not determine the
adaptation band. Nor does it say Qwen3-ASR is a better or worse
foundation model than Whisper; that comparison is not run here. Nor does
it say that low-rank adaptation of this encoder improves Mandarin
dysarthric recognition: on this six-speaker test set, no adapted variant
differs significantly from the unadapted model.

 

## 9. Limitations

These are the binding constraints, stated ahead of the conclusion
because several of them bound claims that would otherwise read as
stronger than the evidence.

1.  1.  Seed coverage, and what is still single-run. The CDSD _adaptation_
        sweep is no longer single-seed: all four bands were retrained at
        seeds 42/43/44 and every CDSD interval in this paper now comes from
        a speaker × seed bootstrap (§6.1.1). That change cost one conclusion
        — upper $`-`$ middle no longer resolves — and left the contrast the
        first contribution rests on intact. What remains single-run is

    1.  (a)
        the CDSD probe curves of §5.1, so no probe curve’s argmax layer
        is claimable, only the band; Every adaptation cell in the model
        × corpus design now carries three seeds: the Whisper-small ×
        CDSD sweep and the §6.7 2×2 were both single-run in earlier
        versions and are not any longer. Adding those seeds changed two
        conclusions and confirmed a third — the 2×2’s budget-matched
        contrast stopped resolving, upper $`-`$ middle on Qwen3-ASR
        stopped resolving, and Whisper-small’s preference for all on
        CDSD proved the most robust contrast in the paper (§6.6). What
        we would now say is that a single-run band sweep is not a sound
        basis for a sub-2 pp claim in either direction: it can overstate
        an effect, and it can also fail to reveal how stable a real one
        is. Seed sensitivity is strongly band-dependent wherever we have
        measured it — on CDSD, seed SD is 0.36 pp for upper and 0.31 for
        middle but 1.15 for lower; on Whisper-small × UASpeech it is
        0.20 pp WER for upper and 0.53 for all but 2.29 for middle — so
        a single run in a high-variance band can sit well away from that
        band’s mean. The general caution we draw from our own
        experience: a band difference of about 1.5 pp is within what a
        training seed can move, and should not be reported as resolved
        from one run.

2.  2.  Six test speakers on CDSD, five on UASpeech. This remains the single
        binding constraint on every adaptation claim. Each bootstrap
        resamples a pool of five or six, so the intervals should be read as
        suggestive rather than decisive, and no sign test can survive
        multiplicity correction at this N even under a perfect result
        (§6.10). Replication across two corpora mitigates this but does not
        remove it. The marginal per-variant CIs are 30–54 pp wide on both
        corpora and we report none of them; only paired contrasts, where
        speaker difficulty cancels within a replicate, are interpretable at
        this N.
3.  3.  upper $`-`$ base is not significant on CDSD, though it is on
        UASpeech ($`-`$7.54 pp, CI excluding zero), along with the other
        three adaptation-versus-baseline contrasts. The CDSD result is
        dominated by CDSD_08, which regresses under every variant and blows
        open every contrast against the unadapted baseline while cancelling
        in band-versus-band contrasts. So “adapting this encoder helps” is
        established on UASpeech and not on CDSD, and we do not pool the two
        corpora, which differ too much in content type to justify a pooled
        estimate.
4.  4.  Two corpora and two architectures throughout, for the probes as well
        as the sweep — but two corpora is still two. The band ordering is
        tested in all four cells of a model × corpus design, and the probe
        results cover the same four cells (§5.6). The UASpeech probes use
        dictionary targets and no integrity gate, so their absolute PER is
        not comparable to the CDSD curves; both corpora are small in the
        unit that matters (6 and 5 test speakers); and neither carries a
        mixed-aetiology cohort — UASpeech is cerebral palsy predominant and
        CDSD’s aetiology mix is not documented per speaker.
5.  5.  The cross-model placement difference rests on CDSD alone; on
        UASpeech the two models agree. This is the binding constraint on the
        paper’s first contribution. The upper $`-`$ all contrast resolves in
        opposite directions on the two architectures on CDSD ($`-`$1.31 CI
    \[$`-`$2.16, $`-`$0.25\] for Qwen3-ASR, +3.40 CI \[+1.84, +5.03\]
    for Whisper-small) — though only the Whisper half survives
    speaker-equal weighting and the exact test (§6.10), so the reversal
    rests on a micro-averaged reading of the Qwen half. On UASpeech
    neither model favours upper: Whisper-small resolves for all (+4.02,
    CI \[+2.36, +6.30\]) and Qwen3-ASR does not resolve it at all
    (+1.66, CI \[$`-`$0.51, +4.46\]), point estimate on the same side
        (Table 24). The corpora do not contradict each other inferentially —
        but a claim built on a reversal needs the reversal, and it is not
        present there.
6.  6.  The cross-model comparison is internally controlled but heavily
        confounded. It is no longer a cross-_paper_ comparison — both the
        probe (§5.5) and the band sweep (§6.6, §6.8) were re-run here under
        our own corpus, split, gate, recipe and scoring. What remains is
        that Whisper-small and Qwen3-ASR differ in depth (12 vs 24 blocks)
        _and_ frame rate (20 vs 77 ms) _and_ decoder type _and_ pre-training
        data _and_ scale _and_, most importantly, unadapted competence. We
        therefore claim that placement differs between these two models on
        one corpus, not that encoder architecture causes it; the experiment
        that would settle the leading explanation is a competence-matched
        third model (§8).
7.  7.  The cross-condition design is matched-distribution, not
        matched-utterance. The prior work matches transcripts per utterance
        and thereby removes lexical confounds by construction; we remove
        them only in expectation, validated by a 411-row strictly paired
        consistency check. The d–u contrast additionally mixes a speaker-set
        difference with a condition difference, so d–r is the contrast the
        claim rests on. No confidence intervals are computed on the
        divergence curves; the shape is the claim, not the magnitudes.
8.  8.  Probe C’s difficulty labels come from the probed model’s own family.
        The prior work labels with a stronger external model; we had no
        stronger _uncontaminated_ Mandarin decoder, so difficulty is defined
        as “hard for a general-purpose ASR model” rather than as an
        independent ground truth. The tier boundaries also deviate from
        plain tertiles, for the reason given in §4.3.
9.  9.  The demographic-skew mechanism is demonstrated for one speaker, not
        established as general. §7.2 shows it for CDSD_08 and quantifies the
        failure as substitution rather than over-generation, but one speaker
        cannot establish that under-representation in training predicts
        regression. An earlier version of this paper argued the point from a
        ten-language synthetic analysis; that analysis rested on data whose
        provenance and rights basis this paper does not establish, and it
        has been removed rather than defended. The question is open.
10. 10. The secondary probe curve from a fine-tuned checkpoint is
        contaminated. That model had seen 81.9% of the probe utterances and
        all six test speakers. Its layer _localisation_ is reported; its
        _magnitude_ is not interpretable as generalisation, and no headline
        claim depends on it.
11. 11. The adaptation set is 14.6% smaller than the prior work’s, because
        it inherits the alignment-integrity gate needed to make probe and
        adaptation manifests commensurable.
12. 12. Probe-training volume is matched, but only for Whisper-small and
        Qwen3-ASR at one setting each. The headline probe curves were
        originally trained on 6,200 utterances against the prior work’s
        $`\approx`$39,000; §5.5 re-runs both architectures at 33,617.
        Absolute PER at 6,200 is not comparable across studies, and shape
        itself is volume-sensitive at low volume, differentially so by depth
        and architecture. The UASpeech probes run at a single volume (5,979
        training utterances) and carry exactly that caveat: the depth at
        which each curve turns was volume-invariant when volume was varied
        on CDSD (Figure 3), but we have not varied it on UASpeech, so its
        stability there is inferred.
13. 13. Severity labels in this corpus are CER-derived, not clinical. The
        corpus provides no clinical severity rating and no healthy controls,
        so the difficulty breakdowns in Tables 10 and 13 stratify by a
        recognition-difficulty proxy and should be read as such.
14. 14. The negative result is about three specific rules and one signal,
        not about every possible probe-based predictor. We do not fit or
        validate a general model mapping probe curves to optimal bands, and
        do not claim none could exist. What we establish is that the three
        readings actually proposed in this literature each fail on at least
        one of two internally-controlled architectures, and that the
        maturation-depth intuition underlying all three is unavailable here.
        The signal is phoneme-identity PER and nothing else; a rule built on
        gradient statistics, residual-stream structure or attention entropy
        could in principle succeed where these fail.
15. 15. The knee rule we proposed before measuring the second architecture
        is withdrawn, and the negative result that replaces it rests on two
        architectures. The rule was post hoc, fitted to two encoders, and
        one of its two inputs was a published figure rather than a
        measurement. Having measured that input ourselves we find it wrong,
        and the rule’s prospective prediction on Whisper-small is falsified
        (§6.6). The replacement claim rests on two internally-controlled
        measurements, but is still a two-architecture result: a third
        encoder could restore some probe-based rule fitting all three,
        though it could not rescue the specific rules refuted here.
16. 16. The cross-architecture comparison confounds baseline competence with
        architecture, and the confound is wider on UASpeech than on CDSD.
        Whisper-small is much weaker on CDSD (77.57 CER) than Qwen3-ASR
        (32.06); on UASpeech the gap is larger still (118.78 WER against
        41.95 CER). The placement difference in §6.6 is therefore
        established as real and not a cross-paper artefact, but is not
        attributed to any single factor, and baseline competence is the
        candidate we consider most likely and least controlled (§8). Because
        the gap is widest in the cell where the two models _agree_, no
        simple monotone story linking competence to placement is available
        from these four cells either.
17. 17. Not every probe curve carries three seeds. The matched-volume CDSD
        re-runs of §5.5 and both UASpeech curves of §5.6 do; the original
        6,200-utterance CDSD curves of §5.1 are single runs, and no probe
        argmin should be read as claimable at layer resolution from those.
        Where three seeds exist they carry paired intervals, but the seeds
        are not independent of the decode. Repeated runs at a _fixed_ seed
        still differ by 0.1–0.4 pp because CTC training on GPU is not
        bit-reproducible (cuDNN algorithm selection and atomics), so a
        “seed” here bounds optimisation variance in general rather than
        initialisation alone. And individual argmin layers remain
        unclaimable even with intervals: adjacent late layers overlap
        heavily, which is why §5.1 and §5.5 report bands and contrasts
        rather than the exact minimising layer.
18. 18. The failure to reproduce the prior work’s mid-stack plateau is
        unexplained. Probe configuration, padding treatment and training
        volume have each been checked and eliminated (§5.5). Baseline
        competence has not been eliminated: against the prior work’s v2
        figures our corrected baseline is 12.4 pp better, so it remains a
        live candidate (§5.5, item 2). Something else differs between the
        two pipelines and we have not identified it. Readers should weight
        our Whisper-small curve accordingly: it is internally controlled and
        consistent across four configurations, but it disagrees with a
        published result for reasons we cannot state.
19. 19. The placement/capacity confound is not resolved at either budget,
        and the 2×2 is CDSD × Qwen3-ASR only. §6.7 equalises the parameter
        count with rank and holds $`\alpha`$ = 2r across all four cells,
        over three seeds. Neither budget-matched placement contrast excludes
        zero: $`-`$1.28 pp, CI \[$`-`$2.36, +0.08\] at the exact 12.58 M
    match, and $`-`$0.78 pp, CI \[$`-`$1.55, +0.39\] at ~4.2 M. Six test
    speakers and three seeds cannot separate placement from per-block
    rank concentration at matched budget. What the 2×2 does establish is
    negative and useful: added capacity helps neither placement (+0.03
    and +0.53 pp, both unresolved), so all is not losing for want of
    parameters. The resolved placement claim is the equal-rank contrast
    of §6.1 ($`-`$1.31 pp, CI \[$`-`$2.16, $`-`$0.25\]), not this one.
        The 2×2 is also run in one cell of the model × corpus design.

## 10. Reproducibility

All experiments ran on a single workstation GPU. The probing stage at
8,800 utterances — feature extraction, 25 taps × 2 models for two
probes, the condition-similarity arm and the difficulty decode — took
under two hours of GPU time. The matched-volume re-runs in §5.5 are
larger: Whisper-small extraction over the 47,687-utterance manifest
produced 107 GB across 13 taps and swept in ~1.7 h, and the Qwen3-ASR
arm 73 GB across 25 taps (41 min at 19.5 utt/s), sweeping in ~55 min.
The UASpeech probes took 484 s of extraction for Qwen3-ASR and 551 s for
Whisper-small, and about 2.5 h for the probes themselves, so replicating
the four-cell probe grid of Table 9 on a new corpus is an afternoon of
one GPU. Each Qwen3-ASR adaptation variant took 3.1–3.5 h on CDSD and
0.53–0.60 h on UASpeech, so the recommended four-band sweep costs
roughly 12–14 h on CDSD and under 2.5 h on UASpeech; the Whisper-small ×
UASpeech cell is cheaper still at 0.36–0.55 h per variant. The
parameter-matched 2×2 is four further CDSD runs at 3.2–5.1 h each.

What the released artefact contains. Because the raw clinical audio
cannot be redistributed, reproducibility here is by identifiers,
configurations and derived statistics rather than by data. The
repository releases: split manifests as each corpus’s own utterance keys
with their split assignment and a manifest fingerprint; every random
seed; the per-speaker and per-seed scores underlying every interval in
this paper; the bootstrap, exact sign-flip and macro-estimator code;
probe curves as CSV rather than figure-only; the LoRA parameter-count
script; and the aggregate result tables.

A frozen decode specification, because decoding is not an implementation
detail here. §6.6.1 showed generation settings moving Whisper CER by
tens of points, so the released artefact pins, in machine-readable form:
model and checkpoint identifiers, the transformers / peft / torch / CUDA
versions, prompt language and task tokens, beam width,
no_repeat_ngram_size, the duration-scaled maximum-output-length rule,
stopping criteria, sampling flags, text normalisation, CER/WER
tokenisation, the checkpoint key-remapping shim, and the expected scores
of a small regression manifest. The last item matters most: it is the
only thing that would catch a silent environment change, and this
project has already seen a load path that produces an apparently working
model with zero checkpoint-key overlap.

Two operational traps specific to the 2×2, both of which cost a run
before they were understood. First, adapter merging must be forced onto
the CPU. peft loads an adapter onto CUDA by default before merging it
into the base weights; on unified-memory hardware the merge of a
rank-192 adapter into a 1.7 B-parameter model then exhausts the shared
pool and the process is killed, with no error that identifies merging as
the cause. Loading base and adapter on CPU, merging there, and saving
before any device move avoids it, at the cost of a slower merge. Second,
training and evaluation require different environments: the trainer
needs a transformers release that the packaged inference path does not
accept, and the evaluator needs one that will load this checkpoint
without silently initialising it at random (see the warning below). The
released scripts therefore name their interpreter explicitly rather than
inheriting whichever environment is active; running the evaluator from
the trainer’s environment is the failure that produces plausible-looking
but meaningless CER.

A memory note that matters for reproduction on unified-memory hardware.
On the GB10 used here, host and device allocations draw on one pool, and
a per-layer feature load of tens of gigabytes leaves CUDA unable to
allocate at model-creation time even when it reports free memory. The
released sweep scripts therefore run one process per layer group and
reserve a CUDA slab before the feature load; a single process sweeping
all layers is killed at the first layer transition, because one layer’s
allocation does not fully release before the next load begins.

Figures. The released scripts/make_figures.py regenerates all four
figures from aggregate CSVs; it requires neither GPU nor corpus access.

Code and aggregate results are released at  
[https://github.com/SMF-Labs/encoder-depth-dysarthric-asr](https://github.com/SMF-Labs/encoder-depth-dysarthric-asr).
The repository holds the full pipeline in run order, the result files
behind every table in this paper, and a README covering the environment,
the scoring conventions and the implementation traps summarised below.
It is made public at the moment the preprint is posted — there is no
journal acceptance to wait for — together with the frozen decode
specification and its regression manifest. The public repository is
published as a single commit, 4ea79e2, whose file tree is identical to
that of the independently audited development head; the development
history is retained privately because some early commits recorded local
filesystem paths. No corpus content appears in either.

One methodological warning, because it determines whether the numbers
can be trusted at all. All decoding here goes through the model’s own
packaged inference path. On a 369-utterance overlap set with recorded
reference predictions (41.87% CER), the generic
chat-template-plus-generate path scored 124.57% where the packaged path
scored 32.38% — a discrepancy large enough to have poisoned every
difficulty label, and visible in no error message. Validate any new
inference path against a known result before trusting it. A second trap
of the same kind, documented in the repository, is that loading this
checkpoint under a current transformers release silently produces a
randomly initialised model.

Data availability. CDSD is distributed by its custodians under an
academic licence signed on 19 February 2026, which permits research use
only, prohibits provision of the audio to second parties, and requires
the credit and citation given in the Acknowledgements. UASpeech was
obtained by written permission of the corpus PI dated 6 February 2026;
there is no separate signed instrument, and the undertaking that the
corpus be used for academic, non-commercial research and not
redistributed was given in our access request and accepted by the grant

What the repository does contain, stated to match §10 exactly: the split
assignments as each corpus’s own utterance keys with a manifest
fingerprint — ‘speaker/subset/utterance’ for CDSD and
‘speaker/block_word’ for UASpeech, which is what a reproducer needs to
rebuild the split, and which carry no audio, no transcript and no path
on our machines, so redistributing no corpus content — together with
every random seed, the per-speaker and per-seed scores underlying each
interval, the bootstrap, exact sign-flip and macro-estimator code, the
probe curves as CSV, the parameter-count script, the frozen decode
specification with its regression manifest, and the aggregate result
tables. Adapted model weights are not released.

## 11. Conclusion

Layer-placement advice for parameter-efficient adaptation of ASR
foundation models to dysarthric speech transfers neither reliably
between encoder families nor reliably between corpora, and the depth
profile usually invoked to explain such differences does not account for
them.

The positive half is a controlled placement result confined to one
corpus. Holding corpus, speaker split, integrity gate, LoRA recipe and
seed discipline constant and swapping the model, the best band changes
on CDSD: on a 24-block foundation model whose decoder is a language
model, the upper band wins and beats adapting the entire encoder at one
third the parameters, though at a matched parameter budget that contrast
is no longer resolvable (§6.7); on a 12-block Whisper-small, all-block
LoRA wins and the upper band ranks worst of the four. Running the same
contrast in all four cells of a model × corpus design shows that the
reversal is specific to CDSD: on UASpeech neither model favours the
upper band (upper $`-`$ all = +4.02 pp CER, CI \[+2.36, +6.30\] for
Whisper-small; unresolved for Qwen3-ASR, point estimate on the same
side). Whisper-small’s preference for all holds on both corpora;
Qwen3-ASR’s upper-versus-all result is corpus-dependent in its observed
point estimates, resolved on CDSD and unresolved with the sign reversed
on UASpeech (Table 24), and an observed change between corpora is not
the same thing as a statistically established cross-corpus difference,
which this design does not test. What does hold across both corpora is
the direction of the 24-block model’s three-band depth ordering — upper
over middle over lower in every cell we ran — together with the finding
that adapting the lower band is reliably worse than leaving the encoder
alone on Qwen3-ASR × UASpeech — the one cell where that contrast
resolves; the CDSD point estimate is also harmful but unresolved, and on
Whisper-small the lower band helps. Of that ordering, upper over lower
resolves on both corpora; the upper-over-middle step resolves on
UASpeech but not on CDSD once training seeds are resampled (§6.1.1).

The negative half concerns the inference from probe to placement.
Measured under our own pipeline rather than cited, Whisper-small’s
phoneme-error minimum sits at 83% of relative depth against 83–92% for
Qwen3-ASR: both probes reach their minima in the late encoder, yet on
CDSD the two models prefer different bands. All three candidate rules
linking probe curve to band — adapt where PER improves most steeply,
adapt where band-mean PER is lowest, adapt where the knee sits — fail on
at least one architecture, the last being the reconciliation we
ourselves proposed. The completed grid shows further that placement is
corpus-dependent while the probe curves are not: all four minima fall
between 83% and 92% of relative depth, with identical band orderings and
no mid-stack plateau anywhere. A near-constant quantity cannot select a
varying outcome, and the route by which a corpus-matched probe might
have predicted the band is thereby excluded for phoneme-identity PER.

Two methodological results explain how the discarded claim survived, and
both generalise beyond this paper. Probe-readout sensitivity to training
volume is depth-dependent and architecture-specific — at a fixed epoch
budget, so volume and optimiser steps are not separated (§5.5) — and a
probe trained at a single modest volume exaggerates late-stack
differences between architectures whose deep layers differ in readout
difficulty — an apparent 6.56-versus-1.85 pp gap here collapses to 0.25
pp at 5.4× the data. And a cross-paper depth comparison is only as good
as the weaker of its two measurements: we cannot reproduce the mid-stack
plateau reported for Whisper-small under matched configuration, padding
or training volume, and we report that as unexplained; baseline
competence is not matched between the two models and is not ruled out as
a contributor (§5.5).

Two constraints bound the result and are reported rather than buried —
the encoder’s frame period makes frame-level boundary probing ill-posed
on this architecture, and shallow adaptation damages acoustically
atypical speakers by replacing transcription with fluent, corpus-typical
text.

analysis of two existing, licensed speech corpora. No new human-subject
data was collected and no participant was contacted. Both corpora were
collected under their originating institutions’ ethical approvals —
UASpeech under University of Illinois Institutional Review Board
approval for the project _Audiovisual Description and Recognition of
Audible and Visible Dysarthric Phonology_ (NIH NIDCD 5R21 DC008090) —
and both are used here within the terms recorded for each. For CDSD that
is the signed academic licence described under Data availability. For
UASpeech it is the corpus PI’s written permission together with the
terms the custodian publishes for the distribution: access limited to
academic and government research laboratories, no commercial use, data
to be protected and not redistributed.

We state the basis separately for each corpus, because it differs. For
UASpeech the originating collection carries the named Institutional
Review Board approval above. For CDSD the corpus paper documents written
informed consent rather than an ethics-committee number: Wan et
al. (2024, §2.1) report that each speaker signed informed consent before
recording, that parental or guardian consent plus the minor’s assent was
obtained for the five speakers under 18, and that the nine speakers
recorded on video signed an additional consent form; speakers were told
they could withdraw at any point, and names were never collected, each
recording being keyed to a serial number instead. No IRB approval number
is cited by that source, and we do not assert one. CDSD is used here
under a signed custom-academic licence that prohibits redistribution and
requires the © Su-Jing Wang credit carried in the Acknowledgements. No
new participant contact or data collection occurred, and no separate
secondary-use determination was sought from the authors’ own
institution. We report this as what was done rather than as a general
rule: whether a given institution requires its own secondary-use or
not-human-subjects determination depends on that institution’s policy
and jurisdiction, and readers should not infer that originating approval
plus a data-use agreement is universally sufficient.

Two findings in this paper are fairness results, not only statistical
ones, and we would rather state that plainly. First, the audio-integrity
gate excludes utterances at a higher rate in the most impaired stratum
(§4), so the population every CER here is measured on is skewed away
from the speakers who need the technology most; a system tuned on such
an evaluation can look better than it is for them. Second, shallow
adaptation does not merely raise average error — it concentrates its
damage on the acoustically atypical speakers (§7.2), and does so in the
specific form of confident, fluent, wrong output rather than visibly
degraded transcription. For accessibility technology, an adaptation
choice that improves the mean while catastrophically failing the least
typical speakers is the failure mode that matters most, and it is the
one least visible in a corpus-level metric. We report per-speaker
results throughout for that reason.

Data protection. No attempt was made to re-identify any speaker. Speaker
identifiers used throughout are the corpora’s own pseudonymous codes. No
audio, transcripts or per-utterance predictions are released (§10).
