# Towards Efficient Simultaneous Inverse Text Normalization with Pretrained Text-to-Text Language Model and Read–Tag–Write Policy

Kiet Anh    Khanh    Bao    Linh    Dung     
Thai    Mai    Tri    Vu

###### Abstract

Streaming Inverse Text Normalization (ITN) is vital for converting
spoken-form outputs from streaming Automatic Speech Recognition into
formatted written text. Existing streaming ITN methods rely on hybrid
systems combining neural tagging with expert-crafted finite-state
transducer rules, limiting scalability across domains and languages.
While end-to-end models offer superior scalability by learning directly
from data, standard encoder-decoder architectures are inherently
non-streaming due to global attention. In this paper, we propose an
efficient streaming end-to-end ITN system, adapting a pretrained
text-to-text model to leverage its robust linguistic knowledge. To
enable streaming, we introduce architectural adaptations, a specialized
training strategy, a Read–Tag–Write decoding policy, and inference
optimizations. Experiments on Vietnamese datasets show accuracy
comparable to non-streaming baselines, outperforming hybrid methods
while satisfying real-time latency requirements.

###### keywords

inverse text normalization, speech recognition, streaming, end-to-end

## 1 Introduction

Inverse Text Normalization (ITN) converts spoken-form transcriptions
produced by Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) systems into readable
written-form representations \[1\]. While ASR models typically train on
verbalized labels to maintain phonetic alignment with audio, their
outputs often lack readability and impair downstream tasks such as
machine translation and natural language understanding. ITN addresses
this by restoring punctuation, applying capitalization, and performing
phrase-level transformations, including semiotic and phonetic
phrases \[2\]. For example, it converts expressions like “one point two
dollars” to “\$1.20”. While spoken and written forms are frequently
identical, certain structured phrases differ substantially and require
contextual reasoning to resolve ambiguities. This makes ITN inherently
context-dependent and necessitates robust contextual representations to
ensure accurate disambiguation across diverse linguistic patterns \[3\].

Recent advances in ITN have focused on end-to-end approaches, which
achieve strong performance across languages and domains \[1\] by
implicitly learning patterns from data, thereby offering greater
scalability and enabling effective model compression \[3\]. These
methods primarily use sequence-to-sequence (seq2seq) architectures to
map spoken-form inputs directly to written-form outputs \[1, 2, 4, 5,
6\]. However, standard seq2seq models often produce unrecoverable
hallucination errors, yielding fluent yet semantically incorrect results
that alter original meaning \[1\]. Additionally, processing full
sentences increases computational cost and risks unnecessary changes to
unchanged tokens \[2\]. Hybrid methods alleviate these problems by
combining neural tagging models with finite-state transducer (FST)
rules \[7, 8, 9\]. They excel at preserving unmodified text but depend
on expert-crafted FST grammars and extensive inside-outside-beginning
(IOB) tag annotation for each ITN category. This dependence limits
scalability and performance improvement due to the substantial manual
effort required for IOB labeling and FST rule creation \[3\]. Pretrained
language models \[1, 6\] and data-driven techniques \[5, 10\] have also
been explored to address data scarcity, improve generalization, and
enhance ITN performance across diverse languages and domains.

Despite advances in offline ITN, simultaneous or streaming ITN remains
underexplored yet essential for real-time applications such as live ASR,
meeting transcription, and speech translation, where formatted text must
be displayed instantaneously to preserve conversational flow. Industrial
systems often rely on cascaded architectures, applying incremental
punctuation and casing models as post-processing steps \[7, 8\]. While
these modular pipelines provide stability, they struggle with complex
semiotic ambiguities that require joint modeling of capitalization,
punctuation, and structural transformations. Pioneering work in this
area \[3\] introduced a hybrid streaming solution employing a
chunk-based Transformer \[11\] tagger to detect ITN categories in
incoming ASR tokens, followed by category-specific FSTs for targeted
conversion. A subsequent study \[12\] adapted a pretrained language
model with dynamic, context-aware processing to handle variable ASR
chunk sizes and accumulated tokens, achieving low latency and accuracy
comparable to non-streaming ITN on a limited dataset. However, current
streaming methods rely exclusively on hybrid architectures and inherit
limited scalability due to the need for manual FST rule development and
extensive IOB labeling. While seq2seq models offer greater scalability,
they cannot operate in a streaming fashion because their encoder-decoder
architectures rely on global cross-attention, which prevents incremental
processing.

Figure 1: Overview of the proposed streaming end-to-end ITN framework
using the Read–Tag–Write policy. The streaming encoder utilizes Dynamic

normalization span, the autoregressive decoder is selectively triggered,
generating normalized text by cross-attending to the relevant encoder

$`\hat{y}_{pre}`$.

To adapt offline seq2seq models for streaming inference, the field of
Simultaneous Machine Translation (SiMT) has introduced various
techniques to enable incremental processing \[13\]. A core component of
SiMT is the read-write policy, which decides whether to wait for new
source tokens (read) or generate target tokens (write) to balance
quality and latency. For instance, the wait-$`k`$ policy \[14, 15, 16\]
enforces a fixed lag by alternating write/read operations after an
initial $`k`$-token delay, while monotonic attention mechanisms \[17\]
restrict attention to left-to-right alignments. Other adaptive
strategies involve learned thresholds for dynamic read-write
decisions \[18\], Hidden Markov Transformers for simultaneous
generation \[19\], or leveraging large language models as
policy-makers \[20\]. However, while effective for translation, these
strategies introduce redundant computation for ITN. In SiMT, the
reordering problem represents a primary conflict between translation
quality and real-time latency. Unlike SiMT, which requires global
structural restructuring, ITN is characterized by local transformations
that maintain the original word order. Because ASR outputs are largely
verbatim, the fixed lags and generative overhead typical of SiMT
policies may be counterproductive for ITN. Consequently, the
applicability of complex SiMT scheduling to the localized domain of ITN
remains unexplored.

In this paper, we propose, to the best of our knowledge, the first
streaming end-to-end ITN model. Our approach achieves accuracy
comparable to non-streaming end-to-end baselines, outperforms streaming
hybrid methods, and eliminates the need for manual FST rules and IOB
labeling. Our experiments are conducted on Vietnamese, a challenging
language for ITN due to its complex morphological nuances and frequent
phonetic borrowing. By relying strictly on standard text-to-text
foundations rather than language-specific heuristics, our approach
ensures cross-linguistic generalization and is compatible with any
seq2seq architecture. Our main contributions include:

- •
  A recipe for making pretrained seq2seq models truly streamable: We
  present a practical set of techniques: architectural changes, a
  streaming-aware multi-task objective, and a novel data augmentation
  technique. That together enable end-to-end ITN with strict
  left-to-right processing.
- •
  An efficient Read–Tag–Write decoding policy with KV Caching and
  Copy–Paste Strategy: We introduce a simultaneous inference procedure
  that (i) passes through non-normalized tokens with minimal delay
  and (ii) triggers generation only upon completing a normalization
  span, further accelerated by KV caching and a Copy–Paste strategy that
  keeps historical context and removes redundant computation.
- •
  Comprehensive empirical evaluation: We rigorously evaluate our
  approach on a newly curated 5M-sentence Vietnamese dataset featuring
  automated alignments and boundary tags, verifying its highly efficient
  performance.

## 2 Streaming End-to-End Inverse Text Normalization

In this section, we explain the chosen pretrained text-to-text models
and describe our proposed framework for streaming end-to-end ITN.

### 2.1 Pretrained Text-to-Text Models

Pretrained text-to-text models such as BART \[21\] and T5 \[22\] have
advanced seq2seq learning by unifying diverse NLP tasks under a
generative framework. Unlike encoder-only architectures, these models
utilize a complete Transformer encoder-decoder structure where both
inputs and outputs are represented as text strings. BART \[21\] is
pretrained using a denoising objective including token masking,
deletion, and sentence permutation to learn robust bidirectional encoder
representations and autoregressive decoder capabilities. Similarly, T5
\[22\] employs a span-corruption objective on the Colossal Clean Crawled
Corpus (C4), facilitating effective transfer learning for downstream
tasks such as translation and summarization. Extensive empirical
evidence indicates that leveraging these pretrained foundations
significantly improves performance on downstream tasks compared to
training from scratch, especially in low-resource or domain-specific
scenarios \[21, 22, 23\].

For the Vietnamese language, EnViT5¹¹ 1
[https://huggingface.co/VietAI/envit5-base](https://huggingface.co/VietAI/envit5-base)
\[24\] adapts the T5-base architecture to a bilingual setting. Following
the standard configuration, the model features 12 layers in both the
encoder and decoder, a hidden dimension of 768, 12 attention heads, and
a gated-GELU feed-forward projection with an intermediate dimension of
2048, totaling approximately 275 million parameters. EnViT5 was
pretrained on a massive bilingual corpus, including an 80GB filtered
subset of CC100 \[25\], using a 15% span-corruption objective for both
English and Vietnamese. It utilizes a shared SentencePiece
tokenizer \[26\] with a 50,048-subword vocabulary specifically optimized
for the morphological nuances of the Vietnamese language. EnViT5
achieves superior performance on Vietnamese-English translation tasks on
the PhoMT benchmark \[27\], outperforming larger models such as mBART
\[24\]. Given its superior semantic alignment and generative efficiency,
we adopt EnViT5 as the foundation for our end-to-end ITN system,
subsequently adapting the architecture to satisfy streaming
requirements.

### 2.2 Model Architecture

The proposed streaming end-to-end ITN model, illustrated in Fig. 1, is
built upon the pretrained EnViT5 architecture. It comprises a
streaming-capable encoder and an autoregressive decoder, optimized for
real-time inverse text normalization.

#### 2.2.1 Streaming Encoder with Joint Tagger

To support streaming inference within the encoder, we employ the Dynamic
Right Context (DRC) masking technique \[28\] during training.
Specifically, we utilize an attention mask to incorporate variable chunk
sizes and dynamic look-ahead while maintaining access to the full
historical left context. By varying these parameters during training,
the masking strategy effectively simulates the restricted future context
inherent in real-time processing, ensuring robustness to diverse latency
constraints during inference.

For an input sequence $`X=\{x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1}\}`$, the encoder
generates hidden representations $`H\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}`$ through
Transformer layers constrained by the DRC mask, where $`d`$ denotes the
hidden dimensionality of the model. A linear tagging layer (the Tagger
module as illustrated in Fig. 1), is integrated atop these hidden
representations to facilitate on-the-fly detection of normalization
spans (e.g., punctuation, capitalization, or numerical expressions).
Following \[2\], the tagger classifies each token $`x_{i}`$ using a
$`\{\text{B, I, O}\}`$ (Beginning, Inside, Outside) labeling scheme. The
predicted tag $`\hat{t}_{i}`$ is determined by:

```math
\hat{t}_{i}=\text{argmax}(H_{i}W_{tag}),\quad W_{tag}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times 3} \tag{1}
```

These predicted tags serve as the control signals for the Read–Tag–Write
policy (described in Section 2.3). Unlike hybrid approaches \[3, 12\]
that require fine-grained tags for various ITN categories to trigger
specific FST rules, our tagger utilizes only 3 classes. This reduced
label space simplifies classification and leads to more robust span
detection compared to complex multi-class systems.

#### 2.2.2 Autoregressive Decoder and Training Objective

During training, the decoder generates written-form tokens $`y_{t}`$ by
cross-attending to the encoder representations $`H`$, conditioned on the
ground-truth previous tokens $`y_{<t}`$. While the encoder is
constrained for streaming, the decoder maintains its standard
autoregressive structure. The probability distribution over the
vocabulary at each step is computed as:

```math
P(y_{t}|y_{<t},H)=\text{softmax}(e_{t}W_{ffn}^{\top}) \tag{2}
```

where $`e_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}`$ is the decoder hidden state at step
$`t`$, $`W_{ffn}\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times d}`$ is the vocabulary projection
matrix, and $`V`$ denotes the vocabulary size.

The model is optimized using a multi-task objective. We define
$`\mathcal{L}_{tag}`$ as the cross-entropy loss for span detection and
$`\mathcal{L}_{gen}`$ as the sequence generation loss:

```math
\mathcal{L}_{tag}=-\frac{1}{\sum_{b=1}^{B}N_{b}}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{b}}\log P(t_{b,i}\mid x_{b,i}) \tag{3}
```

```math
\mathcal{L}_{gen}=-\frac{1}{\sum_{b=1}^{B}T_{b}}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{b}}\log P(y_{b,t}\mid y_{b,<t},H_{b}) \tag{4}
```

Here, $`B`$ denotes the batch size, while $`N_{b}`$ and $`T_{b}`$
represent the source and target sequence lengths for the $`b`$-th
sample, respectively. The terms $`t_{b,i}`$ and $`y_{b,t}`$ denote the
ground-truth tag at step $`i`$ and the target token at step $`t`$. The
probability $`P(t_{b,i}\mid x_{b,i})`$ is computed by applying the
softmax function to the tagger logits $`H_{b,i}W_{tag}`$.

The overall objective is a combination of these components:

```math
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{tag}+\mathcal{L}_{gen} \tag{5}
```

### 2.3 Read–Tag–Write Policy

To enable efficient streaming inference, we propose the Read–Tag–Write
(RTW) policy. Unlike SiMT policies that manage global structural
reordering \[13\], RTW exploits the local, order-preserving nature of
ITN to coordinate the interaction between the streaming encoder and the
autoregressive decoder, ensuring that generative normalization occurs
only when necessary. The policy operates in three distinct phases:

1.  \[leftmargin=\*\]
2.  1.  Read: The system receives incoming ASR tokens in discrete chunks.
        Let $`C_{k}=\{x_{i},\dots,x_{i+c-1}\}`$ be the $`k`$-th chunk of
        size $`c`$. The encoder, constrained by the DRC mask, processes
        $`C_{k}`$ by leveraging the cached historical context and a limited
        look-ahead of $`r`$ tokens to compute hidden representations
        $`H_{i:i+c-1}`$, which are appended to the global hidden state
        sequence $`H_{0:i+c-1}`$.
3.  2.  Tag: A joint tagging layer performs token-level classification on
        the hidden states of the newly arrived chunk. For each token
        $`x_{j}\in C_{k}`$, the model predicts a tag
        $`\hat{t}_{j}\in\{B,I,O\}`$ as:

    ```math
    \hat{t}_{j}=\text{argmax}(H_{j}W_{tag}) \tag{6}
    ```

    The policy identifies transformation spans using $`\{B,I\}`$ labels.
    A span (e.g., a single $`B`$ or a $`B`$-$`I`$ sequence) is
    considered complete only when the tagger encounters a tag
    $`\hat{t}_{j+1}\in\{B,O\}`$ or a sentence boundary. If
    $`\hat{t}_{j+1}=I`$, the token is treated as a continuation of the
    current span and the system continues to “Read” without triggering
    the “Write” phase.

4.  3.  Write: Unlike standard seq2seq models that decode the entire
        sequence, the RTW policy invokes the decoder selectively based on
        predicted tags:
    - •
      Verbatim Pass-through: For tokens tagged as $`O`$, the system
      directly emits the spoken-form token. This ensures minimal latency
      and preserves the integrity of verbatim segments by bypassing the
      generative decoder.
    - •
      Selective Decoding: When a $`B-I`$ span (or a sequence of
      consecutive spans) is completed, the decoder is triggered. To
      optimize throughput, multiple consecutive spans within a chunk are
      concatenated and processed in a single decoder invocation. Let
      $`m`$ be the index of the final token in the last completed span.
      The decoder generates the written-form segment $`\hat{y}_{local}`$
      by cross-attending to the encoder hidden states $`H_{0:m}`$:

      ```math
      P(\hat{y}_{local}\mid\hat{y}_{pre},H_{0:m})=\prod_{u=1}^{U}P(y_{u}\mid[\hat{y}_{pre};y_{<u}],H_{0:m}) \tag{7}
      ```

      where $`[\cdot;\cdot]`$ denotes the concatenation operator,
      $`\hat{y}_{pre}`$ represents the written-form prefix generated in
      previous steps, and $`y_{u}`$ is the $`u`$-th token of the local
      sequence of length $`U`$. Crucially, cross-attention is restricted
      to $`H_{0:m}`$, ensuring look-ahead context does not influence the
      current transformation. This constraint prevents the decoder from
      over-generating or emitting extraneous tokens beyond the target
      span. The decoder generates tokens until an \<EOS\> token is
      predicted, signaling the end of the local transformation. To
      prevent infinite decoding loops in edge cases where the model
      fails to emit an \<EOS\> token, we implement a forced hard-stop
      that truncates generation at a maximum of
      $`L_{\text{max\_local}}`$ tokens per local span.

### 2.4 Inference Optimization via Copy–Paste Strategy and KV Caching

Encoder KV Caching: To maximize efficiency, we implement an incremental
Key-Value (KV) cache for the encoder, enabling the reuse of computations
from previous chunks. Similarly, we cache the encoder hidden states and
the previously decoded written-form prefix. When the decoder is
triggered, the entire cross-attention and self-attention KV states need
to be updated to incorporate the newly computed encoder states. We
perform an initial single batched forward pass through the decoder to
populate the self-attention and cross-attention KV caches using the
existing prefix, bypassing the need for autoregressive decoding during
this initialization phase. Subsequent tokens are then produced via
standard autoregressive generation. These optimizations significantly
improve inference throughput and minimize latency.  
Copy–Paste Strategy: In contrast to \[2\], which completely excludes
historical context from the autoregressive decoder, we argue that such
context is vital for accurate normalization. Since spoken and written
forms are frequently identical in ITN, the $`O`$ tag is the most
prevalent. Our RTW policy leverages this by bypassing the decoder for
these tokens and copying them directly to the output. Crucially, these
skipped tokens are still incorporated into the historical context to
guide the decoding of $`B-I`$ tokens. This approach not only reduces
computational overhead and mitigates hallucinations in verbatim segments
but also allows the decoder to maintain the full historical context
efficiently.  
Decoder KV Caching: To mitigate the computational overhead inherent in
the Copy–Paste mechanism and autoregressive decoding, where standard
processing would redundantly recompute the entire history for both
copied and emitted tokens, we implement the “fully streaming” version by
enabling the decoder KV Caching for both self- and cross-attention. This
allows the model to reuse states from previous invocations and perform
updates only for new tokens. While this eliminates initial passes, it
introduces a training-inference mismatch: training uses the full target
prefix, whereas streaming inference updates states incrementally without
re-attending to past tokens under new encoder states. Our experiments
show this results in only a marginal performance fluctuation compared to
the first strategy.

### 2.5 Prefix-based Training Augmentation

0:  Training pair $`(\mathbf{x}_{0:N-1},\mathbf{y}_{0:M-1})`$,
probability $`p_{\text{trunc}}`$

1:  $`r\sim\text{Uniform}(0,1)`$

2:  if $`r<p_{\text{trunc}}`$ then {Truncated case}

3:   Sample $`k\sim\text{Uniform}\{1,\dots,N-1\}`$

4:   $`\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\leftarrow x_{0:k-1}`$ {No source-side
\<EOS\>}

5:   $`\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\leftarrow[y_{0:l-1};\text{{<EOS>}}]`$
{$`y_{0:l-1}`$ is the target aligned with $`x_{0:k-1}`$}

6:  else {Full-sequence case}

7:
  $`\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\leftarrow[\mathbf{x}_{0:N-1};\text{{<EOS>}}]`$

8:
  $`\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\leftarrow[\mathbf{y}_{0:M-1};\text{{<EOS>}}]`$

9:  end if

10:  return $`(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})`$

Algorithm 1 Prefix-based Training Augmentation

The RTW policy requires the decoder to emit an \<EOS\> token to
terminate each local normalization span. Standard training, however,
typically conditions \<EOS\> prediction on a global source-side
boundary. This creates a train-test mismatch during streaming inference,
where local spans must terminate without observing a global \<EOS\> or
future context. Simply appending an \<EOS\> token to source chunks
during inference is unsuitable, as it would invalidate the incremental
KV cache through self- and cross-attention and exacerbate distribution
shifts.

To bridge this train-test discrepancy, we introduce Prefix-based
Training Augmentation (Alg. 1), a computationally efficient method
applied on-the-fly during training. We stochastically truncate training
examples in an aligned manner with a probability $`p_{\text{trunc}}`$.
In these instances, the source-side \<EOS\> is omitted, while the
target-side prefix is explicitly terminated with an \<EOS\> token. This
strategy compels the model to learn that any valid completion of a
normalization span, even when occurring mid-sentence, must trigger
termination based solely on the current partial encoder hidden states.
By simulating these artificial boundaries, the model becomes robust to
the truncated inputs encountered during streaming inference.
Consequently, the decoder can reliably terminate generation without
compromising the incremental KV cache, ensuring the system remains
responsive to subsequent incoming chunks.

### 2.6 Data Construction

We construct our dataset from the binhvq²² 2
[https://github.com/binhvq/news-corpus](https://github.com/binhvq/news-corpus)
news corpus, a large-scale collection containing approximately 6 billion
words across diverse domains. Using the proposed data generation
pipeline, we produce 5M training sentence pairs, 50k validation pairs,
and 50k test pairs. In addition to spoken-written text pairs, the
dataset provides deterministic phrase-level alignments between
spoken-form inputs and written-form targets, as well as token-level
normalization boundary annotations. For span detection, each token is
labeled using the IOB tagging scheme to identify segments requiring
normalization. These spans encompass the entire affected word. For
instance, a trailing punctuation mark is grouped within the preceding
word’s span. These annotations facilitate both prefix-based training
augmentation and auxiliary tagging supervision.  
Categories: We define a comprehensive set of ITN categories that reflect
common phenomena in ASR outputs. These categories include punctuation
restoration, capitalization, semiotic normalization, and phonetic
normalization. For punctuation restoration, we consider four standard
punctuation marks: comma, period, question mark, and exclamation mark
\[12\]. Semiotic normalization covers entities such as numbers, dates,
times, fractions, currencies, digit sequences, and electronic addresses,
while phonetic normalization targets out-of-vocabulary (OOV)
expressions, including foreign words, named entities, domain names, and
letter sequences.  
Pipeline: The dataset is generated through an automated data generation
pipeline that builds upon the data preparation framework introduced in
\[2\], with substantial extensions to support a broader range of ITN
categories. Specifically, we define a comprehensive set of regular
expressions (regex) to identify normalization regions in written-form
text:

- •
  Capitalization and punctuation restoration: Spoken-form inputs are
  generated by removing all punctuation marks and converting the text to
  lowercase.
- •
  Semiotic normalization: Each class (e.g., cardinal/ordinal numbers,
  decimals, currencies, time expressions, and factions) is identified
  using dedicated regex patterns. Since these expressions are detected
  in written form, rule-based transformations generate corresponding
  spoken-form candidates. Recognizing that a single written entity may
  have multiple valid spoken realizations, the pipeline randomly selects
  one candidate during data generation to reflect natural spoken
  variability. To ensure high quality, we manually inspect
  representative samples from each semiotic class to verify the
  correctness and coverage of the transformation rules.
- •
  Phonetic normalization: We define phonetic phrases as tokens that are
  neither semiotic expressions nor present in the ASR vocabulary. This
  allows us to extract written-form phonetic entities, such as foreign
  named entities and domain-specific terms. We then perform manual
  verification of an automated grapheme-to-phoneme output to construct a
  dedicated phonetic dictionary comprising approximately 125k common
  foreign and OOV terms. This dictionary is subsequently used to map
  written-form phonetic phrases back to their spoken-form counterparts
  during dataset generation.

Through this regex-driven transformation process, the pipeline
deterministically constructs phrase-level spoken-written alignments and
assigns specific category labels to each normalization segment,
providing precise supervision for both prefix-based training
augmentation and the auxiliary tagging task for identifying
normalization boundaries.

## 3 Experimental Setup

### 3.1 Datasets

For training and evaluation, we utilize the dataset described in
Section 2.6. For hyperparameter tuning, we use a small subset of 500k
samples from the training set and evaluate performance on the validation
set. To better assess the system’s real-world performance, we construct
an additional test set by passing all 50k samples from the original test
set through the Microsoft Edge TTS system³³ 3
[https://github.com/rany2/edge-tts](https://github.com/rany2/edge-tts)
to synthesize speech. The generated audio is then processed by the
ChunkFormer ASR model⁴⁴ 4
[https://huggingface.co/khanhld/chunkformer-ctc-large-vie](https://huggingface.co/khanhld/chunkformer-ctc-large-vie) \[29\]
to produce ASR transcripts. The word error rate (WER) of this ASR output
relative to the reference transcripts (spoken text) is $`14.21\%`$. This
error rate reflects typical ASR artifacts, such as misrecognitions and
phonetic ambiguities. These issues are especially pronounced because the
dataset contains numerous OOV and foreign words, providing a challenging
and realistic input stream. This ASR-generated text serves as the input
for evaluating the robustness of our ITN model in a real-world
deployment scenario, following the evaluation protocol in \[3\].

### 3.2 Implementation Details

Training: Models are trained for up to 20 epochs using 4 NVIDIA H100
(80GB) GPUs and are implemented via the Hugging Face Transformers
framework⁵⁵ 5
[https://github.com/huggingface/transformers](https://github.com/huggingface/transformers).
We employ the AdamW optimizer \[30\] with a peak learning rate of
$`10^{-4}`$, a one-epoch linear warmup, and a cosine learning rate
scheduler. Additional optimizer hyperparameters include
$`\beta_{1}=0.9`$, $`\beta_{2}=0.999`$, weight decay $`\lambda=0.01`$,
and a global batch size of 512. Our streaming ITN models utilize dynamic
context masking with variable chunk sizes $`c\in\{1,2,3,4,5\}`$ and
right-context sizes $`r\in\{0,1,2\}`$ with $`p_{\text{trunc}}=0.8`$ for
prefix-based training augmentation. To benchmark against a streaming
hybrid model, we retrain the architecture from \[12\] on our dataset and
integrate it with our own weighted FST module. For a baseline streaming
end-to-end approach, we train a multi-path wait-$`k`$ model \[15\]. We
also compare our approach against the AdapITN model \[2\], which is
trained on our dataset with the biasing mechanism excluded to ensure a
fair comparison.  
Evaluation: During inference, we employ greedy decoding. Following the
methodology in \[1\], we evaluate performance using ITN Word Error Rate
(I-WER) to measure normalization quality and Non-ITN Word Error Rate
(NI-WER) to quantify unintended perturbations to the source text.
Standard WER, the composite error of the entire process of converting
spoken text to written text, is used for comprehensive performance
assessment. Tagging performance is evaluated using the $`F_{1}`$-score.
To simulate real-time inference for benchmarking, inputs are processed
in discrete chunks. The reported metrics represent the average WER
across an ensemble of configurations, where chunk sizes are sampled from
$`\{3,4,5\}`$ tokens and right-context sizes are randomly selected from
$`\{1,2\}`$ tokens. For configurations utilizing the RTW policy, we set
$`L_{\text{max\_local}}=64`$. This evaluation strategy ensures that the
model’s robustness is accurately reflected across various streaming
latencies, reflecting real-world usage scenarios.  
Statistical Evaluation: To ensure the reliability of our results, we
employ a bootstrap method with 10,000 iterations to determine 95%
confidence intervals (CIs) for the metric of interest, leveraging the
toolkit provided in \[31\]. Results are presented in the format
$`\textit{mean}_{[\textit{LB},\textit{UB}]}`$, where mean represents the
metric’s value on the original test set and the interval
$`[\textit{LB},\textit{UB}]`$ denotes the lower and upper bounds of the
95% confidence interval.

### 4.1 ITN Performance

In this section, we comprehensively evaluate the ITN performance of
various model configurations, categorized into three groups:

- •
  Non-streaming end-to-end baselines ($`B`$): $`B_{0}`$ is an EnViT5
  model trained from scratch, while $`B_{1}`$ utilizes pretrained
  weights. Both models are trained in a seq2seq manner using the
  $`\mathcal{L}_{gen}`$ loss function. $`B_{2}`$ further enhances
  $`B_{1}`$ with an additional auxiliary tagging loss,
  $`\mathcal{L}_{tag}`$. We also include AdapITN \[2\] as a comparative
  baseline.
- •
  Hybrid streaming ($`H`$): We benchmark against the hybrid architecture
  from \[12\], denoted as $`H_{1}`$ (with look-ahead $`r=0`$) and
  $`H_{2}`$ (with $`r\in\{1,2\}`$).
- •
  Proposed end-to-end streaming ($`S`$): $`S_{C}`$ is our causal-masked
  encoder model with non-streaming inference. $`S_{0}`$, $`S_{1}`$ and
  $`S_{2}`$ are our streaming variants using the RTW policy, whereas
  $`S_{3}`$ and $`S_{4}`$ represent the “fully streaming” versions
  utilizing decoder KV caching (as described in Section 2.4). Note that
  $`S_{0}`$ is trained from scratch, while all other $`S`$ variants
  leverage pretrained weights. As an additional end-to-end streaming
  baseline, we benchmark against a multi-path wait-$`k`$ model \[15\]
  decoded with $`k=9`$.

Table 1 presents ITN performance across two settings: With PnC
(punctuation, capitalization, and semiotic/phonetic normalization) and
Without PnC (semiotic and phonetic normalization only).

#### 4.1.1 Non-Streaming Evaluation

Leveraging the pretrained EnViT5 ($`B_{1}`$) yields significant gains
over training from scratch ($`B_{0}`$), with relative WER improvements
of $`16.70_{[10.45,26.15]}`$% and $`33.33_{[5.33,63.16]}`$% in the “With
PnC” and “Without PnC” settings, respectively. Consistently lower I-WER
and NI-WER scores highlight the practical benefits of pretrained
text-to-text models for end-to-end ITN. By inheriting linguistic priors,
the model better captures the global context and semantic alignment
necessary to map informal speech to structured written forms.
Incorporating the auxiliary tagging loss ($`B_{2}`$) further improves
results, establishing a strong baseline with WERs of 3.63% and 0.52%.

Although AdapITN \[2\] is an end-to-end system, it is not fine-tuned
from a pretrained text-to-text model. Specifically, AdapITN utilizes
encoder-only pretrained EnViBert \[32\] for both the encoder and
decoder, with a tagging layer to isolate normalization regions. However,
its decoder only processes isolated text spans, excluding the
surrounding sentential context and making capitalization and punctuation
recovery extremely difficult. As a result, it struggles to generalize in
the “With PnC” evaluation, yielding an I-WER of 156.88% and a high
overall WER of 40.08%. Even in the “Without PnC” setting, which is
consistent with its original evaluation, AdapITN’s 1.45% WER is
$`2.79_{[2.61,3.30]}\times`$ higher than our $`B_{2}`$ baseline. These
results demonstrate the critical importance of incorporating surrounding
context and leveraging a pretrained text-to-text model for complex ITN
tasks.

#### 4.1.2 Streaming Evaluation

Streaming performance follows a similar trend. $`S_{1}`$ (pretrained)
outperforms $`S_{0}`$ (trained from scratch) with relative WER
improvements of $`18.94_{[10.92,27.84]}`$% and
$`52.60_{[29.30,67.08]}`$%. Our proposed $`S_{2}`$ variant achieves WERs
of 4.18% and 0.64%, significantly outperforming the Wait-$`k`$
model \[15\] by factors of $`1.67_{[1.60,1.75]}\times`$ and
$`5.81_{[4.96,6.73]}\times`$, respectively. The failures of Wait-$`k`$
stem from forced generation: even after waiting $`k`$ tokens, the source
input may still lag behind the target output, since a single target
token can correspond to multiple source tokens. This situation occurs
during both training and inference. Additionally, $`S_{2}`$
substantially surpasses the hybrid baseline $`H_{2}`$ \[12\] by
$`1.98_{[1.94,2.01]}\times`$ and $`6.56_{[5.74,7.40]}\times`$. The high
I-WERs observed for $`H_{2}`$ (28.39% and 47.36%) highlight the
limitations of manually crafted FST rules for complex normalization,
whereas our end-to-end approach learns these transformations directly
from data.

Regarding the cost of streaming, the causal encoder with non-streaming
inference ($`S_{C}`$) performs comparably to $`B_{2}`$, with marginal
WER increases of $`0.16_{[0.03,0.27]}`$% and $`0.08_{[-0.03,0.18]}`$%.
Under full streaming inference, $`S_{2}`$ remains robust: it incurs only
an $`8.85_{[-4.12,16.77]}`$% relative WER increase over $`B_{1}`$ in the
“With PnC” setting and improves performance by
$`13.51_{[-38.67,50.62]}`$% in the “Without PnC” setting. This
efficiency is attributed to our RTW policy and prefix-based training
augmentation. Compared to $`B_{2}`$, $`S_{2}`$ shows relative WER
increases of $`15.15_{[10.29,17.17]}`$% and $`23.07_{[0.65,47.15]}`$%,
quantifying the inherent trade-off when future context is unavailable to
resolve global dependencies. In terms of tagging accuracy, $`S_{2}`$
achieves a high $`F_{1}`$ score of 97.19%, comparable to the $`B_{2}`$
baseline, demonstrating its reliable boundary detection capability for
the RTW policy.

### 4.2 Impact of KV Caching and Copy–Paste Strategy

![](arxiv-2610-19492v1--db326dd1f4aa.figures/figure-2.webp)

Figure 2: Per-chunk latency breakdown (Encoder vs. Total) for four
streaming configurations across chunk sizes of 3, 4, and 5.

We benchmark per-chunk processing latency across configurations and
chunk sizes with a fixed look-ahead $`r=1`$. To simulate
high-concurrency production, we use a batch size of 100 with sequences
of 100 - 200 words on an NVIDIA H100 GPU. Average encoder and total
latencies are evaluated for four cumulative configurations, as shown in
Fig. 2:

- •
  BL (Baseline): Standard streaming without optimizations, requiring
  full decoder processing for all tokens.
- •
  CP (+Copy–Paste): Uses Copy–Paste strategy to bypass the decoder for
  $`O`$-tagged tokens, directly emit spoken-form text.
- •
  EC (+Encoder Caching): Adds incremental encoder KV caching to CP
  (corresponding to $`S_{1}`$ and $`S_{2}`$ variants).
- •
  DC (+Decoder Caching): The most optimized configuration, adding self-
  and cross-attention decoder KV caching to EC, representing the “fully
  streaming” $`S_{3}`$ and $`S_{4}`$ variants.

As illustrated in Fig. 2, the Copy–Paste strategy significantly reduces
total latency and mitigates hallucinations by eliminating redundant
decoder invocations for verbatim tokens. Encoder KV caching (EC)
dramatically reduces encoder latency from 54 ms to 10 ms, while decoder
KV caching (DC) further minimizes overhead by calculating updates only
for new tokens, bypassing the initial batched forward pass. Combined,
these optimizations achieve a $`3.43\times`$ speedup at $`c=5`$, where
total latency decreases from 206 ms (BL) to 60 ms (DC). The total
latency exhibits only marginal absolute increases across chunk sizes,
offering flexible trade-offs between latency and context.

While the “fully streaming” DC approach introduces a minor
training-inference mismatch, the performance impact is negligible.
Table 1 shows that $`S_{4}`$ (DC) maintains nearly identical performance
to $`S_{2}`$ (EC), with absolute WER increases of only
$`0.03_{[-0.05,0.09]}`$% and $`0.05_{[-0.03,0.11]}`$% across settings.
However, DC improves total per-chunk latency by 31.82% relative to EC at
$`c=5`$. We also evaluate the $`95^{th}`$ percentile ($`p_{95}`$)
latency to assess worst-case scenarios requiring heavy normalization.
The $`p_{95}`$ latency for DC is 165.5 ms, compared to 249.0 ms for EC.
Given that a chunk size of 5 typically represents 500 ms of audio, our
model ensures real-time processing even in the $`95^{th}`$ percentile,
demonstrating its capacity to serve 100 concurrent users in large-scale
deployments.

### 4.3 Impact of Look-ahead

As illustrated in Table 1, incorporating look-ahead context ($`S_{2}`$)
yields consistent performance gains across all primary metrics.
Specifically, the $`S_{2}`$ model achieves relative WER reductions of
$`19.31_{[18.62,19.89]}`$% and $`21.95_{[17.95,25.04]}`$% compared to
the zero-look-ahead variant ($`S_{1}`$) in the “With PnC” and “Without
PnC” settings, respectively. Similarly, the $`S_{4}`$ model achieves
relative WER reductions of $`18.88_{[18.50,19.29]}`$% and
$`22.47_{[20.62,23.99]}`$% compared to its zero-look-ahead counterpart
($`S_{3}`$). A comparable trend is observed in the hybrid streaming
architecture, where $`H_{2}`$ outperforms $`H_{1}`$ with WER reductions
of $`18.60_{[18.33,18.93]}`$% and $`9.09_{[8.79,9.42]}`$% in the
corresponding settings. These findings underscore the critical role of
look-ahead context during both training and inference.

### 4.5 Impact of Truncation Probability

![](arxiv-2610-19492v1--db326dd1f4aa.figures/figure-3.webp)

Figure 3: Impact of truncation probability $`p_{\text{trunc}}`$ on the
Word Error Rate for both offline and streaming inference of our model.

To assess our prefix-based training augmentation, we evaluate
performance on a validation subset across two modes: Streaming Inference
(RTW policy) and Offline Inference (causal encoder). All experiments use
$`c{=}3`$ and $`r{=}1`$. Figure 3 illustrates the sensitivity of WER to
the truncation probability $`p_{\text{trunc}}`$.

For offline inference, the model remains robust across
$`0.0\leq p_{\text{trunc}}\leq 0.9`$. While $`p_{\text{trunc}}=0`$
yields the lowest WER by avoiding termination-related distribution
shifts, performance stays stable as $`p_{\text{trunc}}`$ increases. A
sharp degradation occurs only at $`p_{\text{trunc}}=1.0`$, where the
lack of natural sentence endings during training impairs generalization
to full sequences.

In the streaming scenario, augmentation is essential. Without it
($`p_{\text{trunc}}=0`$), the WER exceeds 100% as the model fails to
predict the local \<EOS\> tokens required by the RTW policy. Introducing
prefix-based augmentation ($`p_{\text{trunc}}>0`$) immediately
stabilizes performance. As shown in Figure 3, increasing
$`p_{\text{trunc}}`$ correlates with improved streaming accuracy, as the
model learns to identify local boundaries from partial context.

The gap between offline and streaming modes narrows significantly for
$`p_{\text{trunc}}\in\{0.6,0.7,0.8,0.9\}`$. The optimal balance is
achieved at $`p_{\text{trunc}}=0.8`$, yielding the lowest streaming WER
while maintaining competitive offline performance. The
$`p_{\text{trunc}}=1.0`$ case is also detrimental to streaming,
suggesting that complete removal of full-sequence supervision harms
long-range dependency modeling. These results confirm that prefix-based
augmentation effectively bridges the training-inference discrepancy for
streaming ITN.

### 4.7 Limitations and Future Work

Despite the efficiency gains of our streaming ITN framework, several
limitations offer avenues for future research:

- •
  Phonetic Normalization and Failure Modes: While semiotic classes are
  handled effectively, qualitative error analysis reveals that
  performance on OOV expressions and foreign entities remains
  challenging. A common failure mode occurs when the tagger successfully
  identifies a phonetic span, but the decoder hallucinates an
  orthographically plausible but factually incorrect spelling for a rare
  foreign entity (e.g., generating “Marid” instead of “Madrid” due to
  phonetic ambiguity in the ASR output). Future work will investigate
  biasing mechanisms to integrate phonetic dictionaries directly into
  the decoding process to improve I-WER without disrupting the
  incremental KV cache or increasing latency.
- •
  Early Emission Strategy: The current RTW policy waits for a complete
  $`B-I`$ span before emission, which may introduce perceived lag for
  long sequences. We aim to explore predictive decoding strategies that
  allow for partial output as soon as sufficient linguistic context is
  captured, rather than waiting for span completion.
- •
  Edge Deployment Optimization: With 275M parameters, the model may
  limit deployment on resource-constrained devices. Future work will
  explore compression methods, such as knowledge distillation and
  quantization, to reduce memory footprint and improve throughput on
  low-power hardware.

## 5 Conclusion

This paper introduces an efficient end-to-end streaming ITN framework
leveraging pretrained text-to-text models. By integrating a chunk-based
causal encoder with an RTW decoding policy, we adapt non-streaming
architectures for real-time inference. To bridge the train-test
discrepancy, we propose a prefix-based training augmentation strategy
that ensures reliable local termination. Combined with KV caching and a
Copy–Paste mechanism, our system meets production-grade latency
requirements without sacrificing accuracy. Experimental results
demonstrate that our approach significantly outperforms traditional
hybrid streaming methods, achieving performance comparable to offline
baselines. Finally, evaluations across architectures and languages
confirm that the framework is scalable, backbone-agnostic, and
language-agnostic, offering a versatile solution for complex
normalization tasks.
