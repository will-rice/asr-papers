# CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition

Martijn Bartelds¹ Ananjan Nandi^(1¹¹footnotemark: 1) Moussa Koulako Bala Doumbouya¹\
Dan Jurafsky¹  Tatsunori Hashimoto¹  Karen Livescu²\
¹Department of Computer Science, Stanford University, Stanford, USA\
²Toyota Technological Institute at Chicago, Chicago, USA Equal contribution. Correspondence to bartelds@stanford.edu.

###### Abstract

Modern deep learning models often achieve high overall performance, but consistently fail on specific subgroups. Group distributionally robust optimization (group DRO) addresses this problem by minimizing the worst-group loss, but it fails when group losses misrepresent performance differences between groups. This is common in domains like speech, where the widely used connectionist temporal classification (CTC) loss not only scales with input length but also varies with linguistic and acoustic properties, leading to spurious differences between group losses. We present CTC-DRO, which addresses the shortcomings of the group DRO objective by smoothing the group weight update to prevent overemphasis on consistently high-loss groups, while using input length-matched batching to mitigate CTC’s scaling issues. We evaluate CTC-DRO on the task of multilingual automatic speech recognition (ASR) across five language sets from the diverse ML-SUPERB 2.0 benchmark. CTC-DRO consistently outperforms group DRO and CTC-based baseline models, reducing the worst-language error by up to $`47.1\%`$ and the average error by up to $`32.9\%`$. CTC-DRO can be applied to ASR with minimal computational costs, and, while motivated by multilingual ASR, offers the potential for reducing group disparities in other domains with similar challenges.

## 1 Introduction

State-of-the-art deep learning models are often highly accurate on training data populations, while consistently underperforming on specific subpopulations or groups (Hashimoto et al., [2018](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib41 "Fairness Without Demographics in Repeated Loss Minimization"); Duchi et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib77 "Distributionally robust losses for latent covariate mixtures")). One practical setting where this issue has very large effects is multilingual automatic speech recognition (ASR), where performance varies substantially between languages (Radford et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib21 "Robust Speech Recognition via Large-Scale Weak Supervision"); Pratap et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib30 "Scaling Speech Technology to 1,000+ Languages"); Shi et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib32 "ML-SUPERB 2.0: Benchmarking Multilingual Speech Models Across Modeling Constraints, Languages, and Datasets")). Such models, which jointly perform language identification (LID) and ASR in many languages, could help improve accessibility and increase digital participation for speakers worldwide (Besacier et al., [2014](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib17 "Automatic speech recognition for under-resourced languages: a survey")).

Distributionally robust optimization (DRO), particularly group DRO (Sagawa et al., [2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib45 "Distributionally Robust Neural Networks for Group Shifts: On the Importance of Regularization for Worst-Case Generalization")), has the potential to mitigate language disparities in multilingual ASR. Group DRO improves group robustness by up-weighting high-loss groups during training, and has been shown to outperform other approaches where the goal is to achieve high performance, even on the worst-performing group (Koh et al., [2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib20 "WILDS: A Benchmark of in-the-Wild Distribution Shifts")). However, it requires comparable training losses between groups to perform well (Oren et al., [2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib111 "Distributionally robust language modeling"); Sagawa et al., [2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib45 "Distributionally Robust Neural Networks for Group Shifts: On the Importance of Regularization for Worst-Case Generalization")), and this condition is often not met in ASR model training, because of differences in input length and speaker and acoustic characteristics across language-specific datasets.

In this paper, we focus on a training approach that has been successful on multilingual ASR benchmarks: pre-trained self-supervised models fine-tuned with the connectionist temporal classification (CTC; Graves et al., [2006](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib25 "Connectionist Temporal Classification: Labelling Unsegmented Sequence Data with Recurrent Neural Networks")) objective (Rouditchenko et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib69 "Comparison of Multilingual Self-Supervised and Weakly-Supervised Speech Pre-Training for Adaptation to Unseen Languages"); Chen et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib80 "Towards robust speech representation learning for thousands of languages"); Pratap et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib30 "Scaling Speech Technology to 1,000+ Languages")). CTC-based models built on encoders such as XLS-R (Babu et al., [2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib31 "XLS-R: Self-supervised Cross-lingual Speech Representation Learning at Scale")) and MMS (Pratap et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib30 "Scaling Speech Technology to 1,000+ Languages")) are widely adopted and offer advantages over autoregressive models like Whisper  (Radford et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib21 "Robust Speech Recognition via Large-Scale Weak Supervision")), including faster inference and reduced hallucinations (Koenecke et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib116 "Careless whisper: speech-to-text hallucination harms"); Peng et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib117 "OWSM-CTC: an open encoder-only speech foundation model for speech recognition, translation, and language identification")), which are crucial for many downstream applications. However, differences in CTC-based training losses due to length, speaker, and acoustics may lead to varying magnitudes and irreducible components of losses across different groups. As a result, the group DRO weights do not have the desired effect.

To address these issues, we present CTC-DRO, which optimizes a generalization of the group DRO objective, specifically by smoothing the up-weighting of high-loss groups. This new objective prevents overemphasis on groups with consistently and disproportionately high training losses. Also, we use length-matched group losses to mitigate the scaling properties of CTC. We evaluate CTC-DRO using language sets randomly selected from the ML-SUPERB 2.0 (Shi et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib32 "ML-SUPERB 2.0: Benchmarking Multilingual Speech Models Across Modeling Constraints, Languages, and Datasets")) benchmark collection, which includes multilingual speech data from 15 diverse corpora across multiple domains, speaking styles and recording conditions. In this setting, CTC-DRO models outperform both group DRO and CTC-based baseline models across five language sets, regardless of whether balanced or unbalanced amounts of training data per language are used during training. Specifically, CTC-DRO models reduce the error rate of the worst-performing language in all of the five sets, with improvements of up to $`47.1\%`$, while also improving the average performance across all languages by up to $`32.9\%`$. While motivated by multilingual ASR, CTC-DRO offers the potential for reducing group disparities in other domains with incomparable training losses between groups, such as medical applications (Ganz et al., [2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib115 "Assessing Bias in Medical AI"); Petersen et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib114 "The path toward equal performance in medical machine learning")). Our code and newly trained models are publicly available at [https://github.com/Bartelds/ctc-dro](https://github.com/Bartelds/ctc-dro).

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Group DRO

Given a family of models $`\Theta`$, loss function $`\ell`$ and training data $`(x,y)`$ drawn from empirical distribution $`\hat{P}`$, the standard training procedure for label prediction involves minimizing the expected loss over the training data:

``` math
\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\hat{P}}\left[\ell(\theta;(x,y))\right].
```

In contrast, group DRO aims to minimize the worst-case expected loss over a set of pre-defined groups or sub-distributions $`\{\hat{P}_{g}:g\in G\}`$ in the training data:

``` math
\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\Big\{\max_{g\in G}\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\hat{P}_{g}}\left[\ell(\theta;(x,y))\right]\Big\}.
```

Following Sagawa et al. ([2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib45 "Distributionally Robust Neural Networks for Group Shifts: On the Importance of Regularization for Worst-Case Generalization")), this objective can be rewritten as:

``` math
\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\Big\{\sup_{q\in\Delta_{|G|}}\sum_{g\in G}q_{g}\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\hat{P}_{g}}\left[\ell(\theta;(x,y))\right]\Big\},
```

where $`\Delta_{|G|}`$ is the $`|G|`$-dimensional probability simplex, and $`q_{g}`$ is a weight for group $`g\in G`$. Sagawa et al. ([2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib45 "Distributionally Robust Neural Networks for Group Shifts: On the Importance of Regularization for Worst-Case Generalization")) propose an online algorithm to optimize this objective, treating the problem as a minimax game and interleaving gradient ascent updates on $`q=\{q_{g}:g\in G\}`$ with gradient descent updates on $`\theta`$ for training data mini-batches (see Algorithm [2](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#alg2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ Appendix C Group DRO Algorithm ‣ CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition") in Appendix [C](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#A3 "Appendix C Group DRO Algorithm ‣ CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition")).

### 2.2 CTC

The CTC objective (Graves et al., [2006](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib25 "Connectionist Temporal Classification: Labelling Unsegmented Sequence Data with Recurrent Neural Networks")) defines a method to learn a mapping between an input sequence $`X=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{D})`$ and an output sequence $`Y=(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{U})`$ without requiring a known alignment between them, but assuming $`U\leq D`$ and a monotonic alignment. CTC uses a blank output token $`\epsilon`$ to handle $`x_{d}\in X`$ that do not map to any output symbol. Consider $`\mathcal{Z}`$, which is the set of all sequences of length $`D`$ that are composed of tokens from $`Y`$, and $`\epsilon`$. Each sequence $`Z\in\mathcal{Z}`$ is a potential alignment between $`X`$ and $`Y`$. CTC defines a collapsing function that merges consecutive, identical symbols and removes $`\epsilon`$ in an alignment $`Z`$. The set of alignments $`Z\in\mathcal{Z}`$ that collapse to $`Y`$ using this function forms the set of valid alignments $`\mathcal{A}(X,Y)`$. For example, a possible alignment $`Z\in\mathcal{A}(X,Y)`$ for $`D=2U+2`$ could be: $`[\epsilon,y_{1},\epsilon,y_{2},y_{2},\epsilon,\ldots,\epsilon,y_{U},\epsilon]`$. The conditional probability $`P_{CTC}(Z|X)`$ for any alignment $`Z`$ is computed as:

``` math
P_{CTC}(Z|X)=\prod_{d=1}^{D}p(z_{d}|X),
```

where $`Z=(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{D})`$ and $`p(z_{d}|X)`$ is the model’s predicted probability for symbol $`z_{d}\in Z`$ at time $`d`$. The predicted probability of the output sequence $`Y`$, $`P_{CTC}(Y|X)`$, is then computed by marginalizing over valid alignments $`Z\in\mathcal{A}(X,Y)`$:

``` math
P_{CTC}(Y|X)=\sum_{Z\in\mathcal{A}(X,Y)}P_{CTC}(Z|X).
```

The CTC loss function for $`(X,Y)`$ is then defined as:

``` math
\mathcal{L}_{CTC}=-\log P_{CTC}(Y\mid X).
```

### 2.3 Limitations of Group DRO Applied to CTC

The CTC loss, as defined in Equation [6](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#S2.E6 "In 2.2 CTC ‣ 2 Background ‣ CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition"), scales with the length of the input sequence $`D`$ and the length of the output sequence $`U`$. This scaling behavior occurs because $`P_{CTC}(Y|X)`$ is a marginalization over all valid alignments $`Z\in\mathcal{A}(X,Y)`$. Each alignment is a sequence of length $`D`$, which collapses to an output sequence of length $`U`$. As $`D`$ increases relative to $`U`$, the number of valid alignments increases as well (Graves et al., [2006](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib25 "Connectionist Temporal Classification: Labelling Unsegmented Sequence Data with Recurrent Neural Networks")). As each alignment’s probability is the product of $`D`$ per-element probabilities, its value typically decreases as $`D`$ increases. Therefore, their sum $`P_{CTC}(Y|X)`$ remains relatively low, as the per-alignment probabilities typically decrease faster than the number of valid alignments increases. In practice, this often results in a higher CTC loss for longer sequences.

Figure 1: Distribution of audio sample lengths across groups (languages) in our experimental setup.

Therefore, differences in the distribution of $`D`$ or $`U`$ between groups can result in CTC losses that are not directly comparable. For example, a long audio sample (large $`D`$) may have fewer errors overall, but a higher loss than a short audio sample (small $`D`$) if their transcription lengths $`U`$ are similar. In Figure [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#S2.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 2.3 Limitations of Group DRO Applied to CTC ‣ 2 Background ‣ CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition"), we illustrate the need to address this challenge, showing that there are large differences in the distribution of audio sample lengths $`D`$ across various groups (in this case, languages) included in our experimental setup, which we further detail in Section [4](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#S4 "4 Experiments ‣ CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition"). In this example, Spanish has a high proportion of long utterances, resulting in higher CTC losses. We find that the group DRO algorithm assigns a larger weight to this group, even though it is among the best groups in terms of downstream performance in our experiments, as shown in Section [5](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#S5 "5 Results ‣ CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition").

Importantly, simply scaling the CTC loss by $`D`$ or $`U`$ is insufficient to address the problem of incomparable CTC losses across languages (see Appendix [G](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#A7 "Appendix G Normalization Experiments ‣ CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition")). In addition, the CTC loss also varies due to differences in linguistic and acoustic properties across the pre-defined groups. This may cause variance in the irreducible component of the training loss (Malinin and Gales, [2018](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib81 "Predictive Uncertainty Estimation via Prior Networks")).

In line with observations made in past work (Oren et al., [2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib111 "Distributionally robust language modeling"); Słowik and Bottou, [2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib78 "On Distributionally Robust Optimization and Data Rebalancing")), we show that this inherent incomparability of losses across groups poses a critical challenge for group DRO. From Algorithm [2](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#alg2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ Appendix C Group DRO Algorithm ‣ CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition"), we compute the gradient ascent update to $`q_{g}`$, given group losses $`\mathcal{L}_{g}`$, as:

``` math
q_{g}\leftarrow\frac{q_{g}\cdot\exp(\eta_{q}\mathcal{L}_{g})}{\sum_{g}\left(q_{g}\cdot\exp(\eta_{q}\mathcal{L}_{g})\right)}.
```

This is equivalent to the Hedge algorithm (Slivkins, [2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib79 "Introduction to Multi-Armed Bandits")) update for the following maximization objective:

``` math
\max_{q\in\Delta_{|G|}}\sum_{g\in G}q_{g}\mathcal{L}_{g}.
```

Now consider a situation where one of the groups $`g^{\prime}`$ consistently has the highest training losses among all groups during training, presumably due to long audio samples or lengthy transcriptions, as well as the highest irreducible loss. This will result in its weight $`q_{g^{\prime}}`$ consistently receiving the largest increases $`\delta q_{g}`$ during training, as:

``` math
\delta q_{g}\propto q_{g}\exp(\eta_{q}\mathcal{L}_{g}).
```

As a result, $`q_{g^{\prime}}`$ will grow disproportionately large over the course of training, eventually drawing all the weight away from the other groups. This can result in other groups being under-weighted, which will cause a substantial decrease in their downstream performance (see Section [5](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#S5 "5 Results ‣ CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition")).

This observation highlights the problems caused by the fundamental mismatch between the computed loss and the ideal loss for use in group DRO. The ideal loss would measure only the excess loss beyond each group’s irreducible component and be length-normalized. However, in our setting, the irreducible component of the training loss is difficult to estimate, and, as we show in Appendix [G](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#A7 "Appendix G Normalization Experiments ‣ CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition"), simple per-utterance scaling does not provide a solution. Existing solutions, such as calibrating group losses or approximating disparities between groups with simpler models (Oren et al., [2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib111 "Distributionally robust language modeling"); Słowik and Bottou, [2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib78 "On Distributionally Robust Optimization and Data Rebalancing")), would either require a substantial increase in computational cost or a proxy for group difficulty, for which there is no reliable model for speech to our knowledge. Therefore, CTC remains inherently incompatible with group DRO.

## 3 CTC-DRO

To address the identified challenges, we propose a new training algorithm: CTC-DRO (Algorithm [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 3 CTC-DRO ‣ CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition")). This algorithm computes length-matched losses across groups to mitigate the scaling properties of CTC, and uses a generalization of the group DRO objective that introduces a new smoothed maximization objective for the group weights to prevent overemphasis on groups with consistently high training losses. Like group DRO, CTC-DRO has minimal computational costs, only keeping track of a single scalar weight for every group in the training data.

1: Input: Step sizes $`\eta_{q},\eta_{\theta}`$; smoothing parameter $`\alpha`$; loss function $`l`$; duration of each batch $`d`$; groups $`G=\{g\}`$; training data $`(x,y,g)\sim D`$; number of training steps $`T`$

2: Initialize $`\theta^{(0)}`$, $`\{q_{g}\}`$

3: Initialize gr_losses\[g\] $`=\emptyset`$ $`\forall g`$

4: for $`t=1`$ to $`T`$ do

5:  Sample $`g\sim G`$

6:  Sample $`\mathcal{B}=\{(x_{i},y_{i},g)\}_{i=1}^{B_{t}}\sim D`$ // selected such that $`\Sigma_{i=1}^{B_{t}}`$ $`duration(x_{i})\approx d`$

7:  for $`i=1`$ to $`B_{t}`$ do

8:   $`\ell_{i}=\ell(\theta^{(t-1)};(x_{i},y_{i}))`$

9:  end for

10:  $`\text{gr\_losses}[g]\leftarrow\text{gr\_losses}[g]\cup\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{B_{t}}\ell_{i}\right\}`$

11:  if $`\text{gr\_losses}[g]\neq\emptyset`$ $`\forall g`$ then

12:   for each group $`g`$ do

13:    $`\bar{\ell}_{g}=\dfrac{\sum_{\mathcal{L}\in\text{gr\_losses}[g]}\mathcal{L}}{|\text{gr\_losses}[g]|}`$

14:    $`q^{\prime}_{g}\leftarrow q_{g}\times\exp\left(\dfrac{\eta_{q}\bar{\ell}_{g}}{q_{g}+\alpha}\right)`$

15:    $`\text{gr\_losses}[g]\leftarrow\emptyset`$

16:   end for

17:   for each group $`g`$ do

18:    $`q_{g}\leftarrow\dfrac{q^{\prime}_{g}}{\sum_{g^{\prime}}q^{\prime}_{g^{\prime}}}`$ // gradient ascent on $`q`$

19:   end for

20:  end if

21:  $`\tilde{\mathcal{L}}=q_{g}|G|\sum_{i=1}^{B_{t}}\ell_{i}`$ // all data from same group $`g`$

22:  $`\theta^{(t)}\leftarrow\theta^{(t-1)}-\eta_{\theta}\nabla_{\theta}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}`$ // gradient descent on $`\theta`$

23: end for

### 3.1 Length-Matched Group Losses

To address incomparable CTC losses across groups due to different distributions of audio lengths, we ensure that the CTC loss for each group is computed using roughly equal total audio durations. Specifically, we create a new batch sampler that selects batches of audio samples and corresponding transcripts $`(x_{i},y_{i})`$, all from a single, randomly-selected group $`g`$, while ensuring that their total audio duration is as close to a fixed value (set as a hyperparameter) as possible.¹¹1Group utterances are iteratively added to a batch until the total duration meets or slightly exceeds the set target duration. Batches with a larger number of shorter audio samples tend to have a lower CTC loss per audio sample than batches with fewer, longer, audio samples. Therefore, we sum the utterance-level CTC losses in a batch (see line 10 in Algorithm [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 3 CTC-DRO ‣ CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition")) and update the group weights using this sum instead of the mean loss used in the group DRO algorithm. During training, these summed losses are tracked for each group, and a group weight update is performed only after at least one batch has been processed for every group. If a group is sampled multiple times before the update, the corresponding summed losses are averaged. This approach effectively increases the batch size for computing the group weight update.

Also, we multiply the losses by the total number of groups (line 21 in Algorithm [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 3 CTC-DRO ‣ CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition")) before performing gradient descent on the model parameters. This ensures that the training losses with CTC-DRO are comparable to a model trained without CTC-DRO, removing the need to tune shared hyperparameters, such as the learning rate, separately for both training algorithms.

### 3.2 Smoothed Maximization Objective

We propose a new method for updating the group weights, which addresses group DRO’s tendency to assign a disproportionately large weight to groups with consistently high training losses (see Section [2.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#S2.SS3 "2.3 Limitations of Group DRO Applied to CTC ‣ 2 Background ‣ CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition")). This approach also helps mitigate the scaling properties of CTC related to transcription length, which cannot be adequately resolved by normalizing for transcript length (see Appendix [G](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#A7 "Appendix G Normalization Experiments ‣ CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition")).

Our proposed update rule introduces a smoothing hyperparameter $`\alpha`$ (see Algorithm [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 3 CTC-DRO ‣ CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition")):

``` math
q_{g}\leftarrow\frac{q_{g}.\exp({\eta_{q}\frac{\mathcal{L}_{g}}{q_{g}+\alpha}})}{\sum_{g\in G}(q_{g}.\exp({\eta_{q}\frac{\mathcal{L}_{g}}{q_{g}+\alpha}}))}.
```

As $`\alpha\to 0`$, the update becomes increasingly more sensitive to the current group weight relative to the group loss. This causes groups with higher weights to receive smaller updates, resulting in a more uniform distribution of the group weights. In contrast, as $`\alpha`$ increases, updates depend more on the group loss compared to the group weight, increasing the group weights more strongly for groups with higher losses. In fact, when $`\alpha\to\infty`$, the update rule reduces to:

``` math
q_{g}\leftarrow\frac{q_{g}.\exp({\eta_{q}\frac{\mathcal{L}_{g}}{\alpha}})}{\sum_{g\in G}(q_{g}.\exp({\eta_{q}\frac{\mathcal{L}_{g}}{\alpha}}))},
```

recovering the form of the group DRO update.

This update rule has several desirable properties. First, the updates to $`q_{g}`$ are smoother, because they are inversely proportional to the current $`q_{g}`$, while still being proportional to the loss $`\mathcal{L}_{g}`$. This discourages any single group from having a disproportionately large weight $`q_{g}`$ relative to its group loss, leading to a more balanced distribution of the group weights. Second, the update rule adjusts for differences in group weights when the CTC losses are similar. Specifically, if two groups with different $`q_{g}`$ have similar CTC losses, the group with the lower $`q_{g}`$ receives a larger update. This helps prevent under-training of lower-weighted groups by reducing the gap between the group weights over time.

Along with these desirable properties, we demonstrate that our new objective does not change the fundamental behavior of the group DRO objective, assigning higher weights to groups with higher losses. Following the Hedge algorithm (Slivkins, [2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib79 "Introduction to Multi-Armed Bandits")), Equation [10](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#S3.E10 "In 3.2 Smoothed Maximization Objective ‣ 3 CTC-DRO ‣ CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition") optimizes the following generalization of the group DRO maximization objective (Equation [8](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#S2.E8 "In 2.3 Limitations of Group DRO Applied to CTC ‣ 2 Background ‣ CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition")):

``` math
\max_{q\in\Delta_{|G|}}\sum_{g\in G}\log(q_{g}+\alpha)\mathcal{L}_{g}.
```

Expanding the conditions for the probability simplex $`\Delta_{|G|}`$ ($`\sum_{g}q_{g}=1`$, $`q_{g}\geq 0`$ $`\forall g`$) and taking the Lagrangian of Equation [12](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#S3.E12 "In 3.2 Smoothed Maximization Objective ‣ 3 CTC-DRO ‣ CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition"), we obtain:

``` math
\mathcal{J}=\sum_{g\in G}\log(q_{g}+\alpha)\mathcal{L}_{g}+\lambda(1-\sum_{g\in G}q_{g})-\sum_{g\in G}\lambda_{g}q_{g},
```

where $`\lambda`$ and $`\lambda_{g}`$ are Lagrange multipliers and $`\lambda_{g}\geq 0`$ for all $`g`$. To find the optimal $`q_{g}`$, we calculate the partial derivative of $`\mathcal{J}`$ with respect to $`q_{g}`$ and set it to 0:

``` math
\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}}{\partial q_{g}}=\frac{\mathcal{L}_{g}}{q_{g}+\alpha}-\lambda-\lambda_{g}=0\quad\implies\quad q_{g}=\frac{\mathcal{L}_{g}}{\lambda+\lambda_{g}}-\alpha.
```

Assuming $`q_{g}>0`$ for all $`g`$, complementary slackness ($`\lambda_{g}q_{g}=0`$ with $`\lambda_{g}\geq 0`$ for all $`g`$) implies $`\lambda_{g}=0`$ for all $`g`$ and:

``` math
q_{g}=\frac{\mathcal{L}_{g}}{\lambda}-\alpha.
```

Since $`\sum_{g}q_{g}=1`$:

``` math
1=\sum_{g}(\frac{\mathcal{L}_{g}}{\lambda}-\alpha)\quad\implies\quad\lambda=\frac{\sum_{g}\mathcal{L}_{g}}{1+|G|\alpha}
```

Substituting in Equation [15](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#S3.E15 "In 3.2 Smoothed Maximization Objective ‣ 3 CTC-DRO ‣ CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition"):

``` math
q_{g}=\frac{\mathcal{L}_{g}(1+|G|\alpha)}{\sum_{g}\mathcal{L}_{g}}-\alpha\quad\implies\quad q_{g}+\alpha\propto\frac{\mathcal{L}_{g}}{\sum_{g^{\prime}}\mathcal{L}_{g^{\prime}}}
```

Thus, the optimal weight for a group ($`q_{g}`$) increases with its loss ($`\mathcal{L}_{g}`$), since $`q_{g}+\alpha`$ is proportional to $`\mathcal{L}_{g}`$ and both $`\alpha`$ and $`\sum_{g^{\prime}}\mathcal{L}_{g^{\prime}}`$ are constant with respect to $`g`$.

## 4 Experiments

We fine-tune the existing, self-supervised multilingual XLS-R and MMS models on the task of multilingual ASR (formulated as a joint task of ASR and LID) using data from the ML-SUPERB 2.0 benchmark (more on the dataset in Section [4.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#S4.SS1 "4.1 Dataset ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition")). These models are licensed under Apache 2.0 and CC-BY-NC-4.0, respectively. Following the setup of ML-SUPERB 2.0, we add two Transformer layers and a softmax layer on top of the pre-trained models to predict a language token followed by character sequences using CTC. We do not use a separate LID head or loss, and update all model parameters. The models we choose have shown the best performance on ML-SUPERB 2.0 (Shi et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib32 "ML-SUPERB 2.0: Benchmarking Multilingual Speech Models Across Modeling Constraints, Languages, and Datasets")), outperforming other models like Whisper (Radford et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib21 "Robust Speech Recognition via Large-Scale Weak Supervision")). The two pre-trained models share the same architecture and training objective (Baevski et al., [2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib26 "wav2vec 2.0: A Framework for Self-Supervised Learning of Speech Representations")), but their training data differs: XLS-R is pre-trained on roughly 436K hours of speech in 128 languages, while MMS is pre-trained on 491K hours of speech in 1406 languages.

We train the models using three approaches. First, our baseline models use the joint ASR and LID training setup adopted in ML-SUPERB 2.0 (as described above), with the addition of our new batch sampler that computes length-matched group losses. Second, we fine-tune models using our proposed CTC-DRO algorithm. Third, we train models using the group DRO algorithm (replicating its original batch sampler) for comparison. When training both CTC-DRO and group DRO models, the groups correspond to the languages in our training datasets (see Section [4.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#S4.SS1 "4.1 Dataset ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition")).

We mostly follow the hyperparameters used by Babu et al. ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib31 "XLS-R: Self-supervised Cross-lingual Speech Representation Learning at Scale")), Pratap et al. ([2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib30 "Scaling Speech Technology to 1,000+ Languages")), and in ML-SUPERB 2.0, but train for 40 epochs, retaining the model checkpoint with the lowest loss on the development data, accumulate gradients across 16 batches, set the batch duration hyperparameter (Algorithm [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 3 CTC-DRO ‣ CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition")) so that batches fit within our NVIDIA A6000 GPU memory, leading to batches of roughly 50 seconds of audio (more details in Appendix [F](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#A6 "Appendix F Results ‣ CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition")), and tune the learning rate of the baseline models on our development data. We also use this learning rate to train models with CTC-DRO and group DRO. Lastly, for the CTC-DRO and group DRO models, we tune the DRO-specific hyperparameters on the development set as well, specifically $`\eta_{q}\in\{10^{-3},10^{-4}\}`$ and $`\alpha\in\{0.1,0.5,1\}`$.

### 4.1 Dataset

We use the ML-SUPERB 2.0 dataset for our experiments. This dataset belongs to an established benchmark where a number of multilingual ASR models have already been compared. It has broad coverage of 141 languages sourced from 15 corpora, and contains substantial variation in domains and recording environments as well as more natural speech compared to smaller, translation focused corpora, such as FLEURS (Conneau et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib48 "FLEURS: FEW-Shot Learning Evaluation of Universal Representations of Speech")). For each language-corpus pair, there is between one and nine hours of training data available, as well as 10 minutes each for development and test data. While we focus on studying relatively small training data sizes, prior work has shown that ASR performance differences between languages persist even when the amount of training data increases substantially (e.g., see Radford et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib21 "Robust Speech Recognition via Large-Scale Weak Supervision")).

For our main experiments, we use a balanced data setup by randomly selecting five diverse sets of groups from ML-SUPERB 2.0, each consisting of six language-corpus pairs, matching the number of groups used in Sagawa et al. ([2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib45 "Distributionally Robust Neural Networks for Group Shifts: On the Importance of Regularization for Worst-Case Generalization")). We thus have one hour of training data, and 10 minutes of development and test data available for each language-corpus pair in each set. The selection of language-corpus pairs is based on the character error rates (CERs) of the best-performing model configuration from ML-SUPERB 2.0. Specifically, for each set, we randomly select two language-corpus pairs from the bottom 10 percentile of CERs, two language-corpus pairs from the top 10 percentile of CERs, and two language-corpus pairs with CERs between the 10th and 90th percentiles.

For the first two language sets, we also investigate the effect of using additional training data in an unbalanced setup, as most languages in these sets have more than one hour of training data available. We show more dataset details in Appendix [D](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#A4 "Appendix D Datasets ‣ CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition").

### 4.2 Evaluation

We compare the performance of CTC-DRO models to the baseline and group DRO models. They are evaluated using the standard CER metric on the test sets from the five language sets (metric details in Appendix [E](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#A5 "Appendix E Evaluation Metric Details ‣ CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition")). We also report the LID accuracy for completeness. We report the CER of the worst-performing language (our primary metric), as well as the average CER across languages. For the CTC-DRO and group DRO models, we report the performance of the model checkpoint with the largest CER improvement on the worst-performing language relative to the baseline on the development set.

### 6.1 Ablation Study

We find that removing either component from CTC-DRO leads to a substantial decrease in performance (see Table [3](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#S6.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 6 Analysis ‣ CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition"); we present detailed results in Appendix [F](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#A6 "Appendix F Results ‣ CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition")). Specifically, the CER of the worst-performing language increases by up to $`171.6\%`$ and the average CER by up to $`302.9\%`$ compared to a model trained using the complete CTC-DRO algorithm. We also find that the smoothed maximization objective has the stronger effect on reducing both the CER of the worst-performing language and the average CER. Note that removing the smoothed maximization objective from CTC-DRO is not similar to training baseline models, as this configuration still uses the group DRO weight update mechanism (see Appendix [C](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#A3 "Appendix C Group DRO Algorithm ‣ CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition")).

### 6.2 Comparison of Group Weights

To analyze the behavior of group DRO and CTC-DRO models during training, we plot the group weights for all languages in set 5 throughout training in Figure [2](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#S6.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 6.2 Comparison of Group Weights ‣ 6 Analysis ‣ CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition") (see Appendix [F](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#A6 "Appendix F Results ‣ CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition") for additional plots). The group weights of the group DRO model fluctuate substantially during training, reaching values close to 0 or 1 at various stages of training. For extended periods of training with group DRO, the group weights are heavily concentrated on a single language, causing the weights for all other languages to reach values close to 0.

In contrast, the group weights of the CTC-DRO model are distributed more evenly across all languages throughout training. The group weights for each language also fluctuate substantially less during training compared to group DRO. The only languages with group weights dropping below 0.1 at any point are German and English, both of which have low CERs on the development set. Importantly, the weight of Japanese (worst-performing) consistently remains among the top two highest group weights.

### 6.3 Scalability to More Groups

To analyze the impact of scaling the number of languages, we conduct additional experiments on 18 languages (our languages from set 1 plus 12 randomly sampled extra languages). We find that CTC-DRO maintains its effectiveness at improving worst-language performance, reducing the worst-language CER by 8.9% for MMS and 9.2% for XLS-R in the balanced data setting compared to baseline models. ## 7 Related Work

#### Robustness to distribution shifts

Prior work categorizes distribution shifts as domain generalization (Quiñonero-Candela et al., [2008](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib82 "Dataset Shift in Machine Learning"); Hendrycks et al., [2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib85 "The Many Faces of Robustness: A Critical Analysis of Out-of-Distribution Generalization"); Santurkar et al., [2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib84 "BREEDS: Benchmarks for Subpopulation Shift")), where train and test data domains have no overlap, or subpopulation shifts (Dixon et al., [2018](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib86 "Measuring and Mitigating Unintended Bias in Text Classification"); Oren et al., [2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib111 "Distributionally robust language modeling"); Sagawa et al., [2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib45 "Distributionally Robust Neural Networks for Group Shifts: On the Importance of Regularization for Worst-Case Generalization")), where train and test data come from the same domains, but do not necessarily appear in the same proportions (Koh et al., [2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib20 "WILDS: A Benchmark of in-the-Wild Distribution Shifts")). Our experimental setup is an example of a subpopulation shift, as all test languages are included in the training data for the models.

Methods for robust generalization are commonly categorized into three groups. Domain invariance methods aim to learn feature representations that are consistent across domains (groups) by encouraging similar feature distributions across domains (Tzeng et al., [2014](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib91 "Deep Domain Confusion: Maximizing for Domain Invariance"); Long et al., [2015](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib92 "Learning Transferable Features with Deep Adaptation Networks"); Ganin et al., [2016](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib93 "Domain-adversarial training of neural networks"); Sun and Saenko, [2016](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib43 "Deep coral: correlation alignment for deep domain adaptation")). Other approaches use invariant prediction methods (Meinshausen and Bühlmann, [2015](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib95 "Maximin effects in inhomogeneous large-scale data"); Peters et al., [2016](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib94 "Causal Inference by using Invariant Prediction: Identification and Confidence Intervals"); Arjovsky et al., [2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib24 "Invariant Risk Minimization"); Rothenhäusler et al., [2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib96 "Anchor regression: Heterogeneous data meet causality")) from the causal inference literature. In contrast, DRO explicitly minimizes the worst-case loss over an uncertainty set, which is typically defined as a divergence ball around the training distribution (Namkoong and Duchi, [2016](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib97 "Stochastic Gradient Methods for Distributionally Robust Optimization with f-divergences"); Bertsimas et al., [2018](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib99 "Data-driven robust optimization"); Esfahani and Kuhn, [2018](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib98 "Data-driven distributionally robust optimization using the Wasserstein metric: Performance guarantees and tractable reformulations"); Duchi and Namkoong, [2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib100 "Variance-based Regularization with Convex Objectives"); Oren et al., [2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib111 "Distributionally robust language modeling"); Sagawa et al., [2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib45 "Distributionally Robust Neural Networks for Group Shifts: On the Importance of Regularization for Worst-Case Generalization")). Our work builds upon group DRO (Sagawa et al., [2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib45 "Distributionally Robust Neural Networks for Group Shifts: On the Importance of Regularization for Worst-Case Generalization")), since it has outperformed other approaches in settings with subpopulation shifts (Koh et al., [2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib20 "WILDS: A Benchmark of in-the-Wild Distribution Shifts")).

#### Robust ASR

Prior work on robustness in ASR primarily focuses on quantifying or addressing biases related to accent, age, dialect, gender, and race  (Tatman, [2017](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib112 "Gender and dialect bias in YouTube’s automatic captions"); Koenecke et al., [2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib42 "Racial disparities in automated speech recognition"); Markl, [2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib101 "Language variation and algorithmic bias: understanding algorithmic bias in British English automatic speech recognition"); Martin and Wright, [2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib102 "Bias in Automatic Speech Recognition: The Case of African American Language"); Ngueajio and Washington, [2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib28 "Hey asr system! why aren’t you more inclusive?"); Feng et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib60 "Towards inclusive automatic speech recognition"); Harris et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib103 "Modeling gender and dialect bias in automatic speech recognition")). Methods to mitigate these biases include data balancing (Dheram et al., [2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib83 "Toward Fairness in Speech Recognition: Discovery and mitigation of performance disparities")) and fairness-promoting training methods (Sarı et al., [2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib106 "Counterfactually Fair Automatic Speech Recognition"); Zhang et al., [2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib105 "Mitigating bias against non-native accents"); Veliche and Fung, [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib104 "Improving fairness and robustness in end-to-end speech recognition through unsupervised clustering")). These methods are not appropriate for reducing ASR language disparities, as they require large amounts of training data unavailable for most languages or have methodological constraints that prohibit direct application to a multilingual setting. Alternative approaches focused on multilingual settings use architectural and representation level improvements to include language information (Chen et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib119 "Improving Massively Multilingual ASR with Auxiliary CTC Objectives"); Lu et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib120 "CA-SSLR: Condition-Aware Self-Supervised Learning Representation for Generalized Speech Processing")). These methods improve multilingual ASR performance by conditioning the model on language identity through auxiliary CTC objectives or conditional adapters. CTC-DRO differs in its objective, directly targeting worst-group performance through robust optimization rather than architectural modifications, but could in principle be combined with such approaches. Gao et al. ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib19 "Domain Generalization for Language-Independent Automatic Speech Recognition")) explored DRO for training language-independent speech recognition models, and reported negative results.

#### Comparison with other approaches

We consider several alternative approaches but find them unsuitable for our multilingual ASR setting. For approaches that calibrate group losses or approximate disparities with simpler models (Oren et al., [2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib111 "Distributionally robust language modeling"); Słowik and Bottou, [2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib78 "On Distributionally Robust Optimization and Data Rebalancing")), Section [2.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#S2.SS3 "2.3 Limitations of Group DRO Applied to CTC ‣ 2 Background ‣ CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition") explains that they would require substantially more computation or a proxy for group difficulty, for which there is no reliable model for speech. For other DRO variants that update on group-averaged losses (e.g., Lokhande et al., [2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib122 "Towards Group Robustness in the Presence of Partial Group Labels")), CTC losses remain not directly comparable across groups (see Section [2.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#S2.SS3 "2.3 Limitations of Group DRO Applied to CTC ‣ 2 Background ‣ CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition")), and loss normalization does not solve this problem (as shown in Appendix [G](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#A7 "Appendix G Normalization Experiments ‣ CTC-DRO: Robust Optimization for Reducing Language Disparities in Speech Recognition")). Alternatively, group-aware reinforcement learning methods (e.g., Tjandra et al., [2018](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib118 "Sequence-to-Sequence Asr Optimization Via Reinforcement Learning")) could be used, but decoding during training and optimizing a sequence-level reward such as CER would be substantially more expensive than the scalar group-weight update used by CTC-DRO. To the best of our knowledge, our work is the first to propose a robust optimization method that successfully reduces cross-lingual performance disparities in ASR.

## 8 Conclusion

CTC-DRO, our robust optimization approach motivated by multilingual ASR, addresses group DRO’s inability to handle group losses that do not accurately reflect performance differences between groups. When applied to data from an established multilingual ASR and LID benchmark, CTC-DRO outperformed baseline CTC-based and group DRO models, reducing the worst-language CER across all sets and improving average CER and LID accuracy in almost all cases. Our analysis showed that this result can be attributed to the smoothed maximization objective and length-matched batching that balance and stabilize the group weights.

While performance disparities are reduced in our approach, they are not eliminated. The improvements may be sufficient to make ASR useful for more languages than before, but additional work is needed before ASR is truly practical for many more languages. A promising direction for future work is to automatically learn data groupings, which removes the need for pre-defined groups that may be unknown or incomplete, as well as applying CTC-DRO to pre-training. Extending CTC-DRO to code-switching scenarios is another promising direction (e.g., see  Liu et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01777v3#bib.bib121 "Enhancing Code-Switching Speech Recognition With Interactive Language Biases")).

Also, we believe the principles underlying CTC-DRO have broader applicability. The smoothed maximization objective could in principle be applied to any setting with group-level losses, suggesting potential extensions to other architectures, loss functions, and groupings. For example, tasks that use variable-length sequences as input data and therefore face similar challenges, such as text classification and video transcription, could potentially benefit from our algorithm, enabling more inclusive processing of other data modalities as well.

The CER can be computed by comparing the system generated and reference transcripts using the formula: ``` math \text{CER}=\frac{I+S+D}{N}\times 100, ``` where $`I`$ is the number of insertions, $`S`$ the number of substitutions, and $`D`$ the number of deletions in a minimum edit distance alignment between the reference and system output, and $`N`$ is the number of characters in the reference transcript. We evaluate four approaches on language set 1 (balanced setup): (1) group DRO with losses normalized by the number of frames in the sequence (Frame); (2) group DRO with losses normalized by the number of target labels (Target); (3) CTC-DRO without our new batch sampler that computes length-matched group losses (instead using the group DRO batch sampler) and with losses normalized by the number of frames in the sequence (Frame; no length-matched); (4) CTC-DRO without our new batch sampler that computes length-matched group losses (instead using the group DRO batch sampler) and with losses normalized by the number of target labels (Target; no length-matched). These experiments follow the same experimental setup used for our main experiments. Normalizing each utterance’s loss by its own length (number of input frames or target labels) also scales the corresponding gradient. The longest utterances are most strongly downweighted, while the gradients of shorter utterances retain relatively more weight within a batch. Importantly, longer sequences inherently provide more information and should influence the gradients more, so reducing their gradients limits the model’s ability to learn from the most informative examples. We note that a different global learning rate would not compensate for this per-utterance imbalance.
