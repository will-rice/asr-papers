# Gradient Norm-based Fine-Tuning for Backdoor Defense in Automatic Speech Recognition

Nanjun Zhou^(1,2,\*), Weilin Lin^(1,\*), Li Liu^(1,$`\dagger`$)\
¹The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology (Guangzhou), Guangzhou, China\
²South China University of Technology, Guangzhou, China \* Equal Contribution.$`\dagger`$ Corresponds to Li Liu ([avrillliu@hkust-gz.edu.cn](mailto:avrillliu@hkust-gz.edu.cn)).

###### Abstract

Backdoor attacks have posed a significant threat to the security of deep neural networks (DNNs). Despite considerable strides in developing defenses against backdoor attacks in the visual domain, the specialized defenses for the audio domain remain empty. Furthermore, the defenses adapted from the visual to audio domain demonstrate limited effectiveness. To fill this gap, we propose Gradient Norm-based Fine-Tuning (GN-FT), a novel defense strategy against the attacks in the audio domain, based on the observation from the corresponding backdoored models. Specifically, we first empirically find that the backdoored neurons exhibit greater gradient values compared to other neurons, while clean neurons stay the lowest. On this basis, we fine-tune the backdoored model by incorporating the gradient norm regularization, aiming to weaken and reduce the backdoored neurons. We further approximate the loss computation for lower implementation costs. Extensive experiments on two speech recognition datasets across five models demonstrate the superior performance of our proposed method. To the best of our knowledge, this work is the first specialized and effective defense against backdoor attacks in the audio domain.

###### Index Terms:

AI Security, Backdoor Defense, Automatic Speech Recognition, Fine-Tuning.

## I Introduction

In recent years, deep neural networks (DNNs) have seen extensive application across a wide range of fields, including face recognition \[[1](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib1), [2](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib2), [3](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib3)\], autonomous driving \[[4](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib4), [5](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib5)\] in the visual domain, and automatic speech recognition \[[6](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib6), [7](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib7)\] in the audio domain. However, with advancements in technology, backdoor attacks \[[8](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib8)\] have emerged as a severe security concern, threatening the safety of DNNs. In backdoor attacks, attackers inject a specific trigger pattern into a portion of training dataset to poison the data. Models trained on such poisoned datasets, known as backdoored models, behave normally when presented with clean data. Conversely, they maliciously misclassify data that contains the trigger pattern to a predefined target label, which is termed as backdoored effect. To avoid this effect, solutions on either the poisoned-input detection (data-level) or the backdoored-model repairing (model-level) are necessary \[[9](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib9)\].

Up to now, numerous studies have been dedicated to developing defenses against backdoor attacks, which have achieved significant results \[[10](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib10), [11](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib11), [12](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib12), [13](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib13), [14](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib14), [15](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib15)\]. However, these defense methods are mostly designed for the visual domain, and no specialized defense is proposed against the backdoor attacks in the audio domain. Due to the different characteristics between the two domains, _e.g._, audio signals own larger information density as spectrum format compared to the RGB images, the existing defense methods adapted from the visual domain \[[10](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib10), [16](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib16), [17](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib17)\] demonstrate limited performance against the audio backdoor attacks. As illustrated in Table [I](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#S4.T1 "Table I ‣ IV-B Main Results ‣ IV Experiments ‣ Gradient Norm-based Fine-Tuning for Backdoor Defense in Automatic Speech Recognition") and Table [II](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#S4.T2 "Table II ‣ IV-B Main Results ‣ IV Experiments ‣ Gradient Norm-based Fine-Tuning for Backdoor Defense in Automatic Speech Recognition"), the model-level defense adapted from the visual domain, Fine-Pruning (FP) \[[10](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib10)\], fails completely in the audio domain. Therefore, in this work, we aim to design the first defense method specifically targeted at audio backdoor attacks.

![Refer to caption](https://arxiv.org/html/x1.png)

Figure 1: Overview of our proposed method (GN-FT).

To investigate the characteristics of the backdoored models in the audio domain, _i.e._, audio-backdoored models, we split its neurons into different types as in \[[18](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib18)\] and further observe their learning behaviors. Specifically, the neurons are categorized into clean neurons, backdoored neurons, hybrid neurons, and redundant neurons according to their loss changes on both clean and backdoor tasks¹¹1Clean task represents the normal classification task on clean samples. Similarly, backdoor task indicates the task on poisoned samples. . Note that backdoored neurons and hybrid neurons are the primary contributors to the backdoor task, and our goal is to weaken their functionality. The gradients with clean inputs on different neuron types are shown in Fig. [2](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ I Introduction ‣ Gradient Norm-based Fine-Tuning for Backdoor Defense in Automatic Speech Recognition"), we can observe that for most clean inputs, backdoored neurons and hybrid neurons exhibit larger gradient values than clean neurons, which solely contribute to clean task.

Based on this observation, we propose Gradient Norm-based Fine-Tuning (GN-FT), where a gradient norm regularization term is added to the original loss function. By doing so, the learning process attempts to suppress the high-gradient backdoored neurons and hybrid neurons, resulting in a repaired clean model after fine-tuning. Considering computational efficiency, we adopt the approximation scheme introduced in \[[19](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib19)\]. Extensive experiments demonstrate that our method significantly outperforms FP in terms of defense effectiveness.

In summary, the main contributions of this work are threefold: 1) We observe that the backdoored neurons in the audio-backdoored models exhibit greater gradient values than others. 2) We propose a gradient-regularized fine-tuning method to effectively mitigate backdoored effect, marking the first specialized defense technique for backdoor attacks in the audio domain. 3) Extensive experiments across two datasets, five models, and seven attacks, show that our proposed method consistently achieves state-of-the-art performance.

## II Related work

Backdoor Attacks. In backdoor attacks \[[8](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib8)\], an attacker injects a specific pattern, known as trigger, into a portion of the training data and assigns these samples a target label. The resulting backdoored model performs normally on clean data but misclassifies inputs with the trigger to the target label. Most backdoor attack techniques \[[8](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib8), [22](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib22), [23](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib23), [24](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib24), [25](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib25), [26](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib26)\] are designed for the visual domain, among which classic examples include BadNets \[[8](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib8)\] and Blended \[[22](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib22)\]. In the audio domain, Ultrasonic attack \[[27](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib27)\] is a representative method for automatic speech recognition tasks, where the attacker uses an ultrasonic signal as the backdoor trigger. To enable attacks in a physical scenario, naturally occurring sounds are chosen as triggers in DABA \[[28](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib28)\]. Various audio-specific methods \[[20](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib20), [29](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib29)\] have also been devised to increase the stealthiness of attacks. Recently, a stealthy attack FlowMur \[[30](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib30)\] was introduced, where it trains a model to generate triggers while ensuring consistency between the target label and ground truth.

Backdoor Defenses. According to \[[9](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib9)\], backdoor defenses can be categorized into data-level and model-level approaches. Data-level defenses aim to identify and remove poisoned data from the dataset, while model-level defenses attempt to mitigate backdoored effect in a well-trained backdoored model using a small amount of clean data. In the audio domain, existing backdoor defenses are all adaptations from the visual domain and are primarily data-level \[[16](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib16), [17](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib17)\]. FP \[[10](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib10)\] is the only adapted model-level defense for audio-backdoored models, which prunes neurons with low activation on clean data and then fine-tunes the pruned model. However, FP fails to effectively defend against most audio backdoor attacks. In this work, we address this issue by proposing a gradient-regularized fine-tuning technique from the model-level perspective, which is the first specialized defense for the audio-backdoored models.

## III Proposed Method

### III-A Problem Formulation

Threat Models. We assume that the attackers have full access to the training set, and they poison it by injecting a trigger into a small amount of randomly selected samples, indicated by the poisoning ratio. The attackers aim to train the model with the poisoned training set so that it misclassifies poisoned data as the target label $`y_{t}`$, while functioning normally on clean data. We denote the model as $`F`$ with $`L`$ layers, where $`f^{(i)}`$ parameterized as $`{\mathbf{θ}}^{(i)}`$ denotes $`i`$-th layer of the model. Considering the convolutional layer, the weights of neurons in the $`i`$-th layer can be denoted as $`{\{{{\mathbf{θ}}^{(i,j)} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{c_{i - 1} \times h \times w}}\}}_{1 \leq j \leq c_{i}}`$, where $`c_{i}`$, $`h`$ and $`w`$ represent the number of neurons in $`f^{(i)}`$, the height and width of the convolutional kernel, respectively.

Defense Setting. The defender aims to eliminate the backdoored effect while preserving the model performance on clean data. Following the previous model-level defense settings \[[10](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib10)\], we assume that only 5% of clean data is accessible to the defender for conducting defense, denoted as $`\mathcal{D}_{c}`$.

### III-B Observations on Audio-Backdoored Models

Classification of Neurons in Backdoored Models. In line with the neuron types defined in \[[18](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib18)\], we categorize the neurons in backdoored models based on pruning and loss change, where pruning j-th neuron in the i-th layer of the model means setting $`{\mathbf{θ}}^{(i,j)}`$ to 0. The loss change of a neuron is defined as the difference between the loss values after and before pruning for the same inputs. To be specific, Clean Loss Change (CLC) and Backdoor Loss Change (BLC) can be formulated as:

$`\text{CLC}{({\mathbf{θ}},i,j)}`$$`= {\mathbb{E}}_{{({\mathbf{x}},y)} \in \mathcal{D}_{c}}\left\lbrack \mathcal{L}{(F{({\mathbf{x}};{\mathbf{θ}} \mid {\mathbf{θ}}^{(i,j)} = 0)},y)} \right.`$$`\left. - \mathcal{L}{(F{({\mathbf{x}};{\mathbf{θ}})},y)} \right\rbrack`$$`\text{BLC}{({\mathbf{θ}},i,j)}`$$`= {\mathbb{E}}_{{({\mathbf{x}}, \ast )} \in {\mathcal{D}_{c},y_{t}}}\left\lbrack \mathcal{L}{(F{(\delta{({\mathbf{x}})};{\mathbf{θ}} \mid {\mathbf{θ}}^{(i,j)} = 0)},y_{t})} \right.`$$`\left. - \mathcal{L}{(F{(\delta{({\mathbf{x}})};{\mathbf{θ}})},y_{t})} \right\rbrack`$

where $`\mathcal{D}_{c}`$ is the given clean data for defense; $`\delta{( \cdot )}`$ is the poisoning function of the attack method; $`y_{t}`$ is a predefined target label; and $`\mathcal{L}{( \cdot )}`$ is the cross-entropy loss. Note that a larger value of CLC (or BLC) represents a larger contribution of the current neuron to the clean task (or backdoor task). Therefore, we can adopt it to classify the neurons into different types.

![Refer to caption](https://arxiv.org/html/x3.png)

Figure 3: A scatter plot showing the BLC and CLC values for neurons in the last two convolutional layers of an audio-backdoored model attacked by Audio BadNets \[[8](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib8)\]. C-zone: Clean Zone; B-zone: Backdoor Zone; H-zone: Hybrid Zone; R-zone: Redundant Zone.

Using this definition, we can obtain the CLCs and BLCs of all neurons within the audio-backdoored model, as illustrated in Fig. [3](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ III-B Observations on Audio-Backdoored Models ‣ III Proposed Method ‣ Gradient Norm-based Fine-Tuning for Backdoor Defense in Automatic Speech Recognition"). We divide the plot into four zones based on the zero values of CLC and BLC: The Clean Zone (C-zone) contains neurons with positive CLCs and negative BLCs, indicating their contribution to the clean task while potentially suppressing the backdoor task. The Backdoor Zone (B-zone) is characterized by neurons with positive BLCs and negative CLCs, suggesting a specific contribution to the backdoor task, potentially at the expense of the clean task. In the Hybrid Zone (H-zone), neurons have both positive CLCs and BLCs, meaning they could contribute to both tasks. Finally, the Redundant Zone (R-zone) contains neurons that do not contribute to either task. Based on these four zones, we can further classify neurons into clean neurons, backdoored neurons, hybrid neurons, and redundant neurons, respectively. Intuitively, our goal is to suppress backdoored neurons and hybrid neurons for backdoor mitigation.

Suggestion Given by the Observation. As illustrated in Section [I](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#S1 "I Introduction ‣ Gradient Norm-based Fine-Tuning for Backdoor Defense in Automatic Speech Recognition"), in the audio-backdoored models, backdoored neurons and hybrid neurons tend to exhibit larger gradients on most clean inputs, while clean neurons stay the smallest. Therefore, it suggests penalizing the high gradient norm during fine-tuning to repair these two kinds of neurons, where the clean neurons are less modified due to their small-gradient characteristic.

### III-C Gradient Norm-based Fine-Tuning

Based on the observations, we propose Gradient Norm-based Fine-Tuning to penalize the high gradients from backdoor neurons and hybrid neurons. An overview of the method is shown in Fig. [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ I Introduction ‣ Gradient Norm-based Fine-Tuning for Backdoor Defense in Automatic Speech Recognition"). An $`L_{2}`$ norm of the gradients is added as a regularization term to the fine-tuning loss function, as shown below:

```math
{{\mathcal{L}{({\mathbf{θ}})}} = {{\mathcal{L}_{ce}{({\mathbf{θ}})}} + {\lambda \cdot \left\| {{\nabla_{\mathbf{θ}}\mathcal{L}_{ce}}{({\mathbf{θ}})}} \right\|_{2}}}},
```

where $`\mathcal{L}_{ce}{( \cdot )}`$ is the original cross-entropy loss, $`\left\| {{\nabla_{\mathbf{θ}}\mathcal{L}_{ce}}{({\mathbf{θ}})}} \right\|_{2}`$ corresponds to the $`L_{2}`$ norm of the gradients of the model, and $`\lambda`$ is a trade-off coefficient to control the strength of penalization. During the fine-tuning process, we aim to minimize this loss function using the available clean set $`D_{c}`$. The object function is formulated as:

```math
{{\min\limits_{\mathbf{θ}}{\mathbb{E}}_{{({\mathbf{x}},y)} \in \mathcal{D}_{c}}}{\lbrack{\mathcal{L}{({F{({\mathbf{x}};{\mathbf{θ}})}},y)}}\rbrack}}.
```

However, direct optimization on equation ([3](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#S3.E3 "Equation 3 ‣ III-C Gradient Norm-based Fine-Tuning ‣ III Proposed Method ‣ Gradient Norm-based Fine-Tuning for Backdoor Defense in Automatic Speech Recognition")) involves calculating a Hessian matrix with $`O{(n^{2})}`$ time and space complexities, which is infeasible. Inspired by the approximation scheme in \[[19](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib19)\], we choose to simplify it similarly using Taylor expansion and additional optimization steps. It can be formulated as:

$`\begin{array}{cl}
{{\nabla_{\mathbf{θ}}\mathcal{L}}{({\mathbf{θ}})}} & {= {{{\nabla_{\mathbf{θ}}\mathcal{L}_{ce}}{({\mathbf{θ}})}} + {\nabla_{\mathbf{θ}}{({\lambda \cdot \left\| {{\nabla_{\mathbf{θ}}\mathcal{L}_{ce}}{({\mathbf{θ}})}} \right\|_{2}})}}}} \\
 & {= {{{\nabla_{\mathbf{θ}}\mathcal{L}_{ce}}{({\mathbf{θ}})}} + {{\lambda \cdot {\nabla_{\mathbf{θ}}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{ce}}}{({\mathbf{θ}})}\frac{{\nabla_{\mathbf{θ}}\mathcal{L}_{ce}}{({\mathbf{θ}})}}{\left\| {{\nabla_{\mathbf{θ}}\mathcal{L}_{ce}}{({\mathbf{θ}})}} \right\|_{2}}}}} \\
 & {\approx {{{\nabla_{\mathbf{θ}}\mathcal{L}_{ce}}{({\mathbf{θ}})}} + {\frac{\lambda}{r} \cdot {({{{\nabla_{\mathbf{θ}}\mathcal{L}_{ce}}{({{\mathbf{θ}} + {r\frac{{\nabla_{\mathbf{θ}}\mathcal{L}_{ce}}{({\mathbf{θ}})}}{\left\| {{\nabla_{\mathbf{θ}}\mathcal{L}_{ce}}{({\mathbf{θ}})}} \right\|_{2}}}})}} - {{\nabla_{\mathbf{θ}}\mathcal{L}_{ce}}{({\mathbf{θ}})}}})}}}} \\
 & {{= {{{({1 - \alpha})}{\nabla_{\mathbf{θ}}\mathcal{L}_{ce}}{({\mathbf{θ}})}} + {\alpha{\nabla_{\mathbf{θ}}\mathcal{L}_{ce}}{({{\mathbf{θ}} + {r\frac{{\nabla_{\mathbf{θ}}\mathcal{L}_{ce}}{({\mathbf{θ}})}}{\left\| {{\nabla_{\mathbf{θ}}\mathcal{L}_{ce}}{({\mathbf{θ}})}} \right\|_{2}}}})}}}},}
\end{array}`$

where $`r`$ is for appropriating the Hessian multiplication, and $`\alpha = \frac{\lambda}{r}`$ is used for trade-off. In the practical defense process, we conduct an additional optimization step to approximate the second term in equation ([5](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#S3.E5 "Equation 5 ‣ III-C Gradient Norm-based Fine-Tuning ‣ III Proposed Method ‣ Gradient Norm-based Fine-Tuning for Backdoor Defense in Automatic Speech Recognition")), aiming to avoid the Hessian computation:

```math
{{{\nabla_{\mathbf{θ}}\mathcal{L}_{ce}}{({{\mathbf{θ}} + {r\frac{{\nabla_{\mathbf{θ}}\mathcal{L}_{ce}}{({\mathbf{θ}})}}{\left\| {{\nabla_{\mathbf{θ}}\mathcal{L}_{ce}}{({\mathbf{θ}})}} \right\|_{2}}}})}} \approx {{{\nabla_{\mathbf{θ}}\mathcal{L}_{ce}}{({\mathbf{θ}})}}|}_{{\mathbf{θ}} = {{\mathbf{θ}} + {r\frac{{\nabla_{\mathbf{θ}}\mathcal{L}_{ce}}{({\mathbf{θ}})}}{{\|{{\nabla_{\mathbf{θ}}\mathcal{L}_{ce}}{({\mathbf{θ}})}}\|}_{2}}}}}}.
```

The details of the algorithm process are illustrated in Algorithm [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ III-C Gradient Norm-based Fine-Tuning ‣ III Proposed Method ‣ Gradient Norm-based Fine-Tuning for Backdoor Defense in Automatic Speech Recognition"). For each iteration (line 1$`\sim`$9 of the Algorithm), we first obtain a mini-batch of data $`{\mathbf{B}}_{c}`$ (line 2) and the current parameters $`{\mathbf{θ}}^{t}`$ (line 3). Based on them, we calculate the first term in equation ([5](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#S3.E5 "Equation 5 ‣ III-C Gradient Norm-based Fine-Tuning ‣ III Proposed Method ‣ Gradient Norm-based Fine-Tuning for Backdoor Defense in Automatic Speech Recognition")) as $`{\mathbf{g}}_{1}`$ (line 4). Then, we temporally conduct an additional optimization step as stated in equation ([6](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#S3.E6 "Equation 6 ‣ III-C Gradient Norm-based Fine-Tuning ‣ III Proposed Method ‣ Gradient Norm-based Fine-Tuning for Backdoor Defense in Automatic Speech Recognition")) to approximate the second term in equation ([5](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#S3.E5 "Equation 5 ‣ III-C Gradient Norm-based Fine-Tuning ‣ III Proposed Method ‣ Gradient Norm-based Fine-Tuning for Backdoor Defense in Automatic Speech Recognition")), as $`{\mathbf{g}}_{2}`$ (line 5$`\sim`$6). By combining the two terms with $`\alpha`$, we can calculate the final gradients to permanently update $`{\mathbf{θ}}^{t}`$ (line 7$`\sim`$8). After $`T`$ iterations, we can obtain a repaired model $`F_{c}`$, which is validated effective towards audio-backdoored model in Section [IV](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#S4 "IV Experiments ‣ Gradient Norm-based Fine-Tuning for Backdoor Defense in Automatic Speech Recognition").

1:Clean dataset $`\mathcal{D}_{c}`$; backdoored model $`F`$; the number of iterations $`T`$; hyper-parameters $`r`$ and $`\alpha`$;

2:The clean model $`F_{c}`$;

3:for $`t = 1`$ to $`T`$ do

4:     Get a mini-batch $`{\mathbf{B}}_{c}`$ from $`\mathcal{D}_{c}`$;

5:     Extract the parameters $`{\mathbf{θ}}^{t}`$ from $`F`$;

6:     Input $`{\mathbf{B}}_{c}`$ into $`F`$, calculate gradients $`{\mathbf{g}}_{1}\leftarrow{{\nabla_{{\mathbf{θ}}^{t}}\mathcal{L}_{ce}}{({\mathbf{θ}}^{t})}}`$;

7:     Copy the model $`F`$ as $`F'`$, and define its parameters as $`{\mathbf{θ}}'\leftarrow{{\mathbf{θ}}^{t} + {r\frac{{\mathbf{g}}_{1}}{\left\| {\mathbf{g}}_{1} \right\|_{2}}}}`$;

8:     Input $`{\mathbf{B}}_{c}`$ into $`F'`$, calculate gradients $`{\mathbf{g}}_{2}\leftarrow{{\nabla_{{\mathbf{θ}}'}\mathcal{L}_{ce}}{({\mathbf{θ}}')}}`$;

9:     Calculate the final gradient $`{\mathbf{g}}\leftarrow{{{({1 - \alpha})}{\mathbf{g}}_{1}} + {\alpha{\mathbf{g}}_{2}}}`$;

10:     Update $`{\mathbf{θ}}^{t}`$ using $`\mathbf{g}`$;

11:end for

12:Return $`F_{c}`$ with parameters $`{\mathbf{θ}}^{T}`$.

## IV Experiments

### IV-A Experimental Setups

Datasets and Models. We use Google’s Speech Commands Dataset (SCD) \[[6](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib6)\], a commonly used dataset for speech recognition tasks. We employ two versions: the first version contains 10 classes that were also used in \[[27](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib27)\] (SCD-10), and the second version includes the full 30 classes (SCD-30). We choose ResNet \[[21](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib21)\], LSTM \[[31](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib31)\], Small CNN \[[32](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib32), [33](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib33)\], KWT \[[34](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib34)\] and EAT \[[35](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib35)\], which are commonly used as speech recognition models, as our experimental models.

Attacker Settings. In our experiments, the poisoning ratio for the attacks is set to 10%, and the target label is set to “up”. We employ seven attack methods: Audio BadNets \[[8](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib8)\], Ultrasonic \[[27](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib27)\], JingleBack \[[20](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib20)\], DABA \[[28](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib28)\], FlowMur \[[30](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib30)\], PBSM and VSVC \[[29](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib29)\]. Among these, we extend the representative attack, BadNets \[[8](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib8)\] from the visual domain, to Audio BadNets in the audio domain, where a white block is added at a fixed position in the MFCC spectrogram \[[36](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib36)\] of the audio signal to be poisoned.

Defender Settings. Since our GN-FT is designed as a model-level defense, we compare it with the only known model-level defense method adapted to the audio domain, FP \[[10](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib10)\]. We follow a similar setting with 5% (known as clean data ratio) of the clean training data for defense purposes. Since the hyperparameters $`r`$ and $`\alpha`$ are more related to the approximation ability, and well-discussed in \[[19](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#bib.bib19)\], we follow the default setup to 0.05 and 0.7, respectively.

Evaluation Metrics. We use two metrics to evaluate the defense methods: Clean Accuracy (CA) and Attack Success Rate (ASR). CA represents the accuracy of the model on clean data, while ASR indicates the proportion of poisoned data that the model predicts as the target label. The boldfaced numbers represent the best performance among the same metric.

### IV-B Main Results

Results On SCD-10. Table [I](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#S4.T1 "Table I ‣ IV-B Main Results ‣ IV Experiments ‣ Gradient Norm-based Fine-Tuning for Backdoor Defense in Automatic Speech Recognition") presents the performance comparisons on SCD-10 dataset using ResNet, LSTM, Small CNN, KWT and EAT. The results demonstrate that our method, GN-FT, exhibits significant advantages over FP, effectively reducing ASR while maintaining a high CA in most cases. For ResNet, GN-FT significantly lowers the average ASR from 91.72% to 9.73%, while the average CA only drops slightly from 94.54% to 90.40%. Although FP performs better ASR in JingleBack and DABA, the sacrifices on CA are unacceptable at 33.69% and 21.45%, respectively. Similarly, for LSTM, Small CNN, KWT and EAT, GN-FT outperforms FP for nearly all attacks. In contrast, FP fails to achieve effective defense against these attack methods.

Results on SCD-30. Table [II](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#S4.T2 "Table II ‣ IV-B Main Results ‣ IV Experiments ‣ Gradient Norm-based Fine-Tuning for Backdoor Defense in Automatic Speech Recognition") presents the defense performance on SCD-30 dataset using ResNet and LSTM. Similar to its performance on SCD-10 dataset, GN-FT significantly outperforms FP on SCD-30 dataset as well. For ResNet, GN-FT demonstrates effective defense against most attacks, although less effective on ASR towards the more stealthy attacks, JingleBack and FlowMur. Notably, in DABA and FlowMur attacks, GN-FT can increase the model’s CA compared to No Defense, improving it by 0.07% and 8.68%, respectively, and the average CA after defense also increases by 0.74%. For LSTM, GN-FT can successfully defend against all five attacks with ASR lower than 10%. Similar to the performance on SCD-10 dataset, FP suffers from high ASRs or significant reduction in CAs.

Overall, we can see that GN-FT is an effective defense method against all attack methods under different experimental setups.

Influences on Backdoored and Hybrid Neurons. To analyze the influences of GN-FT on the backdoor and hybrid neurons, we observe the BLC-CLC distribution of neurons in the last two convolutional layers after defense. As shown in Fig. [4](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ IV-D Further Analysis ‣ IV Experiments ‣ Gradient Norm-based Fine-Tuning for Backdoor Defense in Automatic Speech Recognition"), GN-FT can effectively reduce the number of neurons in the H-zone and B-zone compared to Fig. [3](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01152v1#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ III-B Observations on Audio-Backdoored Models ‣ III Proposed Method ‣ Gradient Norm-based Fine-Tuning for Backdoor Defense in Automatic Speech Recognition"), while the number of neurons in the R-zone increases, indicating that some hybrid neurons and backdoored neurons have been moved to the R-zone after defense, which is considered redundant. The result proves that our method can mitigate the backdoor by reducing the number of backdoored and hybrid neurons.

## V Conclusion

We propose Gradient Norm-based Fine-Tuning to mitigate the backdoored effects in audio-backdoored models. Our study reveals that backdoored and hybrid neurons in these models show larger gradients for clean inputs. Our work highlights the need for specific backdoor defenses for audio-backdoored models, which differ from visual models but were previously overlooked. Our future work will focus on exploring the differences between audio and visual domains to enhance the defense performance against stealthy attacks like DABA and FlowMur.
