###### Abstract

Automatic Speech recognition (ASR) struggles with Pathological speech
due to persistent speaker-specific mismatch. In clinical and
accessibility scenarios, only limited target-speaker data is typically
available, making full model retraining impractical. We present a
component-level analysis of LoRA adaptation in Whisper, investigating
how adapter placement interacts with the model size, training efficiency
and utterance-duration in single-speaker settings. Our experimental
comparative study shows that while scaling reduces errors, it does not
eliminate mismatch. Furthermore, the full attention LoRa provides the
most reliable accuracy, and the best time–accuracy trade-off.
Improvements vary across utterance length, with medium-length recordings
benefiting the most, while short and long utterances remain challenging.
These findings highlight the importance of adaptation placement and
duration-dependent evaluation, under challenging, data-scarce
personalization scenarios.

## 1 Introduction

Modern ASR systems are primarily built and evaluated on typical speech.
Yet, speakers suffering from voice pathologies (i.e., atypical
phonation, articulation, prosody, and reduced intelligibility) lead to
errors that severely affect communication and access to AI-assisted
digital services. The errors are not uniform: short commands, longer
utterances, and persistent speaker-specific acoustic patterns can fail
in different ways.

Whisper is a strong general-purpose ASR model trained on 680,000 hours
of weakly supervised multilingual speech \[20\]. However, robustness
studies show that average performance can hide failures under
distribution shift \[12, 15\]. Recent investigations of Whisper and
other ASR systems under diverse audio transformations and challenging
acoustic conditions further confirm substantial degradation under
non-standard speech scenarios \[14, 13\].Pathological speech is such a
shift, as it reflects a persistent speaker-specific property rather than
a transient noise condition. While scaling the base model size tends to
reduce errors, clinical and accessibility settings require
speaker-specific adaptation from very limited amount of target-speaker
data.

Parameter–efficient methods such as LoRA are well suited to this
setting, as they freeze the base model and store only a small set of
speaker-specific weights. However, for encoder-decoder ASR models, it
remains unclear where these weights should be placed: on the decoder,
the encoder, or both. Furthermore, it is important to understand whether
the most accurate placement is efficient and whether its gains are
stable across utterance lengths. To address these questions, we
investigate the following research questions:

1.  1.  How does a zero-shot Whisper perform on a pathological speaker scale
        with model size?
2.  2.  Which attention-side LoRA placement gives the best fixed-rank
        adaptation (decoder-only, encoder only, or full-attention)?
3.  3.  Does the most accurate placement also offer the best time–accuracy
        trade-off?
4.  4.  How does adaptation performance vary across short, medium, and long
        utterances?

We address these questions through a controlled ablation study,
developed around a real pathological speaker, to exemplify ASR
applications and methodologies in precision medical context. Four
Whisper model sizes (tiny, base, small, medium) are evaluated across
three LoRA placements under a fixed configuration (rank, learning rate,
dropout, and leave-one-out protocol). The design isolates the effect of
adapter placement under data-scarce conditions.

Our findings show that decoder-side adaptation is often ineffective, or
even detrimental, while full-attention LoRA provides the most consistent
accuracy and the best observed time–accuracy trade-off. Moreover,
duration tiers analysis reveals that improvements are not uniform;
medium-length recordings benefit the most, while short and long
recordings expose persistent errors. Overall, this study pinpoints hard
challenges in ASR-based precision medicine, which are the basis of a
more inclusive access of patients to AI-assisted technologies.

## 2 Related Work

Modern ASR systems perform well for typical speakers in standard
recording conditions, but their accuracy drops severely when the speech
signal deviates from the patterns seen during large-scale pretraining.
Pathological and dysarthric speech create this kind of mismatch:
atypical phonation, articulation, prosody, and intelligibility, which
are persistent speaker-intrinsic properties rather than transient
environmental distortions.

Recent surveys describe progress from acoustic-model adaptation toward
deep learning, self-supervised representations, and multimodal systems;
yet data scarcity and high speaker variability remain key
limitations \[3, 19\]. These characteristics make speaker-specific
personalisation especially relevant: limited target-speaker data has
been shown to improve recognition for dysarthric, accented, and
disordered speech \[7, 21\], and atypical speech has also been addressed
using residual adapters and speaker-conditioned hypernetworks \[16,
24\].

Large pretrained systems such as Whisper, wav2vec 2.0, and Conformer
establish strong ASR baselines \[1, 8, 20\]; yet strong average
performance does not guarantee robustness under distribution shift.
Previous work has shown that modern ASR systems degrade substantially
under adverse acoustic conditions. In particular, Katkov et al. \[14\]
demonstrated significant variations in Whisper performance under diverse
audio transformations and real-time constraints, while a multilingual
evaluation revealed similar vulnerabilities across multiple ASR
architectures and acoustic challenges \[13\]. Furthermore, robustness
analyses under synthetic transformations and real noise conditions have
shown that strong average performance can mask substantial failures
under distribution shift \[15\]. Similar findings have been reported in
multilingual evaluations under adverse acoustic conditions, where
performance varies substantially across languages and recording
environments \[13\]. However, such shifts are mostly external to the
speaker, whereas pathological voice quality is persistent across
utterances and therefore motivates speaker-specific adaptation rather
than noise-only robustness.

To address these challenges, parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT)
offers a practical alternative to full model updates in data-scarce
personalisation settings. LoRA keeps the pretrained model frozen and
adds trainable low-rank updates to selected weight matrices \[11\]; it
has also been applied directly to Whisper ASR \[22\]. Related PEFT
methods, including BitFit and other compact adaptation strategies, show
that small targeted updates can be effective  \[2, 9\].

For an encoder-decoder model such as Whisper, however, it remains
unclear where such updates should be applied. The encoder is a plausible
target because it processes the acoustic representation, and prior work
suggests that Whisper encoders retain acoustic information beyond
transcription \[6\]. The decoder, in contrast, controls autoregressive
generation and cross-attention to encoder states. This motivates our
component-level comparison of decoder-only, encoder-only, and
full-attention LoRA under a shared fixed-rank training configuration.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Dataset

The dataset consists of 215 audio recordings of a single Italian female
speaker with a medical condition, provided in WAV format at 16 kHz. The
total duration is 1,321 seconds ($`\approx`$22 minutes). The average
length of a recording is 6.15 seconds, ranging from 0.48 s and 26.37 s.

All recordings are transcribed in Italian, with utterances containing an
average of 9.9 words (range: 3–54). For analysis, the data are grouped
into three duration tiers (Table 1) used for downstream analysis. The
thresholds are empirical bins that distinguish short commands,
medium-length recordings (the dominant category), and longer spoken
responses. Metadata on pathology subtype, severity, detailed
demographics, and recording condition are not available, due to
stringent ethical constraints.

| Tier   | Threshold  | N   | Avg. duration |
| ------ | ---------- | --- | ------------- |
| Short  | $`<3.5`$ s | 21  | 2.50 s        |
| Medium | 3.5–6.0 s  | 139 | 4.81 s        |
| Long   | $`>6.0`$ s | 55  | 10.81 s       |

Table 1: Duration-tier distribution of the dataset.

### 3.2 Acoustic Characterisation of the Target Speaker

To characterise the acoustic properties of the target speaker, we
computed a non-diagnostic acoustic analysis over all 215 recordings.
Because the recordings consist of short prompted phrases, we used
smoothed cepstral peak prominence (CPPS) as the primary descriptor, as
cepstral measures are recommended for instrumental voice assessment and
are robust for running speech \[10, 18, 17\]. Additionally,
Harmonics-to-Noise Ratio, jitter, and shimmer are included as supportive
descriptors. These measures are more sensitive to reliable voicing and
cycle extraction in connected speech \[4, 23, 27\]. For comparison, we
used 500 randomly selected female samples from Common Voice 24,
processed with the same extraction pipeline.

| Metric            | Target median | CV median | Reference                    |
| ----------------- | ------------- | --------- | ---------------------------- |
| CPPS (dB)         | 4.69          | 7.94      | $`>6.49`$ dB \[5\]           |
| HNR (dB)          | 1.32          | 12.20     | $`6.58`$–$`12.22`$ dB \[25\] |
| Jitter local (%)  | 4.48          | 2.12      | $`0.72`$–$`2.12\%`$ \[25\]   |
| Shimmer local (%) | 18.04         | 9.85      | $`8.33`$–$`16.81\%`$ \[25\]  |

Table 2: Acoustic characterisation of the target speaker and comparison
with a female Common Voice (CV) reference subset processed with the same
extraction pipeline.

Table 2 shows a consistent separation between the target speaker and the
female Common Voice reference subset. Specifically, the CPPS median for
the target lies below the retained reference threshold, whereas the
Common Voice median exceeds it. Similarly, HNR is lower for the target
speaker, while jitter and shimmer are higher, with all three measures
deviating from the connected-speech reference ranges shown in the table.
Together these measures provide descriptive evidence consistent with
dysphonic/pathological voice characteristics, although they do not
constitute a clinical diagnosis.

### 3.3 Whisper Models

Whisper is an encoder-decoder transformer architecture \[20\]. The
encoder begins with two Conv1d layers applied to the 80-channel log-mel
spectrogram, followed by transformer blocks that process the resulting
sequence. This ordering is important because the convolutional front-end
processes the acoustic signal before any attention layer. LoRA modifies
only selected attention projections (q_proj and v_proj) \[11\], while
all other parameters remain frozen in our experiments. As a result, the
convolutional front-end lies outside the adaptation path. Table 3 lists
the model sizes used in this study and reports the size of the
convolutional encoder front-end, which remains unchanged across all
configurations.

| Model          | Params | Enc. | Dec. | Dim  | Heads | CNN params |
| -------------- | ------ | ---- | ---- | ---- | ----- | ---------- |
| Whisper-tiny   | 39M    | 4    | 4    | 384  | 6     | 535,296    |
| Whisper-base   | 74M    | 6    | 6    | 512  | 8     | 910,336    |
| Whisper-small  | 244M   | 12   | 12   | 768  | 12    | 1,955,328  |
| Whisper-medium | 769M   | 24   | 24   | 1024 | 16    | 3,393,536  |

Table 3: Whisper variants used in this work. CNN params denotes the
parameters in the two Conv1d layers of the encoder front-end.

### 3.4 Fine-tuning Strategies

We compare four conditions for each Whisper model size: one zero-shot
baseline and three fixed-rank LoRA configurations. The goal is not to
tune a separate configuration for each model, but to probe where a small
speaker-specific update is most effective.

Vanilla. The vanilla condition uses the original Whisper checkpoint
without any parameter update. It serves as a baseline for how much of
the speaker-specific mismatch is reduced by model capacity alone.

Decoder-only LoRA. This configuration inserts LoRA modules only into
decoder attention projections. It tests whether personalisation can be
achieved mainly by changing the output-side pathway, which conditions on
previously generated text and on encoder states. Weak performance in
this setting suggests that the mismatch cannot be fully addressed by
output-side adaptation alone.

Encoder-only LoRA. This configuration inserts LoRA modules only into
encoder attention projections. It tests whether a small update after the
convolutional front-end can improve the acoustic representation of the
pathological speaker while leaving the decoder unchanged.

Full-attention LoRA. This configuration applies LoRA to both encoder and
decoder attention projections. It is the strongest parameter-efficient
setting considered here, allowing adaptation on both sides of the
sequence-to-sequence model; but it still freezes the convolutional
front-end, feed-forward layers, normalisation layers, embeddings, and
output projection. All LoRA variants modify the query and the value
projections (q_proj and v_proj), with all other parameters kept frozen.

### 3.5 Experimental Design

We evaluate these configurations in a controlled experimental design
that crosses four Whisper sizes (tiny, base, small, medium) with three
LoRA placements, in addition to the corresponding zero-shot baselines.
This defines two main dimensions of analysis: model capacity and adapter
placement. Because LoRA rank and optimisation settings are fixed across
all adapted conditions, differences across decoder-only, encoder-only,
and full-attention LoRA can be interpreted as placement effects rather
than model-specific tuning.

In addition to overall accuracy, we include two practical analyses.
First, an efficiency analysis compares WER reduction with average
training time per fold, since personalisation should not require
prohibitively slow speaker-specific updates. Second, a duration-tier
analysis checks robustness across utterance lengths: short utterances
provide limited context, while longer ones allow errors to accumulate,
meaning overall WER can hide failures in short commands or longer spoken
responses.

Training configuration. All adapted runs use AdamW with batch size 1 and
gradient clipping (maximum norm 1.0). Validation is performed every 10
training steps. LoRA adapters have rank $`r=8`$, scaling factor
$`\alpha=16`$, and dropout 0.05. They are trained with a learning rate
1e-4 for up to 300 training steps per fold. The base Whisper model
weights are frozen; only the LoRA parameters introduced by the selected
placement are updated.

### 3.6 Evaluation Protocol

All experiments use leave-one-out cross-validation. For each of the 215
folds, one recording is held out for testing. From the remaining 214
recordings, one is reserved inside the fold for validation and early
stopping, and the others are used for weight updates. The same fold
construction is used for all model and adapter configurations. Training
stops when validation loss fails to improve by $`10^{-4}`$ for five
consecutive evaluations. Because each fold includes only one validation
utterance, early stopping can be noisy; therefore, all reported deltas
are computed fold-by-fold and then averaged.

### 3.7 Evaluation Metrics

All metrics are computed after text normalisation: references and
hypotheses are lowercased and punctuation is removed. Let $`S`$, $`D`$,
and $`I`$ denote substitutions, deletions, and insertions, and let $`N`$
denote the length of the reference sequence. For word-level scoring,
$`N=N_{w}`$ is the number of reference words; for character-level
scoring, $`N=N_{c}`$ is the number of reference characters. We report
Word Error Rate (WER) and Character Error Rate (CER) as percentages:

```math
\mathrm{WER}=\frac{S_{w}+D_{w}+I_{w}}{N_{w}}\times 100,\qquad\mathrm{CER}=\frac{S_{c}+D_{c}+I_{c}}{N_{c}}\times 100.
```

WER is the primary metric because word-level errors directly affect
command recognition and transcription usability. CER shows whether WER
changes reflect complete word failures or smaller character-level near
misses.

We also report two auxiliary similarity metrics, where higher values are
better, to check whether WER/CER trends reflect broader transcript
quality rather than a metric-specific effect. The chrF score is computed
from character $`n`$-gram precision $`P`$ and recall $`R`$ using the
standard F-score form:

```math
F_{\beta}=(1+\beta^{2})\frac{PR}{\beta^{2}P+R}.
```

Cosine similarity is computed between reference ($`\mathbf{r}`$) and
hypothesis ($`\mathbf{h}`$) embeddings from a multilingual MiniLM L12 v2
\[26\]:

```math
\cos(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{h})=\frac{\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{h}}{\|\mathbf{r}\|_{2}\,\|\mathbf{h}\|_{2}}.
```

chrF rewards partial character $`n`$-gram overlap in inflected words,
while cosine similarity gives a softer sentence-level indication of
semantic closeness. They are not substitutes for WER/CER, but
corroborate whether adaptation improves the transcription as a whole.

For adaptation results, deltas are measured relative to the
corresponding vanilla baseline:

```math
\Delta\mathrm{WER}=\mathrm{WER}_{\mathrm{after}}-\mathrm{WER}_{\mathrm{vanilla}},\qquad\Delta\mathrm{CER}=\mathrm{CER}_{\mathrm{after}}-\mathrm{CER}_{\mathrm{vanilla}}.
```

Negative deltas therefore indicate improvement. For the efficiency plot,
WER reduction is reported with the opposite sign,

```math
\mathrm{WER\ reduction}=\mathrm{WER}_{\mathrm{vanilla}}-\mathrm{WER}_{\mathrm{after}},
```

so positive values indicate improvement. All values are computed per
held-out fold and then averaged across the 215 folds.

### 4.1 Zero-shot Baselines

Table 4 reports the vanilla Whisper baselines. WER and CER decrease
monotonically with model size: tiny starts at 68.8/24.9, while medium
reaches 12.2/5.0.

| medium | 12.2 | 5.0  | 92.0 | 0.941  |

Table 4: Vanilla Whisper baselines (215-fold LOO average). WER and CER
are percentages.

The baseline results suggest that capacity alone reduces a large part of
the mismatch, but not all of it. WER drops by 22.6 percentage points
from tiny to base and by 23.0 points from base to small, then by a
smaller 11.0 points from small to medium. CER follows the same
direction, with the largest drop between tiny and base. This suggests
diminishing returns from scaling alone: medium is already much stronger
than the smaller models, but its 12.2% WER still leaves a clear
opportunity for speaker-specific adaptation.

the largest absolute payoff when the zero-shot baseline is weak: LoRA

on the encoder side. Decoder-only LoRA is not consistently reliable in
this protocol, which suggests that the main pathological-speaker
mismatch is unlikely to be fully addressed by adjusting the output-side
language-generation path alone. Full-attention LoRA still improves over
encoder-only LoRA, indicating that adapting decoder attention becomes
useful when it is paired with an adapted acoustic representation.

### 4.3 Training-efficiency Trade-off

Figure 1 compares the average wall-clock training time per leave-one-out
fold with the corresponding WER reduction. Timing is measured
consistently for the LoRA update stage and averaged across folds, so it
should be read as relative within-run efficiency. Each point is one
fixed-rank LoRA configuration. The x-axis reports average training time
per fold, and the y-axis reports the absolute WER reduction in
percentage points; negative values indicate that adaptation worsened
WER.

Figure 1: Training time versus WER improvement for fixed-rank LoRA
configurations. Each point corresponds to one model size and one LoRA
placement.

In these runs, LoRA full provides the most favourable observed
time–accuracy trade-off across the considered model sizes. For tiny and
base, it gives large WER reductions of 15.0 and 11.2 percentage points
with average fold times of 34.4 s and 50.0 s, respectively. For small
and medium, the gains are smaller, 3.6 and 1.8 percentage points, but
LoRA full remains the most effective configuration. Decoder-only
adaptation is not efficient in this protocol: it often gives little
improvement, or even degrades WER, despite similar training time. Thus
the extra attention-side coverage of LoRA full is justified for the
smaller models by a much larger gain, while for medium it is mainly a
choice for maximising accuracy when even a small WER reduction matters.

## 5 Conclusion

In this single-speaker case study, we evaluate fixed-rank LoRA
personalisation of Whisper for an Italian speaker with pathological
speech under data-scarce conditions. Larger models achieve lower error
rates, but adaptation remains highly sensitive to placement. In
particular, decoder-only LoRA is often ineffective or detrimental,
whereas full-attention LoRA provides the most consistent placement.
These results suggest that speaker-specific mismatch in pathological
speech is better addressed by adapting both acoustic and decoder
attention, rather than modifying the output pathway alone. Scaling
reduces the absolute WER payoff of LoRA full—from 15.0 pp for tiny to
1.8 pp for medium—but in relative terms the reduction remains within a
narrower 15–24% band across all four model sizes, indicating that the
apparent diminishing returns are partly an artefact of larger models
starting from a stronger zero-shot baseline.

Across the analyses, the same pattern is observed. Full-attention LoRA
consistently achieves the most favourable time–accuracy trade-off, and
auxiliary metrics (CER, chrF, cosine similarity) follow the same trends
as WER. Duration-tier analysis further shows that performance gains are
not uniform across utterance lengths: medium handles long utterances
well but struggles on short ones, while the small model degrades on long
utterances under full-attention LoRA.

##### Limitations.

As this is a single-speaker case study, the findings should not be
interpreted as population-level results; future work should evaluate
broader and more diverse cohorts across speakers, pathology types,
severity levels, languages, and recording conditions, and investigate
whether adapting or controlled fine-tuning at the currently frozen
pre-attention encoder front-end can further reduce the remaining
performance gap. Several further limitations deserve acknowledgement.
First, because recording conditions and detailed pathology metadata were
unavailable, the acoustic deviations reported in Section 3.2 cannot be
fully disentangled from possible recording-condition effects; controlled
recordings with documented conditions and a matched healthy-speech
baseline would be needed to isolate the pathology-specific contribution.
Second, the speaker’s specific pathology and severity could not be
disclosed due to ethical and privacy constraints on the dataset, which
limits the interpretability of the acoustic characterisation. Third,
both absolute (pp) and relative (%) WER and CER reductions are now
reported in Tables 5 and 7 for transparency; however, the analysis does
not include formal statistical significance testing, and—given the
variability of single-utterance folds under leave-one-out
cross-validation—paired tests across folds would further strengthen
confidence in the reported differences. Finally, the study is restricted
to LoRA, and comparing against other parameter-efficient methods already
surveyed in Related Work—such as BitFit \[2\] and adapter-based
approaches \[24\]—would clarify whether the observed placement effects
are specific to low-rank adaptation or generalise across PEFT
strategies.
