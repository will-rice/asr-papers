# Automatic Speech Recognition using Advanced Deep Learning Approaches: A survey

Hamza Kheddar kheddar.hamza@univ-medea.dz    Mustapha Hemis hemismustapha@yahoo.fr    Yassine Himeur yhimeur@ud.ac.ae LSEA Laboratory, Department of Electrical Engineering, University of Medea, 26000, Algeria LCPTS Laboratory, University of Sciences and Technology Houari Boumediene (USTHB), P.O. ###### Abstract

Recent advancements in deep learning (DL) have posed a significant challenge for automatic speech recognition (ASR). ASR relies on extensive training datasets, including confidential ones, and demands substantial computational and storage resources. Enabling adaptive systems improves ASR performance in dynamic environments. DL techniques assume training and testing data originate from the same domain, which is not always true. Advanced DL techniques like deep transfer learning (DTL), federated learning (FL), and deep reinforcement learning (DRL) address these issues. DTL allows high-performance models using small yet related datasets, FL enables training on confidential data without dataset possession, and DRL optimizes decision-making in dynamic environments, reducing computation costs.

This survey offers a comprehensive review of DTL, FL, and DRL-based ASR frameworks, aiming to provide insights into the latest developments and aid researchers and professionals in understanding the current challenges. Additionally, Transformers, which are advanced DL techniques heavily used in proposed ASR frameworks, are considered in this survey for their ability to capture extensive dependencies in the input ASR sequence. The paper starts by presenting the background of DTL, FL, DRL, and Transformers and then adopts a well-designed taxonomy to outline the [state-of-the-art](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.71.71.71) ([SOTA](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.71.71.71)) approaches. Subsequently, a critical analysis is conducted to identify the strengths and weaknesses of each framework. Additionally, a comparative study is presented to highlight the existing challenges, paving the way for future research opportunities.

## 1 Introduction

### 1.1 Preliminary

Advancements in [artificial intelligence](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.3.3.3) ([AI](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.3.3.3)) have significantly improved [human-machine interaction](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.37.37.37) ([HMI](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.37.37.37)), especially with technologies that convert speech into executable actions. [automatic speech recognition](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) ([ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6)) emerges as a leading communication technology in [HMI](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.37.37.37), extensively utilized by corporations and service providers for facilitating interactions through [AI](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.3.3.3) platforms like chatbots and digital assistants. Spoken language forms the core of these interactions, emphasizing the necessity for sophisticated speech processing in [AI](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.3.3.3) systems tailored for [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6). [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) technology encompasses the analysis of (i) acoustic, lexical, and syntactic aspects; and (ii) semantic understanding. The [acoustic model](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.4.4.4) ([AM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.4.4.4)) processing includes speech coding \[[1](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib1)\], enhancement \[[2](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib2)\], source separation \[[3](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib3), [2](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib2)\], alongside securing speech via steganography \[[4](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib4), [5](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib5), [6](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib6)\] and watermarking \[[7](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib7), [8](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib8), [9](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib9)\]. These components are integral to audio analysis. On the other hand, the [semantic model](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.69.69.69) ([SM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.69.69.69)), often identified as [language model](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.42.42.42) ([LM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.42.42.42)) processing in literature, involves all [natural language processing](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.55.55.55) ([NLP](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.55.55.55)) techniques. This [AI](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.3.3.3) branch aims at teaching computers to understand and interpret human language, serving as the basis for applications like music information retrieval \[[10](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib10)\], sound file organization \[[11](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib11)\], [audio tagging](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.8.8.8) ([AT](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.8.8.8)), and [event detection](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.26.26.26) ([ED](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.26.26.26)) \[[12](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib12)\], as well as converting speech to text and vice versa \[[13](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib13)\], detecting hate speech \[[14](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib14)\], and cyberbullying \[[15](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib15)\]. Employing [NLP](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.55.55.55) across various domains enables [AI](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.3.3.3) models to effectively comprehend and respond to human inputs, unveiling extensive research prospects in diverse sectors.

Recent advancements in [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) have been significantly propelled by the evolution of [deep learning](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.20.20.20) ([DL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.20.20.20)) methodologies. An extensive range of [DL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.20.20.20) models has been developed, demonstrating remarkable improvements and surpassing former [SOTA](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.71.71.71) achievements \[[16](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib16), [17](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib17)\]. Transformers, a notable innovation within these [DL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.20.20.20) approaches, have become a cornerstone in advancing various [NLP](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.55.55.55) tasks, including [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6). Initially conceptualized for [sequence-to-sequence](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.63.63.63) ([S2S](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.63.63.63)) applications in [NLP](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.55.55.55), their success is largely attributed to their adeptness at discerning long-range dependencies and complex patterns within sequential data. A hallmark of Transformer models is their utilization of an attention mechanism, which precisely focuses on specific portions of the input sequence during prediction tasks. This mechanism is particularly effective in [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6), facilitating the detailed modeling of contextual nuances and the interconnections among acoustic signals, essential for accurate transcription. Models such as the Transformer Transducer, Conformer, and [end-to-end speech processing network](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.27.27.27) ([ESPNet](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.27.27.27)), leveraging self-attention and parallel processing, have achieved leading performance in [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) tasks. Their robustness across diverse languages further underscores their capability to adapt to a wide range of linguistic features and acoustic variations, making Transformers an exceptionally promising option for enhancing [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) systems, surpassing the constraints of conventional models.

The integration of [DL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.20.20.20) with its variants in [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) introduces substantial challenges, especially concerning its application in natural [HMI](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.37.37.37). Despite [DL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.20.20.20)’s numerous advantages, it encounters various obstacles. The inherent complexity of [DL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.20.20.20) models, which stems from their need for extensive training data to attain high performance, demands significant computational and storage resources \[[18](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib18)\]. Moreover, the issue of data scarcity in [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) reflects the inadequate quantities of training data available for exploiting complex [DL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.20.20.20) algorithms effectively \[[19](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib19)\]. The paucity of annotated data further complicates the development of supervised [DL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.20.20.20)-based [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) models. Additionally, the presumption that training and testing datasets originate from the same domain, possessing identical feature spaces and distribution characteristics, is often misguided. This mismatch challenges the practical deployment of [DL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.20.20.20) models in real-world settings \[[20](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib20)\]. Thus, the performance of [DL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.20.20.20) models may be compromised when faced with limited training datasets or discrepancies in data distribution between training and testing environments \[[21](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib21)\]. These challenges highlight the critical need for adaptive methodologies and improved data management approaches to fully harness the capabilities of [DL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.20.20.20) in [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) systems.

In an effort to address existing challenges and increase the robustness and flexibility of [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) systems, novel [DL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.20.20.20) methodologies have been introduced. These include [deep transfer learning](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) ([DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25)), [deep reinforcement learning](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22) ([DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22)), and [federated learning](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.31.31.31) ([FL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.31.31.31)), which collectively aim at overcoming difficulties related to the transfer of knowledge, enhancing the generalization capabilities of models, and optimizing training processes. These innovative approaches significantly broaden the operational scope of conventional [DL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.20.20.20) frameworks within the [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) field.

Figure [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1.1 Preliminary ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Automatic Speech Recognition using Advanced Deep Learning Approaches: A survey") highlights critical areas in speech processing where [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25), [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22), and [FL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.31.31.31) can be applied. Consequently, domains such as [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6), [speech enhancement](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.67.67.67) ([SE](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.67.67.67)), [hate speech detection](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.39.39.39) ([HSD](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.39.39.39)), and [speech security](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.72.72.72) ([SS](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.72.72.72)) are closely interconnected. [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) provides acoustic parameters to [NLP](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.55.55.55) for [HSD](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.39.39.39) task, which in turn provides semantic details to [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6). Additionally, [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) can be employed in the SS domain as a steganalytic process to verify the integrity of speech \[[4](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib4), [22](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib22)\]. Furthermore, [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) and [SE](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.67.67.67) can mutually offer performance feedback.

### 1.2 Contribution of the paper

This article offers an extensive examination of contemporary frameworks within advanced deep learning approaches, spanning the period from 2016 to 2023. These approaches include [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25), [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22), [FL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.31.31.31), and Transformers, all within the context of [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6). To the best of the authors’ knowledge, there has been no prior research paper that has intricately explored and critically evaluated contributions in the aforementioned advanced DL-based [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) until now.

In recent years, numerous survey papers have been published to assess various aspects of [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) models. Some of these surveys concentrate on specific languages, such as Portuguese \[[23](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib23)\], Indian \[[24](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib24)\], Turkish \[[25](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib25)\], Arabic \[[26](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib26)\] and tonal languages (including Asian, Indo-European and African) \[[27](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib27)\]. Additionally, Abushariah et al.’s review emphasizes bilingual [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) \[[28](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib28)\]. On the non-specific language review front, specific areas within [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) have been targeted, including [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) using limited vocabulary \[[29](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib29)\], [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) for children \[[30](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib30)\], error detection and correction \[[31](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib31)\], and unsupervised [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) \[[32](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib32)\]. Systematic reviews with a focus on neural networks \[[33](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib33)\] and [deep neural networks](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.21.21.21) \[[34](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib34)\] have also been proposed. In another comprehensive review, Malik et al. \[[35](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib35)\] discussed diverse feature extraction methods, [SOTA](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.71.71.71) classification models, and some deep learning approaches. Recently, the authors presented an [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) review focused on [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) for [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) \[[36](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib36)\]. Table [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 1.2 Contribution of the paper ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Automatic Speech Recognition using Advanced Deep Learning Approaches: A survey") presents a summary of the main contributions of the proposed [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) review compared to other existing [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) reviews/surveys.

This survey article offers several significant enhancements and additions compared to previous [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) surveys. Firstly, it consolidates works that utilize both [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) and advanced DL approaches, providing a comprehensive overview of their intersection. Secondly, it provides performance evaluation results of all considered approaches. Thirdly, it includes metrics and dataset reviews used in [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) models. Furthermore, it tackles ongoing challenges and consequently proposes future directions. The main contributions of this article can be summarized as follows:

- •
  Presenting the background of advanced DL techniques including [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25), [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22), [FL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.31.31.31) and Transformers. Describing the evaluation metrics and datasets employed for validating [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) approaches.
- •
  Introducing a well-defined taxonomy categorizing [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) methodologies based on the domains of [AM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.4.4.4) and [LM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.42.42.42).
- •
  Identifying challenges and gaps in advanced DL-based [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6).
- •
  Proposing future directions to enhance the performance of advanced DL-based [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) solutions and predicting the potential advancements in the field.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Acoustic and language models

The [AM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.4.4.4) is in charge of capturing the sound characteristics of different phonetic units. This involves generating statistical measures for characteristic vector sequences from the audio waveform. Various techniques, such as [linear predictive coding](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.43.43.43) ([LPC](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.43.43.43)), Cepstral analysis, filter-bank analysis, [Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.49.49.49), wavelet analysis, and others, can be used to extract these features \[[37](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib37)\]. In the processing stage, a decoder (search algorithm) uses the acoustic lexicon and [LM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.42.42.42) to create the hypothesized word or phoneme. You can see the overall process illustrated in Figure [3](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#S2.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 2.1 Acoustic and language models ‣ 2 Background ‣ Automatic Speech Recognition using Advanced Deep Learning Approaches: A survey").

\Acp

LM provide probabilities of sequences of words, crucial for [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) systems to predict the likelihood of subsequent words in a sentence \[[38](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib38)\]. A domain-specific LM is trained on text data from the target domain to capture its unique vocabulary and grammatical structures \[[39](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib39)\]. For n-gram models, this involves calculating the conditional probability of a word given the previous $`n - 1`$ words \[[40](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib40)\]:

``` math
{P\left( w_{n} \middle| {w_{n - 1},w_{n - 2},\ldots,w_{n - {({n - 1})}}} \right)} = \frac{C\left( w_{n - {({n - 1})}},\ldots,w_{n} \right)}{C\left( w_{n - {({n - 1})}},\ldots,w_{n - 1} \right)}
```

In the context of [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6), the [LM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.42.42.42) complements the [AM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.4.4.4) by providing linguistic context. The combined probability from the [AM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.4.4.4) and the [LM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.42.42.42) helps in determining the most likely transcription for a given audio input during the decoding process. The frequently utilized [LM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.42.42.42) in [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) systems is the backoff n-gram model.

## 3 Advanced ASR methods and applications

Traditional statistical [LMs](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.42.42.42), such as backoff n-gram [LMs](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.42.42.42), have been widely used due to their simplicity and reliability. However, [bidirectional encoder representations from transformers](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.9.9.9) ([BERT](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.9.9.9)) \[[60](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib60)\], which utilize attention models, have shown better contextual understanding compared to single-direction LMs, as demonstrated in the work of Devlin et al. \[[61](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib61)\].

In terms of [AM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.4.4.4), deep learning-based models like the deep neural network-hidden Markov model (DNN-HMM) and the [connectionist temporal classification](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.15.15.15) ([CTC](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.15.15.15)) have made significant advancements \[[60](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib60)\]. DNN-HMM models have been extensively studied in [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) research, while [CTC](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.15.15.15) is an end-to-end training method that does not require pre-alignment and only needs input and output sequences. The [S2S](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.63.63.63) model has also been successful in solving [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) tasks without using an [LM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.42.42.42) or pronunciation dictionary, as described in Chiu et al. \[[62](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib62)\].

[ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) systems often face performance degradation in certain situations due to the "one-model-fits-all" approach. Additionally, the lack of diverse and sufficient training data affects [AM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.4.4.4) performance. To overcome these constraints and improve the resilience and flexibility of [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) systems, advanced DL methodologies such as [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) and it sub-field [domain adaptation](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.17.17.17) ([DA](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.17.17.17)), [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22), and [FL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.31.31.31) have surfaced. These innovative methodologies collectively address issues concerning knowledge transfer, model generalization, and training effectiveness, offering remedies that expand upon the capabilities of traditional DL models within the [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) sphere. Thus, many research studies have focused on enhancing existing [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) systems by applying the aforementioned algorithms. ### 3.1 Transformer-based ASR

The Transformer stands as a prominent deep learning model extensively employed across diverse domains, including [NLP](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.55.55.55), [computer vision](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.16.16.16) ([CV](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.16.16.16)), and speech processing. Originally conceived for machine translation as a [S2S](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.63.63.63) model, it has evolved to find applications in various fields. The Transformer heavily relies on the self-attention mechanism, enabling it to capture extensive dependencies in input sequences. The standard Transformer model incorporates the query–key–value (QKV) attention mechanism. In this setup, given matrix representations of queries $`\mathbf{Q} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{N \times D_{k}}`$, keys $`\mathbf{K} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{M \times D_{k}}`$, and values $`\mathbf{V} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{M \times D_{v}}`$, the scaled dot-product attention is defined as formulated in Equation [2](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#S3.E2 "In 3.1 Transformer-based ASR ‣ 3 Advanced ASR methods and applications ‣ Automatic Speech Recognition using Advanced Deep Learning Approaches: A survey"):

``` math
{\text{Attention}{(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V})}} = {\text{softmax}\left( \frac{{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}}^{T}}{\sqrt{D_{k}}} \right)\mathbf{V}}
```

Here, $`N`$ and $`M`$ represent the lengths of queries and keys (or values), and $`D_{k}`$ and $`D_{v}`$ denote the dimensions of keys (or queries) and values. The softmax operation is applied row-wise to the matrix $`\mathbf{A}`$. Within the Transformer architecture, three attention mechanisms exist based on the source of queries and key–value pairs:

- •
  Self-attention: In the Transformer encoder, the queries ($`\mathbf{Q}`$), keys ($`\mathbf{K}`$), and values ($`\mathbf{V}`$) are all equal to the outputs of the previous layer, considered as $`\mathbf{X}`$, or to the initial embeddings in the case of the first layer.
- •
  Masked Self-attention: In the Transformer decoder, self-attention is constrained, allowing queries at each position to attend only to key–value pairs up to and including that position. This is accomplished by implementing a mask function, before normalization, on the attention matrix $`\hat{\mathbf{A}} = {\exp\left( \frac{{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}}^{T}}{\sqrt{D_{k}}} \right)}`$, where illegal positions are masked out by setting $`{\hat{A}}_{ij} = {- \infty}`$ if $`i < j`$. This type of self-attention is often referred to as autoregressive or causal attention.
- •
  Cross-attention: In cross-attention, queries originate from the results of the preceding (decoder) layer, while keys and values stem from the outputs of the encoder.

Numerous studies in the [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) field have introduced Transformer-based approaches, encompassing both the acoustic and language domains. ### 3.2 DTL-based ASR

Overall, [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) consists of training a DL model on a specific domain (or task) and then transferring the acquired knowledge to a new, similar domain (or task). In what follows, we present some of the definitions that are essential to understand the principle of [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) for [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) applications.

\Ac

DTL refers to a [DL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.20.20.20) paradigm where knowledge gained from pre-training a model (Source model) on one domain or task is leveraged to enhance performance of target model, on a different but related domain or task. In this context, a "domain" refers to a specific data distribution, while a "task" represents a learning objective. \AcDA in [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) involves adapting a model trained on a $`D_{S}`$ to perform well on a target domain. This is crucial when there are differences in data distributions between the two domains. Fine-tuning is a technique where a pre-trained model is further trained on task-specific data to improve its performance on a related task. Cross-domain learning extends transfer learning to scenarios where the source and target domains are distinct. Zero-shot learning involves training a model to recognize classes not present in the training data. Transductive [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) focuses on adapting a model based on a specific set of target instances. Inductive [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) aims to generalize knowledge across domains by training a model to handle diverse tasks and domains simultaneously \[[36](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib36), [80](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib80), [81](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib81)\]. These techniques contribute to the versatility and adaptability of [DL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.20.20.20) models in various applications. Figure [8](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#S3.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ 3.2 DTL-based ASR ‣ 3 Advanced ASR methods and applications ‣ Automatic Speech Recognition using Advanced Deep Learning Approaches: A survey") depicted the principle of [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) techniques. Table [5](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#S3.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 3.2.2 Language domain ‣ 3.2 DTL-based ASR ‣ 3 Advanced ASR methods and applications ‣ Automatic Speech Recognition using Advanced Deep Learning Approaches: A survey") summarises the most recent DTL-based [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) techniques used in [AM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.4.4.4) and [LM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.42.42.42) domains.

Auxiliary cross-attentions aid dialect ID recognition, providing dialect information. Adaptive cross-entropy loss automatically balances multi-task learning. Experimental results demonstrate a significant reduction in error rates compared to various single- and multi-task models on multi-dialect speech recognition and dialect ID recognition tasks. Similarly, in the realm of computer vision, [CNNs](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.13.13.13) models like ConvNeXt have outperformed cutting-edge Transformers, partly due to the integration of [depthwise separable convolutions](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.23.23.23) ([DSC](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.23.23.23)). [DSC](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.23.23.23), which approximates regular convolutions, enhances the efficiency of [CNNs](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.13.13.13) in terms of time and memory usage without compromising accuracy—in some cases, even enhancing it. The study \[[85](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib85)\] introduces [DSC](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.23.23.23) into the pre-trained audio model family for audio classification on AudioSet (target task), demonstrating its advantages in balancing accuracy and model size. Xin et al. \[[86](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib86)\] introduce an audio pyramid Transformer with an attention tree structure, with four branches, to reduce computational complexity in fine-grained audio spectrogram processing. It proposes a [DA](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.17.17.17) transfer learning approach for weakly supervised sound [ED](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.26.26.26), a sub-field of [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6), enhancing localization performance by aligning feature distributions between frame and clip domains with a [DA](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.17.17.17) detection loss.

#### 3.2.2 Language domain

The methodology is founded on the utilization of the [BERT](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.9.9.9) model \[[87](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib87)\], which involves pretraining language models and demonstrates improved performance across various downstream tasks. [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) approaches for language models, specifically employed in the domain of voice recognition, are referred to as [LM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.42.42.42) adaptation. ### 3.3 FL-based ASR

\Ac

FL revolutionizes [AI](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.3.3.3) model training by enabling collaboration without the need to share sensitive training data. Traditional centralized approaches are evolving towards decentralized models, where [ML](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.51.51.51) algorithms are trained collaboratively on edge devices like mobile phones, laptops, or private servers \[[98](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib98)\]. The mathematical formulation of [FL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.31.31.31) focuses on training a single global model across multiple devices or nodes (clients) while keeping the data localized. The objective is to minimize a global loss function that is typically the weighted sum of the local loss functions on all clients. The standard [FL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.31.31.31) problem can be formulated as:

``` math
{{\min\limits_{\theta}F}{(\theta)}} = {\min\limits_{\theta}{\sum\limits_{k = 1}^{K}{\frac{n_{k}}{N}F_{k}{(\theta)}}}}
```

In this context, $`\theta`$ denotes the parameters of the global model to be learned, $`K`$ represents the total number of clients, $`n_{k}`$ signifies the number of data samples at client $`k`$, $`N = {\sum_{k = 1}^{K}n_{k}}`$ stands for the total number of data samples across all clients, and $`F_{k}{(\theta)}`$ indicates the local loss function computed on the data of client $`k`$. In [FL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.31.31.31), the goal is to find the global model parameters $`\theta`$ that minimize the global loss function $`F{(\theta)}`$, which is an aggregate of the local loss functions from all participating clients. This process typically involves iterative updates to the model parameters using algorithms like [federated averaging](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.29.29.29) ([FedAvg](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.29.29.29)), where clients compute gradients or updates based on their local data and send these updates to a central server. The server then aggregates these updates to improve the global model.

- •
  Horizontal federated learning (HFL): In [HFL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.36.36.36), clients train a shared global model using their respective datasets, characterized by the same feature space but different sample spaces. Each client utilizes a local [AI](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.3.3.3) model, and their updates are aggregated by a central server without exposing raw data. The [HFL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.36.36.36) training process involves: (1) initialization, (2) local training, (3) encryption of gradients, (4) secure aggregation, and (5) global model parameter updates. The objective function minimizes a global loss across all parties’ datasets \[[98](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib98)\].
- •
  \Ac
  VFL: [vertical federated learning](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.84.84.84) ([VFL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.84.84.84)) trains models on datasets sharing the same sample space but having different feature spaces. Through entity data alignment (EDA) and encrypted model trained (EMT), [VFL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.84.84.84) allows clients to cooperatively train models without sharing raw data. The training process involves the same steps as [HFL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.36.36.36) \[[98](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib98)\].

FL presents a paradigm shift in [AI](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.3.3.3) training, promoting collaboration while respecting data privacy. The working principle of [HFL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.36.36.36), and [VFL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.84.84.84) is depicted on Figure [9](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#S3.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ 3.3 FL-based ASR ‣ 3 Advanced ASR methods and applications ‣ Automatic Speech Recognition using Advanced Deep Learning Approaches: A survey"), they cater to various data distribution scenarios, offering flexible solutions for decentralized and secure [ML](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.51.51.51). The application of these FL frameworks extends across diverse domains, promising improved model accuracy and privacy preservation. The first work introducing [FL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.31.31.31) in [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) is presented in \[[99](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib99)\]. The authors introduced a FL platform that is easily generalizable, incorporating hierarchical optimization and a gradient selection algorithm to enhance training time and SR performance. Guliani et al. \[[100](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib100)\] proposed a strategy to compensate [non-identically distributed](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.56.56.56) ([non-IID](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.56.56.56)) data in federated training of [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) systems. The proposed strategy involved random client data sampling, which resulted in a cost-quality trade-off. Zhu et al. \[[101](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib101)\] addressed also FL-based [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) in [non-IID](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.56.56.56) scenarios with personalized [FL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.31.31.31). DecoupleFL reduces computation on clients by shifting the computation burden to the server. Additionally, it communicates secure high-level features instead of model parameters, reducing communication costs, particularly for large models. In \[[102](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib102)\], the authors proposed a client-adaptive federated training scheme to mitigate data heterogeneity when training [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) models. Nguyen et al. \[[103](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib103)\] used FL to train an [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) model based on a wav2vec 2.0 model pre-trained by self supervision. Yang et al. \[[104](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib104)\] proposed a decentralized feature extraction approach in federated learning. ### 3.4 DRL-based ASR

\Ac

DRL is a [ML](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.51.51.51) paradigm where an agent learns optimal decision-making by interacting with an environment. The agent receives feedback in the form of rewards or penalties, adapting its behavior to maximize cumulative reward over time through a trial-and-error process. [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22) involves several key concepts, as defined in the following terms: Environment model, serving as a representation of contextual dynamics; State ($`s`$), denoting the current situation perceived by the agent; Observation ($`o`$), a subset of the state directly perceived by the agent; Action ($`a`$), the decision made by the agent in response to the environment; Policy ($`\pi`$), describing how the agent converts environmental conditions into actions; Agent, the entity making decisions based on current states and past experiences; Reward, numerical values assigned by the environment to the agent based on state-action interactions. Figure [10](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#S3.F10 "Figure 10 ‣ 3.4 DRL-based ASR ‣ 3 Advanced ASR methods and applications ‣ Automatic Speech Recognition using Advanced Deep Learning Approaches: A survey") illustrates the principle of [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22).

![Refer to caption]()

Figure 10: DRL principle.

[Markov decision process](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.48.48.48) ([MDP](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.48.48.48)) is a fundamental framework for dynamic and stochastic decision-making, characterized by state space $`S`$, action space $`\mathbb{A}`$, transition probabilities $`\mathbb{P}`$, and a reward function $`R`$. The primary objective in an [MDP](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.48.48.48) is to identify an optimal policy $`\pi^{\ast}`$ maximizing the expected discounted total reward over time, expressed as:

``` math
{\pi^{\ast} = {{\max\limits_{\pi}{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}}{\lbrack{\sum\limits_{t = 0}^{T}{\gamma^{t}r_{t}{(s_{t},{\pi{(s_{t})}})}}}\rbrack}}},
```

where $`\gamma`$ is the discount factor. [MDPs](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.48.48.48) find extensive applications in addressing uncertainties in intelligent systems within dynamic wireless environments, including spectrum management, cognitive radios, and wireless security.

In the field of [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6), [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22) has primarily been proposed to tackle discrepancies between the training and testing phases. Two main discrepancies leading to potential performance deterioration have been identified: 1) The conventional use of the cross-entropy criterion maximizes log-likelihood during training, while performance is assessed by [WER](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.85.85.85), not log-likelihood; 2) The teacher-forcing method, which relies on ground truth during training, implies that the model has never encountered its own predictions before testing. [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22) addresses these discrepancies by bridging the gap between the training and testing phases. Several [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22)-based approaches for [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) have been proposed \[[111](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib111), [112](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib112), [113](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib113), [114](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib114), [115](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib115), [116](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib116), [117](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib117), [118](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib118), [119](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib119), [120](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib120)\]. For example, in \[[111](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib111)\], the authors introduced a [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22)-based optimization method for the [S2S](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.63.63.63) [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) task called [self-critical sequence training](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.65.65.65) ([SCST](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.65.65.65)). This method can be conceptualized as a sequential decision model, depicted in Figure [11](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#S3.F11 "Figure 11 ‣ 3.4 DRL-based ASR ‣ 3 Advanced ASR methods and applications ‣ Automatic Speech Recognition using Advanced Deep Learning Approaches: A survey"). The entire encoder-decoder neural network is treated as an agent. At each time step $`t`$, the current state $`s_{t}`$ is formed by concatenating the acoustic feature $`x_{t}`$ and the previous prediction $`Y_{t - 1}`$. The output token serves as the action, updating the generated hypotheses sequence. [SCST](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.65.65.65) associates training loss and [WER](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.85.85.85) using a WER-related reward function, calculating the reward $`r_{t}`$ at each token generation step by comparing it with the ground truth sequence $`Y^{\ast}`$. [SCST](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.65.65.65) uses the test-time beam search algorithm to sample hypotheses for reward normalization, assigning positive weights to high-reward hypotheses that outperform the current test-time system and negative weights to low-reward hypotheses. The framework is illustrated in Figure [11](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#S3.F11 "Figure 11 ‣ 3.4 DRL-based ASR ‣ 3 Advanced ASR methods and applications ‣ Automatic Speech Recognition using Advanced Deep Learning Approaches: A survey").

Figure 11: Example of sequential decision model of DRL-based [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) \[[111](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib111)\].

In \[[112](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib112)\], the authors developed a [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22) framework for speech recognition systems using the policy gradient method. They introduced a [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22) method within this framework, incorporating user feedback through hypothesis selection. Tjandra et al. \[[113](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib113), [114](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib114)\] also employed policy gradient [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22) to train a [S2S](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.63.63.63) [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) model. In \[[115](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib115)\], the authors constructed a generic [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22)-based AutoML system. This system automatically optimizes per-layer compression ratios for a [SOTA](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.71.71.71) attention-based end-to-end [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) model, which consists of multiple [LSTM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.45.45.45) layers. The compression method employed in this work is [singular value decomposition](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.75.75.75) ([SVD](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.75.75.75)) low-rank matrix factorization. The authors improved this approach by combining iterative compression with AutoML-based rank searching, achieving over 5 x [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) compression without degrading the WER \[[116](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib116)\]. Shen et al. \[[117](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib117)\] suggested employing [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22) to optimize a [SE](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.67.67.67) model based on recognition results, aiming to directly enhance [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) performance. AutoML-based [low-rank factorization](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.44.44.44) ([LRF](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.44.44.44)) achieves up to 3.7× speedup. In the shade of this, Mehrotra et al. in their work \[[116](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib116)\] propose an iterative AutoML-based [LRF](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.44.44.44) that employs [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22) for the iterative search, surpassing 5× compression without degrading [WERs](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.85.85.85), advancing [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6). ## 4 Open Issues and Key challenges

Integrating advanced techniques like [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25), [FL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.31.31.31), and [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22) into [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) systems presents exciting opportunities but comes with its set of challenges. This section delves into the distinct challenges associated with each approach, emphasizing the critical areas that demand attention and innovation.

### 4.1 Transformer-based

Transformer-based [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) systems have revolutionized the field of speech processing with their superior ability to handle sequential data. However, their deployment in the acoustic and language domains presents unique challenges and open issues. For the acoustic domain, one of the key challenges in the [AM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.4.4.4) is the handling of long audio sequences. Transformers require significant memory and computational resources, making it difficult to process lengthy audio inputs efficiently. Additionally, the variability in speech, such as accents, speech disorders, and background noise, can affect the robustness of transformer-based [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) systems. Developing models that can generalize across these variations without substantial data for each scenario remains an open issue. For the language domain, the major challenge in the [LM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.42.42.42) lies in capturing the nuances of different languages and dialects. The transformer’s reliance on large amounts of training data poses a challenge for low-resource languages. Furthermore, language models need to understand context deeply to accurately predict words in continuous speech, which can be particularly challenging in languages with rich morphology. For both domains, integrating acoustic and language models in transformer-based [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) systems to work seamlessly is complex. Achieving real-time processing speeds while maintaining high accuracy is a persistent challenge. Furthermore, the interpretability of these models is limited, making it difficult to diagnose and correct errors in [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) predictions. Addressing the trade-off between model complexity and the ability to deploy these systems on devices with limited computing power is also a critical challenge.

### 4.2 DTL and DA-based

This section discusses challenges and concepts related to [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) and [DA](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.17.17.17) in speech recognition, including distribution shift, feature space adaptation, label distribution shift, catastrophic forgetting, domain-invariant feature learning, sample selection bias, and hyperparameter optimization.

When applying a model trained on one domain (source) to another (target), a distribution shift often occurs, referred to as domain shift. Formally, if $`P_{S}{(X,Y)}`$ and $`P_{T}{(X,Y)}`$ represent the joint distributions of features $`X`$ and labels $`Y`$ in the source and target domains, respectively, the challenge arises when $`{P_{S}{(X,Y)}} \neq {P_{T}{(X,Y)}}`$. To address this, techniques focus on learning a transformation of the feature space to minimize the difference between the source and target distributions. This involves finding a mapping function $`f:{X\rightarrow Z}`$, where $`Z`$ is a latent space in which the distributions of transformed features $`f{(X_{S})}`$ and $`f{(X_{T})}`$ are more similar, quantified using measures such as the [maximum mean discrepancy](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.52.52.52) ([MMD](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.52.52.52)). Label distribution shift occurs when the distributions of labels ($`P_{S}{(Y)}`$ vs. $`P_{T}{(Y)}`$) differ, even if the feature distributions align. This poses challenges, especially with underrepresented classes in the target domain. Addressing this mathematically involves adjusting the model or learning process, possibly by re-weighting the loss function based on class distribution estimates. Catastrophic forgetting is a risk during fine-tuning on a new domain, where the model may lose its performance on the original task. Balancing loss functions ($`L_{S}`$ for the source and $`L_{T}`$ for the target) is crucial, often weighted by a hyperparameter $`\lambda`$ to control their importance. Domain-invariant feature learning aims to learn features invariant across domains while remaining predictive. It involves optimizing a feature extractor $`f`$ and predictor $`g`$ to minimize the $`D_{S}`$ loss $`L_{S}`$ and the domain discrepancy (e.g., [MMD](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.52.52.52)). The problem of sample selection bias occurs in selecting samples for [DA](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.17.17.17), affecting the effectiveness of adaptation strategies. Mathematically, addressing this bias involves weighting or selecting samples to minimize it, often using importance sampling or re-weighting techniques. Hyperparameter optimization is critical in [DA](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.17.17.17), where the choice of hyperparameters (e.g., $`\lambda`$) significantly impacts performance. Finding the optimal hyperparameters typically involves solving complex optimization problems using techniques like grid search, random search, or Bayesian optimization on a validation set.

Moreover, the unification of [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) in [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) poses a challenge due to the varied mathematical formulations used in different studies. While efforts have been made to unify definitions and formulations, further work is needed for a consistent understanding of [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25). Speech-based [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) processing faces challenges compared to image-based processing due to potential mismatches between source and target databases arising from factors like language, speakers, age groups, ethnicity, and acoustic environments. The [CTC](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.15.15.15) approach, while promising, is limited by the assumption of frame independence. Cross-lingual [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) challenges include incorporating linguistic characteristics from multiple sources and integrating knowledge at different hierarchical levels, considering linguistic differences. Finally, computational burden remains a significant challenge in [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) and [DA](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.17.17.17) processes. Knowledge transfer between domains can incur additional computational costs, especially considering the extensive computational resources required for deep architectures inherent in [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) techniques.

### 4.3 FL-based

[FL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.31.31.31) has significant potential for [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) systems, particularly in enhancing privacy and personalization. However, deploying this technology in [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) also comes with a set of challenges. Typically, in [FL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.31.31.31), data is inherently decentralized and can vary significantly across devices. This heterogeneity in speech data—due to differences in accents, dialects, languages, and background noise—can make it challenging to train a model that performs well across all nodes. Ensuring robustness and generalization of the [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) model under these conditions is a complex task. Moving on, [FL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.31.31.31) requires periodic communication between the central server and the devices to update the model. For [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) systems, where models can be quite large, this can result in substantial communication overhead. Optimizing the efficiency of these updates, in terms of both bandwidth usage and energy consumption, especially on mobile devices, is a significant challenge. Besides, although [FL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.31.31.31) is designed to enhance privacy by not sharing raw data, there are still privacy challenges. For instance, it’s possible to infer sensitive information from model updates. Ensuring that these updates do not leak private information about the users’ speech data is a critical concern that requires sophisticated privacy-preserving techniques like differential privacy or secure multi-party computation.

Additionally, one of the advantages of [FL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.31.31.31) is the ability to personalize models based on local data. However, balancing personalization with the need for a generally effective model—especially in a diverse ecosystem with varying speech patterns—is challenging. Achieving this balance without compromising the model’s overall performance or the personalization benefits is a key challenge. Moving forward, [FL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.31.31.31) systems need to manage potentially thousands or millions of devices participating in the training process. Scalability issues, including managing updates from such a large and potentially unreliable network of devices, ensuring consistent model improvements, and handling devices joining or leaving the network, are significant technical hurdles. Lastly, in [FL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.31.31.31), the distribution of data across devices is often [non-IID](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.56.56.56). This means that the speech data on one device might be very different from that on another, leading to challenges in training a model that generalizes well across all devices. Overcoming the bias introduced by [non-IID](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.56.56.56) data is a major challenge in [FL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.31.31.31) for [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6).

### 4.4 DRL-based

Using [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22) in [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) systems offers promising avenues for improvement but also presents several challenges. Specifically, one of the primary challenges in applying [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22) to [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) is the issue of sparse and delayed rewards. In many [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) tasks, the system only receives feedback (rewards or penalties) after processing lengthy sequences of speech, making it difficult to attribute the reward to specific actions or decisions. This delay complicates the learning process, as the model struggles to identify which actions led to successful outcomes. Moreover, balancing exploration, trying new actions to discover their effects, with exploitation, using known actions that yield the best results, is a critical challenge in [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22). In the context of [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6), this means the system must balance between adhering to known speech patterns and exploring new patterns or interpretations. Overemphasis on exploration can lead to inaccurate transcriptions, while excessive exploitation may prevent the model from adapting to new speakers or accents. Additionally, [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22) models typically require a significant amount of interaction data to learn effectively. In [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6), obtaining large volumes of labeled speech data, especially with user feedback, can be challenging and expensive. Additionally, [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22) algorithms can be sample-inefficient, meaning they need a lot of data before they start performing well, which can be a bottleneck in practical applications.

Moving forward, most [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) systems are built using supervised learning techniques that rely on vast amounts of annotated data. Integrating [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22) into these systems poses technical challenges, as it requires a different training paradigm that focuses on learning from user interactions and feedback rather than static datasets. Besides, using [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22) in [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) often involves collecting and analyzing user feedback and interactions to improve the model. This raises concerns about user privacy and data security, as sensitive information might be inadvertently captured and used for training. Ensuring that data is handled securely and in compliance with privacy regulations is a significant challenge.

Designing an appropriate reward function that accurately reflects the desired outcomes in [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) is challenging. The reward function must capture the nuances of speech recognition, such as accuracy, naturalness, and user satisfaction, which can be difficult to quantify. Poorly designed reward functions can lead to suboptimal learning outcomes or unintended behaviors. Lastly, [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) systems are used in a wide range of environments, from quiet offices to noisy streets. [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22) models need to adapt to these varying conditions, but training them to handle such diversity can be complex. The environment’s variability requires models that can generalize well across different acoustic conditions, which remains a challenge for [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22)-based [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) systems.

## 5 Future directions

### 5.1 Personalized data augmentation for dysarthric and older people

While [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) technologies have advanced significantly, especially in recognizing typical speech patterns, they still struggle to accurately identify speech from individuals with dysarthria or older adults \[[121](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib121)\]. Gathering extensive datasets from these groups is challenging due to mobility limitations often associated with these populations. In this context, personalized data augmentation plays a crucial role \[[122](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib122), [123](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib123)\]. Personalized data augmentation tailors the training process to accommodate the unique speech patterns and challenges associated with these groups. Dysarthria, a motor speech disorder, and the natural aging process can lead to speech that deviates from the normative models typically used to train [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) systems, making accurate recognition difficult \[[124](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib124)\]. Personalized data augmentation introduces a wider range of speech variations into the training dataset, including those specific to dysarthric speakers or older adults. This can include variations in speech rate, pitch, articulation, and clarity. By training on this augmented dataset, the [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) system learns to recognize and accurately transcribe speech that exhibits these characteristics \[[125](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib125)\]. Moreover, this helps the [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) models generalize better to unseen examples of speech from dysarthric speakers or older adults. This enhanced generalization is crucial for real-world applications where the system encounters a wide range of speech variations. Moving forward, personalized data augmentation can employ specific techniques tailored to the needs of dysarthric speakers or older adults, such as simulating the slurring of words, varying speech tempo, or introducing background noise \[[126](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib126)\], commonly challenging for these groups \[[127](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib127)\]. Techniques like pitch perturbation, temporal stretching, and adding noise can simulate real-world conditions more accurately for these users. A typical example is presented in \[[128](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib128)\], where a unique approach utilizes speaker-dependent [generative adversarial networks](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.34.34.34) has been proposed.

### 5.2 Multitask learning for ASR

\Ac

MTL enhances the performance of [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) systems by leveraging the inherent relatedness of multiple learning tasks to improve the generalization of the primary [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) task. This approach allows the [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) model to learn shared representations that capture underlying patterns across different but related tasks, leading to several key benefits \[[129](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib129)\]. Typically, [multitask learning](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.54.54.54) ([MTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.54.54.54)) encourages the [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) system to learn representations that are beneficial across multiple tasks. This can lead to more robust feature extraction, as the model is not optimized solely for transcribing speech but also for other related tasks, such as speaker identification or emotion recognition. This shared learning process helps in capturing a broader range of speech characteristics, which can improve the [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) system’s ability to handle varied speech inputs. Moving on, by simultaneously learning related tasks, [MTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.54.54.54) acts as a form of regularization, reducing the risk of overfitting on the primary [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) task. This is because the model must find a solution that performs well across all tasks, preventing it from relying too heavily on noise or idiosyncrasies specific to the training data of the main task. Besides, learning auxiliary tasks alongside the main [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) task can improve the model’s generalization capabilities. For example, learning to identify the speaker or the language can provide additional contextual clues that help the [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) system better understand and transcribe ambiguous audio signals.

Additionally, [MTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.54.54.54) can make more efficient use of available data by leveraging auxiliary tasks for which more data might be available. In scenarios where annotated data for [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) is limited, incorporating additional tasks with more abundant data can help improve the learning process and performance of the [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) system. Moreover, [MTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.54.54.54) allows [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) systems to better handle acoustic variability in speech, such as accents, dialects, or noisy environments, by incorporating tasks that directly or indirectly encourage the model to learn features that are invariant to these variations. Last but not least, modern [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) systems often employ [DL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.20.20.20) architectures that can benefit from end-to-end learning strategies. [MTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.54.54.54) fits naturally into this paradigm, allowing for the joint optimization of multiple objectives within a single model architecture. This can simplify the training process and reduce the need for separately trained models or handcrafted features.

### 5.3 Federated multi-task learning and distillation for ASR

\Ac

FMTL extends the concept of [FL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.31.31.31) by allowing each client to learn a personalized model that addresses its specific task, while still benefiting from collaboration with other clients. This approach recognizes the heterogeneity in clients’ data distributions and tasks. Mathematically and compared with [FL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.31.31.31) (Equation [3](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#S3.E3 "In 3.3 FL-based ASR ‣ 3 Advanced ASR methods and applications ‣ Automatic Speech Recognition using Advanced Deep Learning Approaches: A survey")), [federated multi-task learning](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.32.32.32) ([FMTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.32.32.32)) can be formulated as:

``` math
{\min\limits_{\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\ldots,\theta_{K}}{\sum\limits_{k = 1}^{K}{F_{k}{(\theta_{k})}}}} + {\lambda R{(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\ldots,\theta_{K})}}
```

Different from [FL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.31.31.31), $`R{(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\ldots,\theta_{K})}`$ is a regularization term that encourages some form of similarity or sharing among the model parameters of different tasks, promoting collaboration among clients. $`\lambda`$ is a regularization coefficient that balances the trade-off between fitting the local data well and collaborating with other clients. [FMTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.32.32.32) has task-specific model parameters $`\theta_{k}`$ for each client, where only a single global model parameter $`\theta`$ in [FL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.31.31.31).

In this regard, [FMTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.32.32.32) offers a promising approach to improving [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) systems while also enhancing privacy and security measures. This learning paradigm extends the traditional [FL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.31.31.31) model by enabling the simultaneous training of multiple tasks across distributed devices or nodes, without the need to share raw data \[[130](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib130)\]. [FMTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.32.32.32) leverages data from a wide range of devices and users, each potentially offering unique speech data, accents, dialects, and noise conditions. This diversity helps in training more robust [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) models that can perform well across various speech patterns and environments \[[131](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib131)\]. By learning from many tasks simultaneously, [FMTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.32.32.32) can personalize [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) models to individual users or specific groups without compromising the model’s general performance \[[132](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib132)\]. This is particularly beneficial for users with unique speech patterns, such as those with accents or speech impairments. Moreover, [FMTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.32.32.32) encourages the development of compact models that can handle multiple tasks efficiently. For [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) systems, this means that a single model can potentially perform speech recognition, speaker identification, and even emotion detection, reducing the computational overhead on client devices \[[133](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib133)\].

On the other hand, in [FMTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.32.32.32), raw data remains on the user’s device and does not need to be shared or transferred to a central server. This inherently reduces the risk of data breaches and unauthorized access, as sensitive speech data is not centralized \[[134](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib134)\]. Additionally, [FMTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.32.32.32) can be combined with differential privacy techniques to further anonymize the model updates sent from devices to the central server. This ensures that the shared information does not reveal sensitive details about the data or the user, enhancing privacy protection \[[135](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib135)\]. Moving on, the aggregation process in [FMTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.32.32.32) can be secured using cryptographic techniques, ensuring that the aggregated model updates cannot be traced back to individual users. This secure aggregation process protects user privacy while allowing the benefits of collective learning \[[136](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib136)\]. Lastly, by aggregating model updates from a wide range of tasks and users, [FMTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.32.32.32) can improve the system’s robustness to malicious attempts at data poisoning. The diversity of inputs helps in diluting the impact of any adversarial data introduced to compromise the model.

Delving deeper into techniques for [FL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.31.31.31) distillation (optimizing model size) within [FL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.31.31.31) frameworks is essential. This exploration involves researching methods to compress neural [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) models effectively while ensuring their performance remains intact, especially tailored for edge devices with storage and computational constraints. It is imperative to investigate the trade-offs associated with reducing model size while maintaining performance metrics like [WER](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.85.85.85). Developing strategies to strike a balance between downsizing models and preserving satisfactory performance levels within [FL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.31.31.31) environments is crucial.

### 5.4 Recent DRL techniques for [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6)

Exploring incremental [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22) approaches \[[137](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib137), [138](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib138), [139](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib139)\] in [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22)-based [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) systems, could be very interesting. This approach involves the model continuously learning from newly acquired data and dynamically adjusting its ASR functionalities over time. By incrementally updating its knowledge base, the model can enhance its performance without necessitating full retraining, thus enabling continual enhancement of [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) systems. This capability not only fosters greater resilience and adaptability in speech recognition capabilities but also offers potential applications in scenarios where real-time adaptation to changing conditions is crucial, such as in noisy environments or with varying speaker accents. Moreover, incremental [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22) can potentially lead to more efficient use of computational resources, as the model only needs to focus on learning from new data, rather than reprocessing the entire dataset. Further research in this area could unlock new possibilities for [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) systems to evolve and improve over time, ultimately enhancing their usability and effectiveness in diverse real-world settings.

Although some [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) schemes based on [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22) have been proposed, there remains a notable scarcity in the application of [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22) techniques to enhance [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) methods. While policy gradient and Q-learning are commonly employed, the realm of [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22) encompasses various subcategories such as [double deep Q-network](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.19.19.19) ([DDQN](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.19.19.19)), [actor-critic](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.2.2.2) ([AC](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.2.2.2)), [deep deterministic policy gradien](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.18.18.18) ([DDPG](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.18.18.18)), and more \[[140](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib140)\], which hold promise for advancing [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) with innovative approaches. Researchers are encouraged to delve into these diverse [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22)-based methods to further enrich the field of [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) for both [AM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.4.4.4) and [LM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.42.42.42) fields.

### 5.5 Online [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25)

Online [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) combines the principles of DTL and online learning with [DNNs](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.21.21.21), enabling models to adapt in real-time to new tasks or data distributions. This approach is beneficial in dynamic environments where data arrives sequentially. [DL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.20.20.20) models, specifically [DNNs](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.21.21.21), learn through optimizing the weights $`\theta`$ to minimize a loss function $`L`$, which measures the discrepancy between predicted outputs $`\hat{y}`$ and true outputs $`y`$: $`\theta^{\ast} = {{\arg{\min_{\theta}L}}{(D;\theta)}}`$. On the other hand, DTL improves learning in a new target task through the transfer of knowledge from a related source task, adapting a pre-trained model $`\theta_{S}`$ on $`D_{S}`$ to a $`D_{T}`$: $`\theta_{T}^{\ast} = {{\arg{\min_{\theta}L}}{(D_{T};\theta_{T})}}`$. In this regard, online learning updates the model incrementally as new data $`(x_{t},y_{t})`$ arrives:

``` math
\theta_{t + 1} = {\theta_{t} - {\alpha_{t}{\nabla_{\theta}L}{(y_{t},{f{(x_{t};\theta_{t})}})}}}
```

Where $`\alpha_{t}`$ is the learning rate, and $`\nabla_{\theta}L`$ is the gradient of the loss with respect to $`\theta`$. Moving on, online [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) integrates these concepts to continuously adapt a deep learning model to new tasks or data streams, often involving techniques such as feature extraction, fine-tuning, model adaptation, and continual learning. The adaptation process at each time step $`t`$ can be viewed as $`\theta_{t + 1}^{\ast} = {{\arg{\min_{\theta}L}}{(D_{T_{t}};\theta_{T_{t}})}}`$. Where $`D_{T_{t}}`$ represents the data available at time $`t`$, including new target domain data.

The approach of online [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) offers a forward-looking solution to this issue. Particularly, discrepancies in class distributions and the representation of features between the $`D_{S}`$ and $`D_{T}`$ amplify the complexity of online [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) \[[141](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib141)\]. To navigate the complexities mentioned, online [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) has been dissected into two primary methodologies. The first, known as homogeneous online [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25), operates on the premise of a unified feature space across both domains. Conversely, heterogeneous online [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) acknowledges the distinct feature spaces intrinsic to each domain \[[142](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib142)\]. An exemplary solution to the challenges of heterogeneous online [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) includes leveraging unlabeled instances of co-occurrence to forge a connective bridge between the $`D_{S}`$ and $`D_{T}`$, facilitating the precursor to knowledge transfer \[[143](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib143)\]. Furthering the discourse, online [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) augmented with extreme learning machines introduces a novel framework \[[144](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#bib.bib144)\]. Addressing the challenge of limited data in the $`D_{T}`$, the technique of [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) with lag, rooted in shallow neural network embeddings, has been applied. This method ensures the continuity of knowledge transfer, notwithstanding fluctuations in the feature set.

### 5.6 Transformers and LLMs-based ASR

\Acp

LLM and Transformers represent the forefront of [AI](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.3.3.3), trained on vast datasets spanning various domains, including text, speech, images, and multi-modal inputs. Despite extensive research on [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6), existing [SOTA](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.71.71.71) approaches often lack integration of advanced [AI](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.3.3.3) techniques like [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22) and [FL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.31.31.31) into both [AM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.4.4.4) and [LM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.42.42.42) domains. For example, \AcLLM based on [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) has demonstrated significant potential for [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) tasks, particularly for both [LM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.42.42.42) and [AM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.4.4.4) components. The incorporation of [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) techniques into [large language model](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.41.41.41) ([LLM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.41.41.41)) facilitates the transfer of knowledge from extensive pre-training tasks to enhance [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) effectiveness. In terms of [AM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.4.4.4), fine-tuning [LLM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.41.41.41) can leverage insights gained from pre-trained models exposed to sample acoustic data. This enables the [AM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.4.4.4) component to grasp acoustic features like spectrograms or Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs) and utilize pre-trained knowledge to improve speech recognition accuracy. Through fine-tuning, the model can adjust and specialize in specific datasets or acoustic domains, leading to enhanced [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) performance.

Similarly, the [LM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.42.42.42) aspect of [LLM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.41.41.41) based on [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) can enhance [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) by leveraging [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25). Pre-training [LLM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.41.41.41) on vast text corpora equips it with extensive language representations, aiding in addressing diverse language challenges. Fine-tuning enables adaptation to specific language characteristics, improving transcription accuracy and contextual appropriateness. Both [AM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.4.4.4) and [LM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.42.42.42) fine-tuning can benefit from [DA](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.17.17.17), incorporating target domain data to tailor models, reducing domain mismatch, and enhancing effectiveness and generalization. Utilizing [LLM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.41.41.41) for objective [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) testing and MOS evaluation involves compiling diverse datasets, fine-tuning [LLM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.41.41.41), and integrating it into the [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) system. Evaluation metrics like [CER](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.11.11.11) and Pearson’s correlation gauge system performance, guiding further fine-tuning iterations for improved results. This iterative process ensures the [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) system’s continual enhancement and accurate MOS scale generation. Researchers are invited to explore these gaps and advance the integration of more advanced [AI](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.3.3.3) techniques such as [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22) and [FL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.31.31.31) into both [AM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.4.4.4) and [LM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.42.42.42) domains within Transformer-based models. Additionally, there is a need for further investigation into Transformers and [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25)-based ASR schemes specifically tailored for the [LM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.42.42.42) domain. Closing these gaps will contribute to the development of more robust and effective language models across various applications.

## 6 Conclusion

In conclusion, recent advancements in deep learning have presented both challenges and opportunities for [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6). Traditional [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) systems require extensive training datasets, often including confidential information, and consume significant computational resources. However, the demand for adaptive systems capable of performing well in dynamic environments has spurred the development of advanced deep learning techniques such as [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25), [FL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.31.31.31), and [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22), with all their variant techniques. These advanced techniques address issues related to [DA](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.17.17.17), privacy preservation, and dynamic decision-making, thereby enhancing [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) performance and reducing computational costs.

This survey has provided a comprehensive review of [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25), [FL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.31.31.31), and [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22)-based [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) frameworks, offering insights into the latest developments and helping researchers and professionals understand current challenges. Additionally, the integration of Transformers, powerful [DL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.20.20.20) models, has been explored for their ability to capture complex dependencies in [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) sequences. By presenting a structured taxonomy and conducting critical analyses, this paper has shed light on the strengths and weaknesses of existing frameworks, as well as highlighted ongoing challenges. Moving forward, further research is needed to overcome these challenges and unlock the full potential of advanced DL techniques in [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25). Future work should focus on refining existing approaches, addressing privacy concerns in [FL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.31.31.31), improving [DRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.22.22.22) algorithms for [DTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.25.25.25) optimization, and exploring innovative ways to leverage Transformers for more efficient and accurate speech recognition. By continuing to innovate and collaborate across disciplines, we can push the boundaries of [ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01255v2#Sx1.6.6.6) technology and realize its transformative impact on various fields, including healthcare, communication, and accessibility.
