###### Abstract

Data scarcity and the modality gap between the speech and text modalities are two major obstacles of end-to-end Speech Translation (ST) systems, thus hindering their performance. Prior work has attempted to mitigate these challenges by leveraging external MT data and optimizing distance metrics that bring closer the speech-text representations. However, achieving competitive results typically requires some ST data. For this reason, we introduce ZeroSwot, a method for zero-shot ST that bridges the modality gap without any paired ST data. Leveraging a novel CTC compression and Optimal Transport, we train a speech encoder using only ASR data, to align with the representation space of a massively multilingual MT model. The speech encoder seamlessly integrates with the MT model at inference, enabling direct translation from speech to text, across all languages supported by the MT model. Our experiments show that we can effectively close the modality gap without ST data, while our results on MuST-C and CoVoST demonstrate our method’s superiority over not only previous zero-shot models, but also supervised ones, achieving state-of-the-art results.¹¹1[https://github.com/mt-upc/ZeroSwot](https://github.com/mt-upc/ZeroSwot)

## 1 Introduction

Traditionally, the standard approach for Speech translation (ST) has been the cascade model Ney ([1999](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib47)); Mathias and Byrne ([2006](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib45)), where Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) and Machine Translation (MT) systems were chained together to produce the desired output. However, in recent years, there has been a paradigm shift towards end-to-end models, which aim to directly map the input speech to the target text without the intermediate transcription step Bérard et al. ([2016](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib10)). Such models offer several advantages, including reduced error propagation, more compact architectures, and faster inference times Sperber and Paulik ([2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib58)).

Figure 1: Proposed Zero-shot Speech Translation

Despite their advantages, end-to-end models require parallel ST data, which are often limited. To mitigate this *data scarcity*, several strategies aim to leverage the more abundant ASR and MT data Bérard et al. ([2018](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib9)); Anastasopoulos and Chiang ([2018](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib3)). Then, another challenge that arises is the *modality gap*, which emerges from the inherent differences between the speech and text modalities, manifesting in *length mismatch* and *representation mismatch*. Convolutional Length Adapters Li et al. ([2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib41)) or Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) Graves et al. ([2006](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib30)); Liu et al. ([2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib43)) can alleviate the length mismatch to some extent. To ease the representation gap, previous works have proposed a shared encoder Ye et al. ([2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib75)) and optimizing distance metrics to bring the speech-text representation closer to the shared semantic space Fang et al. ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib22)); Ye et al. ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib76)). Due to its ability to handle representations of different lengths, the Wasserstein distance Frogner et al. ([2015](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib23)) using Optimal Transport, has emerged as an effective distance metric, specifically during pretraining Le et al. ([2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib40)); Tsiamas et al. ([2023b](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib66)). Although these methods show promise in reducing the modality gap and increasing translation quality, they still require at least some ST data.

To this end, we propose Zero-shot ST with Subword compression and Optimal Transport (ZeroSwot), a novel approach that aims to bridge the modality gap in a zero-shot fashion, only requiring ASR and MT data. Using Optimal Transport, we train a speech encoder based on wav2vec 2.0 Baevski et al. ([2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib6)) to produce representations akin to the embedding space of a massively multilingual MT model, thus enabling zero-shot ST inference across all its supported target languages (Fig. [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Pushing the Limits of Zero-shot End-to-End Speech Translation")). To map the two embedding spaces, our method learns the tokenization of the MT model through CTC, and compresses the speech representations to the corresponding subwords, thus closing the length mismatch. Experiments on MuST-C Cattoni et al. ([2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib12)) reveal that our method is not only better than existing zero-shot models, by a large margin, but also surpasses supervised ones, achieving state-of-the-art results. On CoVoST Wang et al. ([2020b](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib69)), ZeroSwot outperforms the original version of the multimodal SeamlessM4T Seamless-Communication ([2023a](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib56)), while evaluations on the 88 target languages of FLEURS Conneau et al. ([2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib15)) showcase the massively multilingual capacity of our method. ZeroSwot is also vastly superior to comparable CTC-based cascade ST models, and while it is on par with cascades that utilize strong attention-based encoder-decoder ASR models, it ranks better in terms of efficiency. Finally, we provide evidence of our method’s ability to close the modality gap, both in terms of length and representation.

## 2 Relevant Research

### 2.1 End-to-end Speech Translation

Data Scarcity. To alleviate data scarcity, end-to-end ST methods usually utilize the more abundant data of ASR and MT. Such methods include pretraining Bérard et al. ([2018](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib9)); Bansal et al. ([2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib7)); Wang et al. ([2020a](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib68)); Alinejad and Sarkar ([2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib1)), multi-task learning Anastasopoulos and Chiang ([2018](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib3)); Indurthi et al. ([2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib34)), data augmentation Jia et al. ([2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib35)); Pino et al. ([2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib54)); Lam et al. ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib39)); Tsiamas et al. ([2023a](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib64)), and knowledge distillation Liu et al. ([2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib42)); Gaido et al. ([2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib25)). Furthermore, many methods indirectly leverage external data through large foundation models Bommasani et al. ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib11)). Initializing parts of the ST model with wav2vec 2.0 Baevski et al. ([2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib6)) and mBART50 Tang et al. ([2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib62)) is a quite common practice Li et al. ([2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib41)); Ye et al. ([2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib75)); Han et al. ([2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib31)); Tsiamas et al. ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib65)), while more recently Gow-Smith et al. ([2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib29)) utilized a massively multilingual MT model NLLB et al. ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib48)) and Zhang et al. ([2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib77)) a large language model (LLaMa2) Touvron et al. ([2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib63)). Similarly, here employ wav2vec 2.0 and NLLB NLLB et al. ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib48)) to ease the data scarcity issue.

Modality Gap. Implicit alignment techniques to bridge the representation gap involve a shared semantic encoder in a multitasking framework Liu et al. ([2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib43)); Ye et al. ([2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib75)); Tang et al. ([2021b](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib61)), which can be improved by mixup Fang et al. ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib22)); Zhou et al. ([2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib80)). In addition, explicit alignment methods optimize a distance metric to bring the speech-text representations closer in the shared semantic space. Such distance metrics include the euclidean Liu et al. ([2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib43)); Tang et al. ([2021a](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib60)), cosine Chuang et al. ([2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib14)), and contrastive Ye et al. ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib76)); Ouyang et al. ([2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib50)), but they typically require some transformation in the representations, such as mean-pooling, while our approach optimizes a distance that does not alter the representation space. Methods to reduce the length discrepancy usually include sub-sampling the speech representation using convolutional length adaptors Li et al. ([2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib41)); Gállego et al. ([2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib26)); Fang et al. ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib22)); Zhao et al. ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib79)) or character/phoneme-based CTC compression Liu et al. ([2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib43)); Gaido et al. ([2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib24)); Xu et al. ([2021a](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib72)). Several methods have also used phonemized text in order to better match the representations in both length and content Tang et al. ([2021a](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib60), [2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib59)); Le et al. ([2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib40)), but potentially limiting the quality of the text branch due to noise. In this work, we introduce a novel CTC-based compression to subword units, directly aligning with the tokenization of MT models.

### 2.2 Optimal Transport

Optimal Transport (OT) Peyré and Cuturi ([2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib53)), traditionally used in NLP and MT Chen et al. ([2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib13)); Alqahtani et al. ([2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib2)), has recently also been applied to ST. Zhou et al. ([2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib80)) used OT to find the alignment between speech and text features to apply Mixup. Le et al. ([2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib40)) proposed to use OT in a siamese pretraining setting in combination with CTC, yielding improvements compared to the standard ASR pretraining, while also proposing a positional regularization in order to make OT applicable to sequences. Tsiamas et al. ([2023b](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib66)) extended this pretraining in the context of foundation models, while also freezing the text branch and using CTC compression in order to achieve better adaptation with the text decoder during ST finetuning. Inspired by these works, we also follow the siamese paradigm, but as a means of directly integrating the speech encoder to the text space of an MT model, thus not requiring any ST data.

### 2.3 Zero-shot Speech Translation

Escolano et al. ([2021b](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib20)) were the first to achieve zero-shot ST, by incorporating a speech encoder to the representation space of an MT model using language-specific encoders-decoders Escolano et al. ([2021a](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib19)). Dinh et al. ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib17)) combined multi-task learning on ASR and MT, introducing an auxiliary loss to minimize the euclidean distance between mean-pooled speech and text representations. T-modules Duquenne et al. ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib18)) harnessed mined data to learn a fixed-size multilingual and multimodal representation space, subsequently enabling zero-shot ST encoding and decoding from that space. DCMA Wang et al. ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib70)) projects both speech and text into a fixed-length joint space using an attention-driven shared memory module, after which it enforces the speech distribution of a codebook to closely mirror its textual counterpart. Gao et al. ([2023b](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib28)) proposed a strategy that employs a multimodal variant of cross-lingual consistency regularization Gao et al. ([2023a](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib27)), training a model with wav2vec 2.0 and a shared encoder on both MT and ASR data. Recently, Yang et al. ([2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib74)) introduced a zero-shot ST training method by adapting a new speech encoder to bilingual MT models using OT and a CTC module that shares the vocabulary of the MT model, attaining improvements over zero-shot methods and CTC-based cascade systems. In contrast, our approach focuses on achieving multilingual zero-shot ST, by adapting a large CTC-based speech encoder Baevski et al. ([2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib6)) to a massively multilingual MT model NLLB et al. ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib48)), aiming for high-quality results comparable to those of state-of-the-art cascade and end-to-end models. To address the unique challenges associated with adapting to the large vocabulary size of multilingual models (e.g., 250k tokens for NLLB et al. ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib48))), we propose a novel compression method that effectively learns the subword tokenization of the MT model, rather than predicting actual tokens. This decoupling from the vocabulary size ensures efficiency and scalability, overcoming limitations that may arise in previous methods Yang et al. ([2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib74)), due to the need of sharing the MT vocabulary with the CTC module.

### 3.1 Problem Definition & Proposed Solution

End-to-end Speech Translation. A speech translation corpus consists of speech-transcription-translation triplets, $`\mathcal{D} = {\{{(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}\}}`$, where $`\mathbf{s}`$ is the speech signal, $`\mathbf{x}`$ is the transcription, and $`\mathbf{y}`$ the translation. End-to-end ST systems aim to learn a model $`\mathcal{M}^{\text{ST}}`$ that generates the translation $`\mathbf{y}`$ directly from the speech $`\mathbf{s}`$, using the parallel ST data $`\mathcal{D}^{\text{ST}} = {\{{(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{y})}\}}`$. The transcription $`\mathbf{x}`$ is optionally utilized, as in pretraining or multi-task learning.

Zero-shot Speech Translation. In a zero-shot ST setting, we aim to learn a model $`\mathcal{M}^{\text{ZS-ST}}`$ that generates the translation $`\mathbf{y}`$ with access to corpora $`\mathcal{D}^{\text{MT}} = {\{{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}\}}`$ and $`\mathcal{D}^{\text{ASR}} = {\{{(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{x})}\}}`$, but without directly utilizing the parallel corpus $`\mathcal{D}^{\text{ST}}`$.

Proposed Solution. Assuming access to an MT model $`\mathcal{M}^{\text{MT}}`$ obtained via $`\mathcal{D}^{\text{MT}}`$, we utilize $`\mathcal{D}^{\text{ASR}}`$ to learn a speech encoder $`\mathbf{S}\text{-}{\mathbf{E}\mathbf{n}\mathbf{c}}`$ that produces representations akin to those expected by $`\mathcal{M}^{\text{MT}}`$. On inference time, we achieve zero-shot ST by replacing the embedding layer of $`\mathcal{M}^{\text{MT}}`$ with $`\mathbf{S}\text{-}{\mathbf{E}\mathbf{n}\mathbf{c}}`$.

### 3.2 Model Architecture

Our model architecture includes a speech and a text branch. The text branch consists of a text encoder, which remains frozen during training. The speech branch consists of an acoustic encoder, a CTC module, a compression adapter, a speech embedder and a semantic encoder, of which the parameters are shared with the frozen text encoder (Figure [2](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Pushing the Limits of Zero-shot End-to-End Speech Translation")a).

The text encoder is a Transformer, of which the parameters are initialized from a massively multilingual MT model NLLB et al. ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib48)), and kept frozen during training. The labels $`\mathbf{z}`$ are usually obtained from the transcription $`\mathbf{x}`$ via splitting on words and removing characters absent from $`\mathcal{V}`$, such as punctuation. But this implies a word-level tokenization and text normalization, which is inconsistent with the text branch (§[3.2.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#S3.SS2.SSS1 "3.2.1 Text Encoder ‣ 3.2 Model Architecture ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Pushing the Limits of Zero-shot End-to-End Speech Translation")). #### 3.2.4 Compression Adapter

![Refer to caption](https://arxiv.org/html/extracted/5646934/figures/compression-example-red.png)

Figure 3: Example of the proposed compression.

The compression adapter (Fig. [2](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Pushing the Limits of Zero-shot End-to-End Speech Translation")b) takes as input the acoustic representation $`\mathbf{a}`$ and the CTC probabilities $`\mathbf{p}`$, to produce a compressed acoustic representation $`\mathbf{a}^{\text{sw}} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{n^{\text{sw}} \times d}`$ (where $`n^{\text{sw}} \leq n`$) that resembles in length and content the text embedding $`\mathbf{e}^{\text{x}}`$ (Eq. [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#S3.E1 "In 3.2.1 Text Encoder ‣ 3.2 Model Architecture ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Pushing the Limits of Zero-shot End-to-End Speech Translation")). It involves two stages: compression to characters Liu et al. ([2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib43)), followed by a novel compression to subwords.

Character Compression. Each vector $`\mathbf{a}_{i} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{d}`$ of the acoustic representation is labeled according to the corresponding greedy CTC prediction $`\pi_{i} = {\text{argmax}{(\mathbf{p}_{i})}}`$. Then, consecutive same-labeled representations are grouped together, and combined via mean-pooling, while removing those labeled as blanks, thus having a character-like representation $`\mathbf{a}^{\text{char}} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{n^{\text{char}} \times d}`$, where $`n^{\text{char}} \leq n`$. Similarly, $`\mathbf{p}`$ is compressed to $`\mathbf{p}^{\text{char}} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{n^{\text{char}} \times {|\mathcal{V}|}}`$.

$`\mathbf{π}`$$`= {\text{argmax}{(\mathbf{p})}}`$$`\mathbf{p}^{\text{char}}`$$`= {\text{char-compress}{(\left. \mathbf{p} \middle| {\mathbf{π}} \right.)}}`$$`\mathbf{a}^{\text{char}}`$$`= {\text{char-compress}{(\left. \mathbf{a} \middle| {\mathbf{π}} \right.)}}`$

Subword Compression. The character representation $`\mathbf{a}^{\text{char}}`$ is split into chunks $`\mathbf{c}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{c}^{(n^{\text{sw}})}`$ according to the positions labeled as separators, where $`n^{\text{sw}}`$ is the number of predicted separators, $`\mathbf{c}^{(i)} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{k_{i} \times d}`$ is the $`i -`$th chunk representation (with $`k_{i}`$ number of characters), and $`n^{\text{char}} = {\sum_{i = 0}^{n^{\text{sw}}}k_{i}}`$. Since the CTC is predicting the tokenization of the text branch through the proposed target labels, each chunk is combining information that resemble subword tokens from $`\mathcal{V}`$. Each chunk of character representations is processed independently with $`{{\mathbf{S}\mathbf{u}\mathbf{b}\mathbf{w}\mathbf{o}\mathbf{r}\mathbf{d}}\text{-}{\mathbf{E}\mathbf{n}\mathbf{c}}}:{{\mathbb{R}}^{k_{i} \times d}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}`$, which is Transformer encoder that prepends a trainable \<cls\> token to each chunk, which is then used a subword representation of the whole chunk Devlin et al. ([2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib16)). Then, by concatenation we obtain a subword-like compressed representation $`\mathbf{a}^{\text{sw}} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{n^{\text{sw}} \times d}`$, where $`n^{\text{sw}} \leq n^{\text{char}} \leq n`$ (Fig. [3](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3.2.4 Compression Adapter ‣ 3.2 Model Architecture ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Pushing the Limits of Zero-shot End-to-End Speech Translation")).

$`\mathbf{c}^{(1)}`$$`,\ldots,\mathbf{c}^{(n^{\text{sw}})} = \text{split}{(\mathbf{a}^{\text{char}}|{\mathbf{π}}^{\text{char}})}`$$`\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\text{sw}}`$$`= {{\mathbf{S}\mathbf{u}\mathbf{b}\mathbf{w}\mathbf{o}\mathbf{r}\mathbf{d}}\text{-}{\mathbf{E}\mathbf{n}\mathbf{c}}\left( \mathbf{c}^{(i)} \right)}`$

#### 3.2.5 Speech Embedder

We concatenate to the compressed acoustic representation two vectors $`{\mathbf{\epsilon}^{\text{<lang>}},\mathbf{\epsilon}^{\text{</s>}}} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{d}`$ that function as the source language and end of sentence embeddings. Similar to the text branch, we scale the representation by $`\sqrt{d}`$ and add sinusoidal positional encodings with $`{\mathbf{P}\mathbf{o}\mathbf{s}}{( \cdot )}`$ (Eq. [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#S3.E1 "In 3.2.1 Text Encoder ‣ 3.2 Model Architecture ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Pushing the Limits of Zero-shot End-to-End Speech Translation")), thus obtaining a speech embedding $`\mathbf{e}^{\text{s}} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{n' \times d}`$, where $`n' = {n^{\text{sw}} + 2}`$. The special embeddings are initialized from the text embedding (Eq. [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#S3.E1 "In 3.2.1 Text Encoder ‣ 3.2 Model Architecture ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Pushing the Limits of Zero-shot End-to-End Speech Translation")), and remain frozen.

#### 3.2.6 Semantic Encoder

The speech embedding $`\mathbf{e}^{\text{s}}`$ is passed through a Transformer encoder $`\mathbf{T}\text{-}{\mathbf{E}\mathbf{n}\mathbf{c}}`$, which is frozen and shared with the text branch (Eq. [2](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#S3.E2 "In 3.2.1 Text Encoder ‣ 3.2 Model Architecture ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Pushing the Limits of Zero-shot End-to-End Speech Translation")), to obtain a semantic speech representation $`\mathbf{h}_{L}^{\text{s}} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{n' \times d}`$.

$`\mathbf{e}^{\text{s}}`$$`= {{\sqrt{d}{\lbrack\mathbf{\epsilon}^{\text{<lang>}},\mathbf{a}^{\text{sw}},\mathbf{\epsilon}^{\text{<s>}}\rbrack}} + {{\mathbf{P}\mathbf{o}\mathbf{s}}{(n')}}}`$$`\mathbf{h}_{L}^{\text{s}}`$$`= {\mathbf{T}\text{-}{\mathbf{E}\mathbf{n}\mathbf{c}}{(\mathbf{e}^{\text{s}})}}`$

### 3.3 Optimal Transport

To align a speech representation $`\mathbf{h}^{\text{s}} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{n' \times d}`$ to the text representation $`\mathbf{h}^{\text{x}} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{m \times d}`$, we are minimizing their Wasserstein loss Frogner et al. ([2015](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib23)) using Optimal Transport (OT) Peyré and Cuturi ([2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib53)), as in Le et al. ([2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib40)); Tsiamas et al. ([2023b](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib66)).

We assume two uniform probability distributions $`\mathbf{\phi}^{\text{s}},\mathbf{\phi}^{\text{x}}`$, with $`\mathbf{\phi}_{i}^{\text{s}} = {1/n'}`$ and $`\mathbf{\phi}_{j}^{\text{x}} = {1/m}`$, that define the mass of each position in the speech and text representations. Using a squared euclidean cost function we obtain a pairwise cost matrix $`\mathbf{C} \in {\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{n' \times m}`$, where $`\mathbf{C}_{ij} = {\|{\mathbf{h}_{i}^{\text{s}} - \mathbf{h}_{j}^{\text{x}}}\|}_{2}^{2}`$. Then, the Wasserstein distance $`W_{\delta} \in {\mathbb{R}}_{+}`$ is defined as the minimum transportation cost over all possible transportation plans $`\mathbf{Z} \in {\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{n' \times m}`$ of the total masses $`\mathbf{\phi}^{\text{s}},\mathbf{\phi}^{\text{x}}`$. The optimization is thus subjected to constraints $`{\sum_{j = 1}^{m}\mathbf{Z}_{:,j}} = {1/n'}`$ and $`{\sum_{i = 1}^{n'}\mathbf{Z}_{i,:}} = {1/m}`$.

``` math
W_{\delta} = {\underset{\mathbf{Z}}{\text{min}}{\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{n'}{\sum\limits_{j = 1}^{m}{\mathbf{Z}_{ij}\mathbf{C}_{ij}}}}}
```

In practice, to make the Wasserstein distance differentiable and efficient to compute using the Sinkhorn algorithm Knopp and Sinkhorn ([1967](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib37)), we use its entropy-regularized upper-bound estimation. Furthermore, since the Wasserstein distance is permutation invariant, we follow Le et al. ([2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib40)) and incorporate two positional regularization vectors $`\mathbf{v}^{\text{s}} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{n'}`$ and $`\mathbf{v}^{\text{x}} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{m}`$ in $`\mathbf{h}^{\text{s}}`$ and $`\mathbf{h}^{\text{x}}`$ accordingly, thus penalizing the transportation of mass between two distant positions $`i`$ and $`j`$.

$`{\overline{\mathbf{h}}}^{\text{s}}`$$`= {{\lbrack\mathbf{h}^{\text{s}};{\mu\mathbf{v}^{\text{s}}}\rbrack}{\text{a}\text{, where~}\text{a}}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\text{s}}} = \frac{i - 1}{n' - 1}`$$`{\overline{\mathbf{h}}}^{\text{x}}`$$`= {{\lbrack\mathbf{h}^{\text{x}};{\mu\mathbf{v}^{\text{x}}}\rbrack}{\text{a}\text{, where~}\text{a}}\mathbf{v}_{j}^{\text{x}}} = \frac{j - 1}{m - 1}`$

And thus for the positional regularized cost matrix $`\overline{\mathbf{C}}`$, the upper-bound Wasserstein distance that we use as a loss function is defined as:

``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\text{WASS}} = {\underset{\mathbf{Z}}{\text{min}}\left( {{\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{n'}{\sum\limits_{j = 1}^{m}{\mathbf{Z}_{ij}{\overline{\mathbf{C}}}_{ij}}}} - {\lambda\text{H}{(\mathbf{Z})}}} \right)}
```

Where $`\lambda > 0`$ is a hyperparameter, and $`\text{H}{( \cdot )}`$ denotes the von Neumann entropy of a matrix.

### 3.4 Final Loss

We apply a Wasserstein loss (Eq. [16](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#S3.E16 "In 3.3 Optimal Transport ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Pushing the Limits of Zero-shot End-to-End Speech Translation")), not only at the final layer but also at intermediate ones, since they also carry important information, and can thus help producing more robust speech representations. Thus, the final loss is defined as:

$`\mathcal{L}`$$`= {{\sum\limits_{l \in \mathcal{I}}{\frac{\alpha}{|\mathcal{I}|}\mathcal{L}_{\text{WASS}}^{(l)}}} + {{({1 - \alpha})}\mathcal{L}_{\text{CTC}}}}`$

Where $`\mathcal{I}`$ is the set of shared encoder layers for which we apply Wasserstein losses, and $`\alpha > 0`$ is a hyperparameter to balance the relative importance between the Wasserstein and CTC losses.

### 3.5 Zero-shot Speech Translation Inference

To enable zero-shot ST inference, we simply replace the MT embedding layer with the trained speech encoder. (ZeroSwot, Fig. [2](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Pushing the Limits of Zero-shot End-to-End Speech Translation")c).

## 4 Experimental Setup

Data. For training we are using Common Voice Ardila et al. ([2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib4)), the speech-transcription pairs of MuST-C v1.0 Cattoni et al. ([2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib12)), and LibriSpeech Panayotov et al. ([2015](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib51)). We evaluate our models on three multilingual ST benchmarks: MuST-C v1.0 (En $`\rightarrow`$ 8 lang.), CoVoST 2 Wang et al. ([2020b](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib69)) (En $`\rightarrow`$ 15 lang.), and FLEURS Conneau et al. ([2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib15)) (En $`\rightarrow`$ 88 lang.) (Appendix [A](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#A1 "Appendix A Data Processing and Filtering ‣ Pushing the Limits of Zero-shot End-to-End Speech Translation")).

Model Architecture. The acoustic encoder follows wav2vec 2.0 Large Baevski et al. ([2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib6)) with $`N = 24`$ layers and dimensionality $`d = 1024`$ (315M parameters). The CTC module uses a vocabulary with size $`{|\mathcal{V}|} = 30`$, and together with the acoustic encoder are initialized from a CTC-finetuned version of wav2vec 2.0 on LibriSpeech Panayotov et al. ([2015](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib51)). The subword encoder has 3 layers with dimensionality of 1024 (35M). The text encoder has either $`L = 12`$ or $`L = 24`$, both with dimensionality 1024, and an embedding size of $`{|\mathcal{B}|} = {256k}`$. We initialize its parameters from two dense versions of NLLB NLLB et al. ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib48)), the 600M (Medium) or the 1.3B (Large). Based on the size of NLLB, the resulting ZeroSwot models used for inference have 0.95B (Medium) or 1.65B (Large) parameters, while both of them the have the same number of training parameters (350M). We also train a Small version based on wav2vec 2.0 Base and NLLB-Medium (Appendix [C.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#A3.SS1 "C.1 Model Architecture ‣ Appendix C ZeroSwot Models ‣ Pushing the Limits of Zero-shot End-to-End Speech Translation")).

Training details. We use AdamW Loshchilov and Hutter ([2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib44)) with a learning rate of 3e-4, inverse square root scheduler with warmup, and batch size of 32M tokens. We apply dropout of 0.1 as well as time and channel masking Baevski et al. ([2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib6)). Speech input is 16kHz waveforms, and text is tokenized with sentencepiece Kudo and Richardson ([2018](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib38)), using the NLLB model (Appendix [C.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#A3.SS2 "C.2 Training details ‣ Appendix C ZeroSwot Models ‣ Pushing the Limits of Zero-shot End-to-End Speech Translation")).

Loss. We set $`\alpha = 0.9`$ and apply Wasserstein losses for $`\mathcal{I} = {\{ 6,7,8,9,10,11,12\}}`$ (Medium) and $`\mathcal{I} = {\{ 12,14,16,18,20,22,24\}}`$ (Large) (Eq. [17](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#S3.E17 "In 3.4 Final Loss ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Pushing the Limits of Zero-shot End-to-End Speech Translation")). Positional and entropy regularization are set to $`\mu = 10`$ (Eq. [14](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#S3.E14 "In 3.3 Optimal Transport ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Pushing the Limits of Zero-shot End-to-End Speech Translation"), [15](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#S3.E15 "In 3.3 Optimal Transport ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Pushing the Limits of Zero-shot End-to-End Speech Translation")), and $`\lambda = 1`$ (Eq. [16](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#S3.E16 "In 3.3 Optimal Transport ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Pushing the Limits of Zero-shot End-to-End Speech Translation")).

Evaluation. We apply checkpoint averaging and use a beam search of size 5. We evaluate with case-sensitive detokenized BLEU Papineni et al. ([2002](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib52)) using sacreBLEU Post ([2018](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib55)) (Appendix [B](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#A2 "Appendix B Evaluation ‣ Pushing the Limits of Zero-shot End-to-End Speech Translation")).

Does our compression reduce the length gap? We replace our proposed compression adapter (§[3.2.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#S3.SS2.SSS4 "3.2.4 Compression Adapter ‣ 3.2 Model Architecture ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Pushing the Limits of Zero-shot End-to-End Speech Translation")), with either a length adaptor Li et al. We also consider different types of tokenization than the proposed "Subword+Unk" (Table [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3.2.3 Connectionist Temporal Classification ‣ 3.2 Model Architecture ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Pushing the Limits of Zero-shot End-to-End Speech Translation")), which affect the placement of the separator tokens in the CTC target labels, and thus which representations are combined by the compression adapter. Apart from translation quality in MuST-C, we measure the length gap between the speech and text representations at the output of the shared encoder, in terms of absolute differences $`{|{\mathbf{\Delta}\text{len}}|} = {|{{\text{len}{({speech})}} - {\text{len}{({text})}}}|}`$, and relative ratios $`{r{(\text{len})}} = {{{\text{len}{({speech})}}/\text{len}}{({text})}}`$. Our findings in Table [7](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#S5.T7 "Table 7 ‣ 5 Results ‣ Pushing the Limits of Zero-shot End-to-End Speech Translation") show that our proposed method (final row) surpasses previous methods (rows 1-3), by 0.6-0.7 points, and closes the absolute length gap to an average of 1.4 tokens. We also find that without learning the text branch tokenization (rows 4-5), our compression falls behind previous methods. The length ratio of each method reveals that *over-compression*, i.e. $`{r{(\text{len})}} < 1`$, is what worsens performance, leading to information loss, as even the model without compression ($`{r{(\text{len})}} \approx 11`$) achieves scores higher than our method without proper tokenization with respect to the text branch.

## 6 Conclusions

We presented ZeroSwot, a novel method for zero-shot end-to-end Speech Translation, that works by adapting a speech encoder to the representation space of a massively multilingual MT model. Our method sets new SOTA in MuST-C, while it also surpasses larger, supervised models in CoVoST. We additionally showed that ZeroSwot outperforms cascades, both in performance and efficiency. Finally, we provided evidence of closing the modality gap, with respect to both length and representation. Future research will expand on low-resource scenarios and speech-to-speech translation.

## Limitations

Our method obtains state-of-the-art results in Speech Translation, despite being a zero-shot model. Still there are a couple of limitations inherent to the model, and on its evaluation. Although ZeroSwot is an end-to-end model, in the sense that there is not intermediate transcription step and solves error-propagation, it suffers from some of the caveats of cascade systems. Since the speech encoder "mimics" the representation space of an MT model, no acoustic information is retained. This means that the resulting translation model can’t use acoustic information, like prosody, and can thus be sometimes limited in each ability to carry out correct translations. Furthermore, given the requirement of ASR data to train the speech encoder, this methodology can’t be used for spoken-only languages, at least in its current form.

Finally, in our evaluations we did not test for a different language, other than English, in the source. This was partly due to space constrains in the paper. Although we hypothesize that our proposed method can handle equally well other source languages, it would be interesting to be applied for low-resource scenarios, which we leave as future research.

For the model without compression we add two large MLPs with residual connections at the output of the acoustic encoder. The MLPs consist of up-projection to 8192, GELU activation, and down-projection back to 1024. For the model with the Length Adaptor Li et al. ([2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib41)), we use 2 strided convolutional layers that sub-sample the output of the acoustic encoder by a factor of 4, followed by a transformer layer. For the model using CTC-based compression Liu et al. ([2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib43)); Gaido et al. ([2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib24)) we first mean-pool consecutive same-labeled representations as in §[3.2.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#S3.SS2.SSS4 "3.2.4 Compression Adapter ‣ 3.2 Model Architecture ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Pushing the Limits of Zero-shot End-to-End Speech Translation"), followed by 3 transformer layers. For the models using the proposed compression adapter but different tokenizations, we just change the CTC target labels, and everything else remains the same. ### C.4 Supervised ST finetuning Here we provide details for the ST-finetuned versions of ZeroSwot from Table [10](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#S5.T10 "Table 10 ‣ 5 Results ‣ Pushing the Limits of Zero-shot End-to-End Speech Translation"). We train for 20k steps with a learning rate of 4e-5, linear warmup for 1k steps, fixed for another 4k steps, and reduced with cosine annealing for the rest. For Medium models we finetune the parameters of the compression adapter, text encoder and text decoder, and thus keep frozen the acoustic encoder, speech embedder, and text embedding. For the Large models, we only finetune the compression adapter and apply LNA to the text encoder-decoder Li et al. ([2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib41)). We hypothesize that better results could be obtain with more sophisticated finetuning approaches Hu et al. ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib33)). For inference we average the 10 best checkpoints according to BLEU on MuST-C En-De dev. ## Appendix D Machine Translation Models Model Architecture. For the MT models used in this research we used NLLB-600M (Medium) and NLLB-1.3B (Large), which are dense transformers distilled from a 54B parameter Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model NLLB et al. ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib48)). NLLB supports many-to-many translation between 202 languages. The Medium model has 12 layers in both encoder and decoder, model dimensionality of 1024, feed-forward dimension of 4096, 16 attention heads, ReLU activations, and using pre-layernorm Xiong et al. ([2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib71)). The only difference of the Large model is that has 24 layers in both encoder and decoder, and a feed-forward dimension of 8192. Both use a multilingual shared vocabulary of 256k tokens learned with sentencepiece Kudo and Richardson ([2018](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib38)). Finetuning hyperparameters. In order to adapt them to the domain and languages of MuST-C and CoVoST 2, we did multilingual finetuning of NLLB using their transcription-translation pairs. We used batch size of 32k tokens for the Medium, and 64k for Large. We used the AdamW optimizer Loshchilov and Hutter ([2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib44)) with 2k warm-up updates and an inverse square root scheduler. We also use a dropout of 0.1 for the attention, 0.0 for the activations and a label smoothing of 0.1 for the cross entropy loss, as was done for training the original versions NLLB et al. ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib48)). We evaluate every 500 steps on the dev sets, and early stop if the loss has not improved for 20 consecutive evaluations. At the end of the training we average the best 10 checkpoints according to the dev sets loss. Models were trained with fp16, while to train the Large version on an NVIDIA 3090, we used memory-efficient-fp16 Ott et al. ([2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10422v2#bib.bib49)).
