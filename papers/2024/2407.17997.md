# Introduction

Current literature shows the capability of synthetic data to complement real data and thus improve <span acronym-label="ASR" acronym-form="singular+short">ASR</span> training through various ways and techniques . Commonly end-to-end architectures are trained with a combination of real and synthetic data, where especially models like the <span acronym-label="AED" acronym-form="singular+short">AED</span> benefit from the additional synthetic data . However, we still lack a good understanding of how well synthetic data is able to replace real data. To this end, we suggest to use synthetic training data *only* to analyze and compare its <span acronym-label="ASR" acronym-form="singular+short">ASR</span> training utility against real data. Such a study helps to gain further insight on the discrepancy between synthetic and real data. Recent work has presented large TTS systems trained on much more data than typically available for academically defined tasks . But even with such an industrial scale system it was not possible to create synthetic data that is equally utilizable to real data. In this work we explore the corresponding performance gap for three <span acronym-label="ASR" acronym-form="singular+short">ASR</span> architectures: A classic <span acronym-label="HMM" acronym-form="singular+short">HMM</span>, a <span acronym-label="Hybrid" acronym-form="singular+short">Hybrid</span> and an <span acronym-label="AED" acronym-form="singular+short">AED</span> system. A lot of previous work on synthetic data analysis was done on data generated by autoregressive <span acronym-label="TTS" acronym-form="singular+short">TTS</span> models, like Tacotron-2 , with the exception of . In this work we focus on a simple non-autoregressive TTS model which is similar to FastSpeech, but with a BLSTM-decoder, and an encoder with mixed convolutions and BLSTM. There are more recent architectures which exhibit strong performance on standard TTS tasks. However, we consider a more simple and established TTS architecture that we know from previous experiments to work on ASR-specific training data and thus is expected to be better suited for our analysis. The contributions of this work are as follows:

- Investigating how robust traditional ASR approaches such as <span acronym-label="HMM" acronym-form="singular+short">HMM</span> react to synthetic data compared to a more modern <span acronym-label="Hybrid" acronym-form="singular+short">Hybrid</span> or AED system.

- Quantifying the impact of low-quality Griffin & Lim vocoding for the usability of audio data.

- Showing how simply increasing the number of TTS model parameters already improves the usability of the synthetic data for ASR.

- Showing that in the context of this work, more sophisticated speaker embedding systems greatly influence the performance.

Especially, the effect of hyper-parameter tuning rarely is covered in TTS-literature due to the expensive MOS evaluations this would ensue. A large scale comparison of training various ASR architectures with both real and synthetic data has been done in . We extend and through analyzing the effect of ASR performance by using synthetic data *only* for ASR training. Our work and software is publicly accessible and will remain as such [^1].

# Speech Synthesis

As <span acronym-label="TTS" acronym-form="singular+short">TTS</span> system we use the <span acronym-label="NAR" acronym-form="singular+short">NAR</span> model from , which is closely related to , extended with Gaussian Upsampling . It consists of a phoneme encoder, duration predictor and a feature decoder, which follow exactly the design as in . For both duration prediction and decoding we pass a speaker embedding to enable multi speaker capabilities. In the simplest case this is generated by look-up table on the speaker ID which is learned during training, but we also investigate different more elaborate approaches, by generating fixed speaker embeddings through stronger pre-trained models. Training is done on the reference durations, extracted from a given alignment. As spectrogram targets we use globally normalized 80-dimensional log-mel features with frame shift 12.5 ms and window size of 50 ms. The spectrogram predictions are transformed into 512-dimensional linear features for <span acronym-label="GL" acronym-form="singular+short">GL</span> vocoding via a pre-trained <span acronym-label="LSTM" acronym-form="singular+short">LSTM</span> network. As validated in the baseline experiments of this paper, simple <span acronym-label="GL" acronym-form="singular+short">GL</span> vocoding does not reduce the quality of the generated audio for <span acronym-label="ASR" acronym-form="singular+short">ASR</span> training. Counting both neural models, our architecture consists of 63M parameters. The phoneme set consists of ARPA-BET phoneme symbols without stress marker. We mark word boundaries and possible silence with a `[space]` token between the last phoneme of a word and the first of the next respectively.

# Speech Recognition

Our **<span acronym-label="HMM" acronym-form="singular+short">HMM</span>** model is implemented in RASR , which is functionally close to the <span acronym-label="MFA" acronym-form="singular+short">MFA</span> commonly used for <span acronym-label="TTS" acronym-form="singular+short">TTS</span> works such as FastSpeech-2 . Training parameters are optimized on LibriSpeech-100h with focus on <span acronym-label="ASR" acronym-form="singular+short">ASR</span> performance. The overall training consists of the several steps, which in more detail are explained in . In this work we use the <span acronym-label="HMM" acronym-form="singular+short">HMM</span> twofold. First we use the alignments produced by the system as ground truth alignments for duration prediction in our <span acronym-label="TTS" acronym-form="singular+short">TTS</span>. For this we calculate the Viterbi path for our best alignment, setting a duration of zero for `[space]` tokens where no silence was aligned. For recognition, we use the model together with a pre-trained 4-gram count-based <span acronym-label="LM" acronym-form="singular+short">LM</span> from the LibriSpeech dataset.

On top of the <span acronym-label="HMM" acronym-form="singular+short">HMM</span> we use a **<span acronym-label="Hybrid" acronym-form="singular+short">Hybrid</span>** model which predicts the frame-label posterior probability by a neural network. We use the final alignment output from the <span acronym-label="ASR" acronym-form="singular+short">ASR</span> <span acronym-label="HMM" acronym-form="singular+short">HMM</span> as training targets. The <span acronym-label="NN" acronym-form="singular+short">NN</span> consists of a stack of 8 1024-dimensional <span acronym-label="LSTM" acronym-form="singular+short">LSTM</span> layers followed by a linear layer with softmax activation and output size 12001 to match the corresponding CART labels. In total the model consists of 210M parameters. For recognition we again use the LibriSpeech 4-gram <span acronym-label="LM" acronym-form="singular+short">LM</span>.

Our last model is an **<span acronym-label="AED" acronym-form="singular+short">AED</span>** model as used in with 12 conformer blocks as encoder and single layer LSTM for the decoder, resulting in 98M parameters. We use BPE labels with 2k merge operations as the output units. Different from the <span acronym-label="TTS" acronym-form="singular+short">TTS</span> model we use a frame shift to 10 ms and the window size to 25 ms for the feature extraction. The models uses a downsampling factor of 6. To improve training stability we increase the number of encoder layers over time, starting with 2 layers which are increased by 2 every 5 sub-epochs beginning the first increase after 10 sub-epochs reaching full model size at 10 full epochs (30 sub-epochs). For data augmentation we use SpecAgument and apply speed-pertubation via librosa.resample()[^2] uniformly distributing scales 0.9/1.0/1.1 among the input. Recognition results are without the use of an external language model.

# Pipeline

In general our pipeline consists of 5 steps analogue to , which is visualized <a href="#fig:pipeline" data-reference-type="ref+Label" data-reference="fig:pipeline">1</a>. First the aligner is trained on the pre-processed data and a forced alignment generated as duration reference for the <span acronym-label="TTS" acronym-form="singular+short">TTS</span> model. With that the <span acronym-label="TTS" acronym-form="singular+short">TTS</span> is trained and used for generation of synthetic data. Afterwards the <span acronym-label="ASR" acronym-form="singular+short">ASR</span> models are trained on the generated data for a final evaluation. We remove unnatural long silence portions by using the silence filter from FFmpeg[^3] with a threshold of -50dB. Synthesis is done on two portions of data, the TTS training data and a similar amount of unseen text data. Synthesizing the data seen during <span acronym-label="TTS" acronym-form="singular+short">TTS</span> allows our analysis to be done with as little <span acronym-label="TTS" acronym-form="singular+short">TTS</span> errors as possible, while using unseen data helps verifying the validity of our results. In the baseline case we randomize the speaker ID, using speaker IDs of train-clean-100. The synthesized data is then used to train the different <span acronym-label="ASR" acronym-form="singular+short">ASR</span> systems **without** any additional real data. Similar to , we additionally let the <span acronym-label="TTS" acronym-form="singular+short">TTS</span> model synthesize the data with access to the target durations which were seen during training. We denote this in the tables by marking the durations as *real* durations.

# Experiments

## Data and Training

<span id="tab:maes" label="tab:maes"></span>

For all our experiments we use the the train-clean-100 subset of LibriSpeech as supervised training data. While common literature usually synthesizes an unseen part of train-clean-360 , we also conduct experiments on the previously seen training data. Comparing results to synthesizing unseen data, we can observe generalization effects of the TTS. As <span acronym-label="CV" acronym-form="singular+short">CV</span> set for <span acronym-label="ASR" acronym-form="singular+short">ASR</span> training we use a combination of dev-clean and dev-other. For <span acronym-label="TTS" acronym-form="singular+short">TTS</span> training we construct our own <span acronym-label="CV" acronym-form="singular+short">CV</span> set where we split 4 sequences per speaker from the training data resulting in 1004 sequences. To generate phoneme representations for words not contained in the LibriSpeech lexicon we use Sequitur . For the <span acronym-label="AED" acronym-form="singular+short">AED</span> model we use byte-pair encoding with 2000 merge operations. We evaluate all of our models on *dev-clean* and *dev-other* and do not apply silence removal. The <span acronym-label="NAR" acronym-form="singular+short">NAR</span>-<span acronym-label="TTS" acronym-form="singular+short">TTS</span> is trained for 200 steps, the <span acronym-label="HMM" acronym-form="singular+short">HMM</span> for 100 EM-steps, the <span acronym-label="Hybrid" acronym-form="singular+short">Hybrid</span> for $`\sim`$<!-- -->13 full epochs and the <span acronym-label="AED" acronym-form="singular+short">AED</span> model for $`\sim`$<!-- -->165 full epochs. All experiments were done on a single consumer 11Gb GPU for easy reproducibility. As optimizer we use Adam with a learning rate decay factor of 0.9 based on the CV score.

## Model sizes and Generalization

Next-up we investigate the influence of different model sizes on both the generalization capabilities of the <span acronym-label="TTS" acronym-form="singular+short">TTS</span> model and the influence on <span acronym-label="ASR" acronym-form="singular+short">ASR</span> training. For this we chose two different model scaling approaches. TTS literature usually reports on a single set of hyperparameters, but due to the possibility of automatic evaluation through ASR training and recognition, we can conduct a study on different choices. In the first approach we scale <span acronym-label="TTS" acronym-form="singular+short">TTS</span> model dimensions by a factor $`\gamma`$, meaning that e.g. for $`\gamma = 1.5`$ the 512-dimensional layers are increased to 768. Analogue we indicate scaling the layer amount with $`\omega`$, meaning that e.g. for $`\omega = 2`$ there are twice as many <span acronym-label="LSTM" acronym-form="singular+short">LSTM</span> layers in the <span acronym-label="TTS" acronym-form="singular+short">TTS</span> models. As seen in the upper part of <a href="#tab:general" data-reference-type="ref+Label" data-reference="tab:general">[tab:general]</a> increasing the model size or the layer count for audio generation in both cases helps the <span acronym-label="ASR" acronym-form="singular+short">ASR</span> models. Here the <span acronym-label="AED" acronym-form="singular+short">AED</span> model benefits the most from the larger models, increasing relative performance on dev-clean by $`\sim`$<!-- -->30% and on dev-other by $`\sim`$<!-- -->20%. While the <span acronym-label="Hybrid" acronym-form="singular+short">Hybrid</span> model shows improvements of $`\sim`$<!-- -->15% on dev-other, the <span acronym-label="HMM" acronym-form="singular+short">HMM</span> model only improves marginally with data generated by larger <span acronym-label="TTS" acronym-form="singular+short">TTS</span> models. This also contradicts to the common idea that deeper models are able to hold even with larger models, while having a friction of the parameters. In the case of generating synthetic data for <span acronym-label="ASR" acronym-form="singular+short">ASR</span> training this paradigm seems to be not trivially realizable. We hypothesize that larger <span acronym-label="TTS" acronym-form="singular+short">TTS</span> systems are able to better replicate the acoustic structure of the training data, which then reflects in better synthesis of seen data. In the lower section of <a href="#tab:general" data-reference-type="ref+Label" data-reference="tab:general">[tab:general]</a> we show results on an unseen portion of train-clean-360. Namely, we chose transcriptions that in the original corpus relates to 100h of data, hence the name 100h-LS-360. As to be expected the general performance of the <span acronym-label="TTS" acronym-form="singular+short">TTS</span> on unseen data is worse than on the training data, still the larger <span acronym-label="TTS" acronym-form="singular+short">TTS</span> models are able to improve the performance of the <span acronym-label="ASR" acronym-form="singular+short">ASR</span> models, even though training scores indicated overfitting, as visible in <a href="#tab:maes" data-reference-type="ref+Label" data-reference="tab:maes">[tab:maes]</a>. This confirms our perception that for synthetic data generation <span acronym-label="TTS" acronym-form="singular+short">TTS</span> training loss scores are not meaningful as an indicator for generalisation and performance on a held-out dataset. A notable difference is visible in the performance of the different <span acronym-label="ASR" acronym-form="singular+short">ASR</span> models on the unseen data. The <span acronym-label="HMM" acronym-form="singular+short">HMM</span> is almost able to replicate the performance compared to the seen training data, with an absolute difference of 0.3% WER on dev-clean and dev-other for the best results. A similar result is visible for the <span acronym-label="Hybrid" acronym-form="singular+short">Hybrid</span> model, where the degradation is only 1.1% WER absolute. In the case of <span acronym-label="AED" acronym-form="singular+short">AED</span> the performance is a lot worse, where the best performance with unseen data degrades by almost 50% relative on dev-clean and $`\sim`$<!-- -->15% relative on dev-clean. This indicates that modelling errors done by the <span acronym-label="TTS" acronym-form="singular+short">TTS</span> model during synthesis of unseen data hurt the performance of the <span acronym-label="AED" acronym-form="singular+short">AED</span> a lot more than for the other two models.

## Speaker Representations

As a last study we investigate the influence of the speaker embedding on the performance of the synthetic data in <span acronym-label="ASR" acronym-form="singular+short">ASR</span> training. In order to generate more expressive speaker embeddings we train an X-Vector model on train-clean-100, as well as taking embeddings directly from the pre-trained *Resemblyzer*[^4] model . We train our TTS model by replacing the lookup table by the generated embeddings. The results of this can be found in <a href="#tab:speaker_emb" data-reference-type="ref+Label" data-reference="tab:speaker_emb">[tab:speaker_emb]</a>. For our baseline keeping the speaker tags random does not make the synthesized data worse for <span acronym-label="ASR" acronym-form="singular+short">ASR</span> training. Adding the embeddings generated by both X-Vectors and Resemblyzer does not help improve over the initial baseline and rather the performance degrades, especially in the case of AED. Only when not shuffling the embeddings during synthesis the pre-trained embeddings are able to keep up with the baseline, surpassing it together with real durations. From this we conclude that overall the speaker embeddings generated by the TTS model generalize well. Feeding embeddings from more elaborate models without changes to the model makes the TTS overfit instead of benefiting from richer embeddings. Nevertheless, in the correct setting, they can provide meaningful information to the model, as seen in the results with real durations.

# Conclusions

In this work we used a text-to-speech (TTS) model for the generation of synthetic data for automatic speech recognition (ASR) training. We modified the <span acronym-label="TTS" acronym-form="singular+short">TTS</span> system in different aspects and investigated how this impacts the ASR training on the synthetically generated data. Increasing the size of the <span acronym-label="TTS" acronym-form="singular+short">TTS</span> led to more overfitting according to the training and validation scores. Still, when using the enlarged TTS for synthetic data generation, the <span acronym-label="ASR" acronym-form="singular+short">ASR</span> performance would improve. This means hyperparameter tuning for <span acronym-label="TTS" acronym-form="singular+short">TTS</span> and proper evaluation is required before drawing conclusions from <span acronym-label="ASR" acronym-form="singular+short">ASR</span> training procedures involving synthetic data. Basing model selection solely on loss scores does not suffice. In a second set of experiments, we increased the <span acronym-label="TTS" acronym-form="singular+short">TTS</span> complexity by adding pre-trained networks for speaker modeling. In contrast to enlarging the model, the results were less conclusive. Only in some of the experimental settings the <span acronym-label="ASR" acronym-form="singular+short">ASR</span> performance would improve, and the improvements were not consistent among the different <span acronym-label="ASR" acronym-form="singular+short">ASR</span> architectures used. It seems that the TTS tends to overfit on the given embeddings, which is reflected in the performance increase when using real durations and using the same speaker embedding as seen during training for synthesis. We made the additional observation, that the vocoding of log-mel-features using a low-quality method such as Griffin & Lim does not strongly degrade the utilization of the audio data for <span acronym-label="ASR" acronym-form="singular+short">ASR</span> training, while reducing the overhead for data generation significantly. Overall we have seen that gap between real and synthetic data is smaller for traditional <span acronym-label="ASR" acronym-form="singular+short">ASR</span> systems. Real phoneme variations and stronger speaker embeddings affected these systems much less than an attention-encoder-decoder <span acronym-label="ASR" acronym-form="singular+short">ASR</span> systems. Future work should aim to find suitable aspects in synthetic data which correlate with the ASR performance across different model conditions.
