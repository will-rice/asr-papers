###### Abstract

\Ac

ASR research has achieved impressive performance in recent years and has significant potential for enabling access for people with dysarthria (PwD) in augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) and home environment systems. However, progress in dysarthric ASR (DASR) has been limited by high variability in dysarthric speech and limited public availability of dysarthric training data. This paper demonstrates that data augmentation using text-to-dysarthic-speech (TTDS) synthesis for finetuning large automatic speech recognition (ASR) models is effective for DASR. Specifically, diffusion-based text-to-speech (TTS) models can produce speech samples similar to dysarthric speech that can be used as additional training data for fine-tuning ASR foundation models, in this case Whisper. Results show improved synthesis metrics and ASR performance for the proposed multi-speaker diffusion-based TTDS data augmentation for ASR fine-tuning compared to current DASR baselines.

###### keywords:

Dysarthric speech recognition, diffusion, text-to-speech synthesis, data augmentation

## 1 Introduction

Dysarthria is a type of motor speech disorder (MSD) that reflects abnormalities in motor movements required for speech production \[[1](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib1)\]. The psychosocial impact \[[2](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib2)\] and restrictions on functioning and participation \[[3](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib3)\] for PwD are well documented \[[4](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib4), [5](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib5)\]. \AcDASR has important implications for AAC devices and home environmental control systems \[[6](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib6), [7](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib7)\]. Although the accuracy of ASR systems for typical speech has improved significantly \[[8](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib8), [9](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib9)\], there are challenges inherent with DASR. Due to high inter- and intra-speaker variability in dysarthric speech and limited public availability of dysarthric data, generic ASR models usually do not generalise well to dysarthric speakers \[[10](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib10), [11](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib11)\]. Baseline ASR models \[[12](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib12)\], typically trained on larger amounts of typical speech data, can be adapted to domains with limited data availability, such as dysarthric speech recordings \[[13](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib13)\]. Model adaptation approaches show improved performance \[[14](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib14), [15](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib15)\] and deep learning in combination with data augmentation techniques to address data sparsity \[[16](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib16)\] have achieved state-of-the-art (SotA) performance for DASR.

Transformer-based models have not been adequately explored for dysarthric speech as such architectures require a significant amount of training data that is not publicly available. \[[16](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib16)\] implements a spatial convolutional neural network (CNN) with multi-head Transformers (pre-trained on control speaker data) to recognise visual representations of whole-word dysarthric speech, and \[[17](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib17)\] use dysarthric and typical speech corpora with data augmentation techniques to implement two-step parameter adjustments to train a dysarthric Transformer model. A recurrent neural network (RNN)-Transducer model has been trained on the Euphonia dataset \[[18](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib18)\], which contains over $`1400`$ hours of audio data, however, the dataset is not publicly available. Representations from foundation ASR models have been used as input features for DASR systems (e.g. Wav2Vec2.0 \[[19](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib19)\] and WavLM \[[20](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib20)\]), but foundation models have not yet been adapted for DASR.

Data augmentation techniques have been widely studied for typical speech tasks \[[21](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib21)\], but data augmentation for DASR requires further research \[[22](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib22)\]. Spectro-temporal differences between typical and dysarthric speech (e.g. speaking rate) have influenced approaches, such as vocal tract length perturbation (VTLP) \[[23](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib23)\], tempo-stretching \[[24](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib24)\] and speed perturbation \[[25](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib25)\]. Although slower speaking rates and modifications to the spectral envelope can be modelled, perceptual dysarthric speech characteristics (e.g. articulatory imprecision or voice quality \[[26](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib26)\]) are not captured. Subsequently, generative adverserial networks have been applied to speed-perturbed typical speech for speech synthesis \[[22](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib22)\] and voice conversion (VC) \[[27](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib27)\]. Also, Transformer-based systems have been implemented for TTDS synthesis \[[28](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib28), [29](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib29)\]. Recently, diffusion probabilistic modelling (DPM) has been applied to a VC task for dysarthric data augmentation \[[30](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib30)\]. The results demonstrate improved word error rate (WER) performance of DASR systems using augmented data, and subjective evaluations by human expert listeners show that severity characteristics of dysarthric speech are captured in the synthesis. This paper proposes (i) to create DASR training data by DPM, training Grad-TTS \[[31](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib31)\] from scratch on dysarthric data to (ii) analyse the use of additional augmented data only to finetune large ASR models (here Whisper), i.e. without matched control speaker data. The remainder of the paper is structured as follows: [Section 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#S2 "2 Methodology ‣ Training Data Augmentation for Dysarthric Automatic Speech Recognition by Text-to-Dysarthric-Speech SynthesisThis work was supported by the Centre for Doctoral Training in Speech and Language Technologies (SLT) and their Applications funded by UK Research and Innovation [grant number EP/S023062/1].") describes the TTDS system and the ASR model adaptation. Experiments are described in [Section 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#S3 "3 Experimental Setup ‣ Training Data Augmentation for Dysarthric Automatic Speech Recognition by Text-to-Dysarthric-Speech SynthesisThis work was supported by the Centre for Doctoral Training in Speech and Language Technologies (SLT) and their Applications funded by UK Research and Innovation [grant number EP/S023062/1].") and their results are presented in Section [4](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#S4 "4 Results ‣ Training Data Augmentation for Dysarthric Automatic Speech Recognition by Text-to-Dysarthric-Speech SynthesisThis work was supported by the Centre for Doctoral Training in Speech and Language Technologies (SLT) and their Applications funded by UK Research and Innovation [grant number EP/S023062/1]."). [Section 5](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#S5 "5 Conclusion ‣ Training Data Augmentation for Dysarthric Automatic Speech Recognition by Text-to-Dysarthric-Speech SynthesisThis work was supported by the Centre for Doctoral Training in Speech and Language Technologies (SLT) and their Applications funded by UK Research and Innovation [grant number EP/S023062/1].") concludes the paper.

## 2 Methodology

### 2.1 Dysarthric Speech Synthesis

To synthesise dysarthric mel-spectrogram data $`\mathbf{X}`$ for DASR augmentation, we train the Grad-TTS \[[31](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib31)\] model.¹¹1Grad-TTS code adapted from [https://github.com/huawei-noah/Speech-Backbones](https://github.com/huawei-noah/Speech-Backbones). Implementation available at [https://github.com/WingZLeung/TTDS](https://github.com/WingZLeung/TTDS).

``` math
\mathbf{X} = {\text{Grad-TTS}{(\mathbf{y},s_{E})}}
```

from scratch on dysarthric data to augment the limited available dysarthric speech data for a speaker identity $`s_{E}`$. Grad-TTS \[[31](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib31)\] produces mel-spectrograms matrices $`\mathbf{X}`$ of size $`F \times L`$, with $`F = 80`$ in this work, and $`L`$ being the variable number of frames. This starts by forming initial distributions centred on the Grad-TTS text-encoder output $`\mathbf{E}_{\theta}{(\mathbf{y})}`$ \[[31](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib31)\] with trainable parameters $`\theta`$ for a given text-sequence vector $`\mathbf{y}`$, and applying a diffusion process (cf. ([4](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#S2.E4 "In 2.1 Dysarthric Speech Synthesis ‣ 2 Methodology ‣ Training Data Augmentation for Dysarthric Automatic Speech Recognition by Text-to-Dysarthric-Speech SynthesisThis work was supported by the Centre for Doctoral Training in Speech and Language Technologies (SLT) and their Applications funded by UK Research and Innovation [grant number EP/S023062/1]."))) to denoise the initial distribution of the noisy mel-spectrogram matrix $`\mathbf{X}_{T} \sim {\mathcal{N}{({\mathbf{E}_{\theta}{(\mathbf{y})}},\mathbf{I})}}`$ into an estimate of a training-distribution sample matrix $`\mathbf{X}_{0}`$, with $`\mathbf{I}`$ being the identity matrix, and $`T = 1.0`$. The text encoder $`\mathbf{E}_{\theta}`$ has the same transformer architecture and training objective as Glow-TTS \[[32](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib32)\]. The sampling process from $`\mathbf{X}_{T}`$ to $`\mathbf{X}_{0}`$ (cf. [4](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#S2.E4 "In 2.1 Dysarthric Speech Synthesis ‣ 2 Methodology ‣ Training Data Augmentation for Dysarthric Automatic Speech Recognition by Text-to-Dysarthric-Speech SynthesisThis work was supported by the Centre for Doctoral Training in Speech and Language Technologies (SLT) and their Applications funded by UK Research and Innovation [grant number EP/S023062/1].")) is defined as the reverse of a forward diffusion process from $`\mathbf{X}_{0}`$ to $`\mathbf{X}_{T}`$ with change in $`\mathbf{X}`$ denoted as

``` math
{d\mathbf{X}} = {{\frac{1}{2}{({{\mathbf{E}_{\theta}{(\mathbf{y})}} - \mathbf{X}})}\beta{(t)}dt} + {\sqrt{\beta{(t)}}d\mathbf{W}_{t}}}
```

where $`\mathbf{W}`$ is a random process. The forward process in ([2](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#S2.E2 "In 2.1 Dysarthric Speech Synthesis ‣ 2 Methodology ‣ Training Data Augmentation for Dysarthric Automatic Speech Recognition by Text-to-Dysarthric-Speech SynthesisThis work was supported by the Centre for Doctoral Training in Speech and Language Technologies (SLT) and their Applications funded by UK Research and Innovation [grant number EP/S023062/1].")) is a continuous mean-reverting variance-preserving stochastic differential equation (SDE). The linear noise schedule

``` math
{\beta{(t)}} = {\beta_{0} + {{({\beta_{T} - \beta_{0}})}t}}
```

starts at $`\beta_{0}`$ and ends at $`\beta_{T}`$ to control the perturbation gradient \[[31](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib31)\]. Intuitively, $`\beta{(t)}`$ increases the amount of noise perturbation of $`\mathbf{X}`$ as $`t`$ increases. The amount of noise added to $`\mathbf{X}`$ at each time step $`dt`$ increases proportionally with $`t`$. The encoder term $`\mathbf{E}_{\theta}{(\mathbf{y})}`$ ensures that the distribution of $`\mathbf{X}`$ is centered on the text-encoder prediction for any $`t`$. Default values for Grad-TTS are $`\beta_{0} = 0.05`$ and $`\beta_{T} = 20`$ \[[31](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib31)\], the choice is inspired by diffusion image synthesis \[[33](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib33)\] where $`\mathbf{X}_{T} \sim {\mathcal{N}{(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})}}`$. This work explores reduced $`\beta_{T}`$ to reflect that the TTS diffusion process starts from structured data $`\mathbf{X}_{T} \sim {\mathcal{N}{({\mathbf{E}_{\theta}{(\mathbf{y})}},\mathbf{I})}}`$ rather than an unassuming Gaussian; the effect of these hyperparameters has not yet been explored for dysarthric TTS to the authors’ knowledge. The reverse diffusion process

``` math
{d\mathbf{X}} = {\frac{1}{2}{({{({{\mathbf{E}_{\theta}{(\mathbf{y})}} - \mathbf{X}})} - {{{\nabla\log}p_{t}}{(\mathbf{X})}}})}\beta{(t)}dt}
```

is defined by a probability-flow ordinary differential equation (ODE) \[[33](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib33), [31](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib31)\]. Computing the log-density gradient $`{{\nabla\log}p_{t}}{(\mathbf{X})}`$ in ([4](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#S2.E4 "In 2.1 Dysarthric Speech Synthesis ‣ 2 Methodology ‣ Training Data Augmentation for Dysarthric Automatic Speech Recognition by Text-to-Dysarthric-Speech SynthesisThis work was supported by the Centre for Doctoral Training in Speech and Language Technologies (SLT) and their Applications funded by UK Research and Innovation [grant number EP/S023062/1].")), a.k.a. the *score*, is intractable, hence a trainable estimator $`\mathbf{S}_{\theta}{(\mathbf{X},t,{\mathbf{E}_{\theta}{(\mathbf{y})}},s_{E})}`$ is necessary. $`\mathbf{S}_{\theta}`$ is a U-Net model \[[34](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib34)\] that must be trained on labelled speech data with a score-matching objective \[[33](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib33), [31](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib31)\]. When training on a dataset with multiple speakers, a speaker identity parameter $`s_{E}`$ is given to control generation such that the output is faithful to the target speaker. The probability-flow ODE ([4](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#S2.E4 "In 2.1 Dysarthric Speech Synthesis ‣ 2 Methodology ‣ Training Data Augmentation for Dysarthric Automatic Speech Recognition by Text-to-Dysarthric-Speech SynthesisThis work was supported by the Centre for Doctoral Training in Speech and Language Technologies (SLT) and their Applications funded by UK Research and Innovation [grant number EP/S023062/1].")) is solved backwards in time from $`\mathbf{X}_{T}`$ to $`\mathbf{X}_{0}`$ using the first-order Euler scheme. The HiFi-GAN vocoder \[[35](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib35)\] is then used to transform the mel-spectrograms $`\mathbf{X}`$ into audio waveforms. This pipeline produces speaker-specific augmentation data for the subsequent ASR model to capture the nuances of dysarthric speakers. Although augmentation methods exist, which finetune a pretrained Grad-TTS model for VC \[[30](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib30)\], i.e. for a speech-to-speech task, we explore training Grad-TTS from scratch, i.e. for the more flexible TTS task and without reliance on matched control speech data nor typical TTS pre-training, providing augmentation that only requires labelled dysarthric data.

### 2.2 Model Adaptation for Dysarthric ASR

The data synthesised in [Section 2.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#S2.SS1 "2.1 Dysarthric Speech Synthesis ‣ 2 Methodology ‣ Training Data Augmentation for Dysarthric Automatic Speech Recognition by Text-to-Dysarthric-Speech SynthesisThis work was supported by the Centre for Doctoral Training in Speech and Language Technologies (SLT) and their Applications funded by UK Research and Innovation [grant number EP/S023062/1].") will be used to finetune the Whisper \[[12](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib12)\] ASR multilingual models.²²2Whisper finetune code adapted from [https://github.com/vasistalodagala/whisper-finetune](https://github.com/vasistalodagala/whisper-finetune). Whisper is based on an encoder-decoder Transformer architecture with $`12`$ encoder and $`12`$ decoder layers and is a weakly supervised model trained using up to $`680`$k hours of labelled typical speech data. To date, Transformer models have not been adequately explored in dysarthric ASR due to data sparsity issues, and adaptation of large-scale pre-trained ASR foundation models have not been previously explored. The Whisper model is fine-tuned using labelled data. Parameters in the feature encoder ($`2`$ x conv), model encoder and decoder layers were not frozen. The Whisper-medium (WM) model has $`763.9`$M parameters ($`762.3`$M trainable parameters), and the Whisper-large (WL) model has $`1543.3`$M parameters ($`1541.4`$M trainable parameters). Finally, SpecAugment \[[36](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib36)\] has been shown to improve the performance of dysarthric ASR with synthesised data augmentation \[[30](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib30)\], and therefore the Whisper models were also trained with and without SpecAugment in this work. SpecAugment is directly applied to the feature inputs of the ASR model, time warping the features, and masking blocks of frequency channels & blocks of time steps. Models with SpecAugment were optimised on the probability of frequency and time masking.

## 3 Experimental Setup

The *TORGO* database \[[37](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib37)\] containing dysarthric speech is introduced in [Section 3.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#S3.SS1 "3.1 TORGO Dysarthic Speech Dataset ‣ 3 Experimental Setup ‣ Training Data Augmentation for Dysarthric Automatic Speech Recognition by Text-to-Dysarthric-Speech SynthesisThis work was supported by the Centre for Doctoral Training in Speech and Language Technologies (SLT) and their Applications funded by UK Research and Innovation [grant number EP/S023062/1]."), and the respective data splits & training methods for dysarthric speech synthesis and ASR models are described in Sections [3.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#S3.SS2 "3.2 Text-to-Speech Synthesis ‣ 3 Experimental Setup ‣ Training Data Augmentation for Dysarthric Automatic Speech Recognition by Text-to-Dysarthric-Speech SynthesisThis work was supported by the Centre for Doctoral Training in Speech and Language Technologies (SLT) and their Applications funded by UK Research and Innovation [grant number EP/S023062/1].") and [3.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#S3.SS3 "3.3 Dysarthric ASR ‣ 3 Experimental Setup ‣ Training Data Augmentation for Dysarthric Automatic Speech Recognition by Text-to-Dysarthric-Speech SynthesisThis work was supported by the Centre for Doctoral Training in Speech and Language Technologies (SLT) and their Applications funded by UK Research and Innovation [grant number EP/S023062/1]."), respectively.

### 3.1 TORGO Dysarthic Speech Dataset

The *TORGO* database³³3TORGO database: [http://www.cs.toronto.edu/~complingweb/data/TORGO/torgo.html](http://www.cs.toronto.edu/~complingweb/data/TORGO/torgo.html) contains approx. $`21`$ hours of aligned acoustic and 3D articulatory feature data \[[37](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib37)\], i.e. much less than usually used for ASR training. The data was gathered from $`8`$ American English dysarthric speakers (with a diagnosis of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), or cerebral palsy (CP)), denoted as *TORGO dysarthric*, and seven control speakers that are age-gender-matched to the dysarthric speakers, denoted as *TORGO control*. Utterances with no transcription or that were too short to contain speech were discarded \[[13](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib13)\]. All speakers had the same prompts, and therefore there is a large overlap in word and sentence prompts \[[38](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib38)\]. Since the TORGO dataset does not provide pre-defined data splits, a leave-one-speaker-out (LOSO) approach is commonly implemented for ASR \[[38](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib38)\].\
The dysarthric speakers in *TORGO* were assessed by a speech and language therapist (SLT) using the Frenchay Dysarthria Assessment (FDA) \[[39](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib39)\]. The dysarthria severity ratings of the *TORGO* dysarthric speakers are displayed in [Table 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3.1 TORGO Dysarthic Speech Dataset ‣ 3 Experimental Setup ‣ Training Data Augmentation for Dysarthric Automatic Speech Recognition by Text-to-Dysarthric-Speech SynthesisThis work was supported by the Centre for Doctoral Training in Speech and Language Technologies (SLT) and their Applications funded by UK Research and Innovation [grant number EP/S023062/1]."). ‘F’ and ‘M’ denote gender, and the numeral denotes the participant number in the dataset.

### 3.2 Text-to-Speech Synthesis

For dysarthric speech synthesis, we train the Grad-TTS models (cf. [Section 2.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#S2.SS1 "2.1 Dysarthric Speech Synthesis ‣ 2 Methodology ‣ Training Data Augmentation for Dysarthric Automatic Speech Recognition by Text-to-Dysarthric-Speech SynthesisThis work was supported by the Centre for Doctoral Training in Speech and Language Technologies (SLT) and their Applications funded by UK Research and Innovation [grant number EP/S023062/1].")) using *TORGO dysarthric* from scratch. Previous implementations of DPM TTDS pre-train the synthesis model on age-gender-matched typical speech and finetune on dysarthric speech data \[[30](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib30), [40](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib40)\]. The Grad-TTS models require training and validation data for a given speaker to train a speaker embedding $`s_{E}`$ in ([1](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#S2.E1 "In 2.1 Dysarthric Speech Synthesis ‣ 2 Methodology ‣ Training Data Augmentation for Dysarthric Automatic Speech Recognition by Text-to-Dysarthric-Speech SynthesisThis work was supported by the Centre for Doctoral Training in Speech and Language Technologies (SLT) and their Applications funded by UK Research and Innovation [grant number EP/S023062/1].")). *TORGO* does not have pre-defined data splits. Therefore, data splits were created for TTS training by pairing array and head microphones (of the same utterance) and then randomly splitting utterances into train, validation and test data splits in an $`80`$%, $`10`$%, $`10`$% ratio per speaker. A systematic approach was considered, e.g. considering the distribution of single/multi-word utterances, and distribution of utterances across splits. However, analysis in \[[38](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib38)\] shows there are $`951`$-$`969`$ unique utterances (across $`16,158`$ recordings), and not all dysarthric speakers completed recordings of all utterances. Once the *TORGO dysarthric* TTDS models are trained, the transcripts for all splits are input to the trained models to synthesise additional training data for LOSO \[[38](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib38)\] ASR model adaptation.

As Grad-TTS has not yet been adequately explored with dysarthric speech, we investigate $`\beta_{T}`$ hyperparameter values in ([3](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#S2.E3 "In 2.1 Dysarthric Speech Synthesis ‣ 2 Methodology ‣ Training Data Augmentation for Dysarthric Automatic Speech Recognition by Text-to-Dysarthric-Speech SynthesisThis work was supported by the Centre for Doctoral Training in Speech and Language Technologies (SLT) and their Applications funded by UK Research and Innovation [grant number EP/S023062/1].")). Additionally, in the interest of using as little dysarthric data as possible, we investigate three conditions:

1.  (a)
    An *all-speaker (ASp) model* is trained using the data of all dysarthric speakers (i.e. the *TORGO dysarthric* data). The ASp model is trained using the training and validation splits of the entire dataset, and used to synthesise training data for all speakers.
2.  (b)
    *\AcSSp models* are trained using a single dysarthric speaker’s data (i.e. a model is trained using one speaker’s training and validation data, and this model is used to synthesise data for the same speaker).
3.  (c)
    *\AcDSpG models*: the *TORGO* dysarthric speakers are partitioned into two groups by dysarthria severity rating on the FDA (cf. [Table 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3.1 TORGO Dysarthic Speech Dataset ‣ 3 Experimental Setup ‣ Training Data Augmentation for Dysarthric Automatic Speech Recognition by Text-to-Dysarthric-Speech SynthesisThis work was supported by the Centre for Doctoral Training in Speech and Language Technologies (SLT) and their Applications funded by UK Research and Innovation [grant number EP/S023062/1].")). Severe and mod-severe speakers (i.e. F01, M01, M02, M04, M05) form Group $`1`$ (G$`1`$), and mild and moderate speakers (i.e. F03, F04, M03) Group $`2`$ (G$`2`$). The \AcDSpG G$`1`$ model is trained on G$`1`$ speakers’ training and validation data, and used to synthesise data for these speakers.

Although control speaker data is not used to train models for dysarthric speech synthesis, ASp and single-speaker (SSp) *TORGO control* TTS models were trained in the same manner to compare evaluation metrics. Finally, Grad-TTS models are optimised on hyperparameters of learning rate, epochs and batch size.

### 3.3 Dysarthric ASR

The Whisper ASR model is finetuned on a composition of real (TORGO) data and synthetic data (created by TTDS in [Section 2.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#S2.SS1 "2.1 Dysarthric Speech Synthesis ‣ 2 Methodology ‣ Training Data Augmentation for Dysarthric Automatic Speech Recognition by Text-to-Dysarthric-Speech SynthesisThis work was supported by the Centre for Doctoral Training in Speech and Language Technologies (SLT) and their Applications funded by UK Research and Innovation [grant number EP/S023062/1].")). A cumulative ratio of additional synthetic training data for data augmentation (from $`0`$-$`100`$% in $`10`$% increments) is implemented. The *TORGO dysarthric* ASR models are trained using the LOSO methodology \[[38](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib38)\] to create speaker independent (SI) models: for a given target speaker, the data of the remaining speakers are used to train the model, which is tested on the given target speaker’s data (and therefore the target speaker’s data is not seen by the ASR model). Values for learning rate, warm-up, epochs, and batch size hyperparameters are optimised during training.

### 3.4 Evaluation metrics

To evaluate the quality of the synthesised dysarthric speech, the mean cepstral distortion (MCD) is used as an objective metric, and subjective evaluation by a human expert listener was conducted. The performance of dysarthric ASR systems are measured by WER.

#### 3.4.1 Mean Cepstral Distortion (MCD)

The MCD is defined as the Euclidian distance between the synthesised and reference mel spectra, and is computed by alignment with dynamic time warping (DTW) \[[41](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib41)\]. The MCD has been shown to have correlation to subjective test results in speech synthesis analysis \[[42](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib42)\], although this has not been adequately investigated with dysarthric speech.

#### 3.4.2 Subjective Evaluation

Subjective evaluation by expert listeners (SLTs) has been used to measure the presence and severity of dysarthric speech characteristics in synthesised speech \[[30](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib30)\]. For this study, an SLT with $`> 10`$ years of experience assessing and diagnosing speech disorders perceptually evaluated the synthesised data. For every dysarthric speaker, $`20`$ audio samples from the TORGO database and $`20`$ synthetic audio samples were randomly selected. The selected audio samples were presented to the SLT individually in random order. Every audio sample was rated on overall dysarthria severity on a $`5`$-point scale (between $`0`$ for none and $`4`$ for severe) \[[1](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib1)\]. The mean scores were calculated for TORGO and synthetic audio per speaker, to allow comparison of the presence and severity of dysarthric speech characteristics.

#### 3.4.3 Word Error Rate (WER)

Inference was performed with the pretrained baseline Whisper model, and adapted dysarthric Whisper models on a given target speaker (adapted models were trained on the remaining speakers in a LOSO methodology). Transcripts were processed with Whisper’s English text normalizer,⁴⁴4Whisper normalizer: [https://github.com/openai/whisper/blob/main/whisper/normalizers](https://github.com/openai/whisper/blob/main/whisper/normalizers) and WER calculated between the processed reference and hypothesis transcripts. Average (Avg.) WER is calculated as the average of single-speaker WER scores, and severity group averages calculated as the average scores of speakers in the group (to allow direct comparison to similar studies \[[38](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08568v1#bib.bib38)\]). The overall (Ovl.) WER score, commonly used to assess ASR for typical speech was also calculated by computing the WER score for transcripts across all speakers.

## 5 Conclusion

This work showed that it is possible to train Grad-TTS from scratch without matched control data to synthesise samples with dysarthric speech characteristics. A $`\beta_{T} = 10`$ schedule improves sample quality for the typical and dysarthric speech data used. The results show that Whisper can be finetuned for SotA DASR on *TORGO*, and that data augmentation is beneficial. The amount of synthesised data required is dependent on the severity of the dysarthric speaker.
