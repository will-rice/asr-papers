# A Multi-signal large language model for device-directedness detection

###### Abstract

Device-directedness detection is an important task that enables fluent interactions with speech based virtual assistants. Unlike voice trigger detection, which only ensures the initial activation of a voice assistant based on a keyword phrase, the goal of device-directedness detection extends the decision to triggerless and follow-up interactions with an assistant that do not necessarily include a trigger phrase. We present an architecture for device-directedness detection that treats the task as a text-generation problem. We use a multi-modal fusion approach that combines acoustic information from the recorded waveform with text and confidence information obtained from an automatic speech recognition system. The audio waveform is represented as a sequence of continuous embeddings by an audio encoder and presented as a prefix token to a pre-trained language model. We demonstrate that using multi-modal information within language models yields equal error rate (EER) improvements over text-only and audio-only models of 38.9% and 20.5% respectively.

Index Terms—  device-directed speech detection, large language model, multi-modal, text generation

## 1 Introduction

Speech based virtual assistants allow users to interact with phones, watches, mixed reality headsets, speakers, and headphones via voice commands. Usually, the press of a button or a trigger phrase such as “Hey Siri” \[siri17\] precedes the first command to distinguish audio that is directed towards the device from background speech. To ensure a more natural dialog flow with the user, commands following the initial interaction may not include the trigger anymore. Device-directed speech detection addresses the problem of whether a virtual assistant was addressed or not, without a trigger cue preceding the voice command \[shriberg12_interspeech, mallidi18_interspeech\].

Several previous works report improvements, when combining acoustic or prosodic information with lexical features for device-directed speech detection and barge-in classification. Shriberg et al. \[shriberg12_interspeech\] combine classification scores from individual classifiers trained on lexical, acoustic, and prosodic features in a late-fusion approach using linear logistic regression. In \[mallidi18_interspeech\], a feedforward classification network is trained on feature vectors created by concatenating acoustic embeddings with embeddings obtained from 1-best hypotheses, and decoder features generated by an automatic speech recognition (ASR) system. In \[gillespie20dd\], input features to multiple recurrent and non-recurrent classifiers are generated by concatenating token-level ASR confidences with word representations obtained with a pretrained fastText \[bojanowski2017enriching\] model. Buddi et al. \[buddi23multimodal\] use features obtained from an accelerometer combined with audio recordings to detect smartwatch invocations. In \[sato23multimodal\], log-mel filterbank energy features are combined with word embeddings generated from the 1-best hypotheses of a speech recognizer using two fusion methods (concatenation and a mutual attention mechanism) on top of bi-directional long short-term memory layers. In \[bekal22_interspeech\], pretrained speech representations from Hidden-Unit BERT (HuBERT) \[hubert21\], lexical information generated with a pretrained BERT \[devlin-etal-2019-bert\] model, and information about the dialog state are concatenated and passed through a final linear layer to detect dialog interruptions between a user and a chat bot.

Recent works have extended large language models (LLMs) with the ability to process other modalities. The multi-modal PaLM-E \[driess2023palme\] combines a large pretrained vision transformer \[dehghani2023vit\] with the PaLM LLM \[chowdhery2022palm\] to perform robotic planning as well as visual and language tasks. In \[fathullah2023prompting\], a LLaMA-7B \[touvron2023llama\] LLM is finetuned to perform ASR by conditioning the model on a variable length sequence of audio embeddings. In \[gong2023ltu\], a pretrained audio spectrogram transformer \[gong21b_interspeech\] provides audio representations to a pretrained LLaMA-7B LLM that is finetuned using low-rank adaptation (LoRA) \[hu2022lora\] to perform a variety of open- and closed-ended tasks. The ClipCap \[mokady2021clipcap\] model performs image captioning using embeddings from a pretrained CLIP \[radford2021clip\] model as prefixes that are concatenated with caption token embeddings in a pretrained frozen GPT2 \[radford2019gpt2\] LLM. Kim et al. \[kim2023prefix\] perform audio captioning using a convolutional audio encoder that generates prefixes for a pretrained and frozen GPT2 model, which generates text descriptions corresponding to the input sound. In \[deshmukh2023pengi\], an audio encoder and a text encoder based on CLAP \[elizalde2022clap\] provide representations that are used as prefixes to a pretrained frozen GPT2 model to perform 22 downstream tasks such as audio captioning, speech emotion recognition and question answering.

In this work, we propose a multi-modal classifier for distinguishing device-directed utterances from background speech in interactions with virtual assistants that treats the task as a text-generation problem. Our approach is motivated by the observation that relying on acoustic information alone can fail due to background noises, overlapping speech etc., whereas errors from relying only on lexical information are driven by ambiguous or poorly transcribed sentences. Our model uses multi-modal information comprised of acoustic information obtained from a pretrained audio encoder and 1-best hypotheses as well as utterance-level decoder signals from an ASR system. The acoustic information and decoder signals are represented as prefix tokens to the 1-best hypotheses in a pretrained language model, which is then finetuned to generate the device-directedness decision, thereby allowing the model to jointly learn from acoustic and lexical information. ## 2 Data

### 2.1 Training Data

The training data are comprised of a balanced set of $`\approx`$40k directed utterances ($`\approx`$59 hours) and $`\approx`$40k non-directed utterances ($`\approx`$67 hours). Similar data has been used in \[dighe23a2i\] (???). The directed utterances are anonymized speech signals recorded with devices such as smartphones, which were triggered by a voice triggering system. The non-directed utterances contain utterances that falsely triggered the same voice triggering system, as well as a mixture of proxies such as podcasts, non-target languages, and dictated utterances. The additional proxy data is required, because false triggers of the voice triggering system are very rare and only account for a small portion of the overall data. The text portion of our training data was transcribed with an on-device Conformer based end-to-end speech recognition system (??????), representative of what a device-directedness system would see at decision time.

#### 2.1.1 Additional Text Data

An additional text corpus consisting of $`\approx`$ 3 million utterances of transcribed near-field speech signals recorded with devices such as smartphones is used as additional text-only information in our experiments. A randomly sampled subset of 500k utterances were combined with the multi-modal (text and audio) training set in some of our experiments, effectively simulating a dropout of the audio modality \[neverova16moddrop\].

### 2.2 Evaluation Data

We used three different datasets for evaluation. The first is the structured multi-condition dataset described in \[higuchi21kws\], where positive samples containing a trigger phrase were collected in different acoustic conditions from 100 participants, approximately evenly divided between males and females. ## 3 Method

![Refer to caption](https://arxiv.org/html/clapcap_arch.pdf)

Fig. 1: Architecture overview

The architecture of our model is depicted in Figure [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.14438v2#S3.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 3 Method ‣ A Multi-signal large language model for device-directedness detection"). It has three main components: (1) an audio encoder that extracts a sequence of latent representations in $`{\mathbb{R}}^{N}`$ from the input audio, (2) two mapping networks $`M_{1}`$ and $`M_{2}`$, that map the extracted audio features and the utterance-level ASR decoder signals into the token embedding space, and (3) a language model that generates text conditioned on the prefixes, the 1-best ASR hypotheses, and a text prompt.

Given a dataset of audio waveforms, decoder signals, and 1-best hypotheses $`\left\{ x^{i},d^{i},t^{i} \right\}_{i = 1}^{N}`$, the goal is the generation of a deviced-directedness decision (i.e., directed to a device “yes” or “no”) for an unseen set of features. The 1-best hypotheses are represented by a sequence of tokens $`t^{i} = {t_{1}^{i},\ldots,t_{l}^{i}}`$ that are padded to a maximum length $`l`$.

To extract representations from an audio input $`x^{i}`$, we use a Whisper model \[radford2022robust\] $`\mathcal{W}`$, which yields a sequence of continuous embeddings in $`{\mathbb{R}}^{1024}`$ with length $`T`$. We aggregate these embeddings along the time dimension using mean pooling, which yields a single vector in $`{\mathbb{R}}^{1024}`$ per utterance. Then, we employ a small feedforward network $`M_{1}`$ to map the aggregated embedding to a vector in the prefix token space of the LLM:

```math
a^{i} = {M_{1}{({\mathcal{W}{(x^{i})}})}}
```

Each representation $`a^{i}`$ has the same dimensionality $`E`$ as the token embeddings.

We use a second feedforward network $`M_{2}`$ to extract a latent representation for the decoder signals $`d^{i}`$, which yields a decoder signal prefix $`b^{i}`$ in $`{\mathbb{R}}^{E}`$. We then concatenate the audio prefix $`p^{i}`$ and the decoder signal prefix $`k^{i}`$ to the token embeddings of the corresponding 1-best hypothesis $`t^{i}`$. The language model is presented with the concatenated input features. The training objective is the prediction of directedness tokens conditioned on the preﬁx, the decoder signals, and the 1-best hypothesis tokens in an autoregressive fashion. Therefore, we train the model using cross entropy loss:

```math
\mathcal{L} = {- {\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{L}{\sum\limits_{j = 1}^{l}{{\log p_{\theta}}\left( {t_{j}^{i} \mid {a^{i},b^{i},t_{1}^{i},\ldots,t_{j - 1}^{i}}} \right)}}}}
```

where $`\theta`$ are the trainable parameters of the model.

### 3.1 Audio Encoder

We use the medium-sized (769M parameters) version of Whisper, a powerful encoder-decoder Transformer \[vaswani17attention\] for multilingual speech recognition trained on 680k hours of audio paired with transcripts. Whisper is well-suited for our task as it has seen a wide range of acoustic conditions and is expected to generalize well across domains and languages, although it is not specified to the target audio signals or spoken language content. The model is a good example of an end-to-end ASR system that would be available to employ for a device directedness task. The model receives 80-dimensional log-magnitude Mel spectrogram features with a window length of 25ms and a stride of 10ms.

### 3.2 Decoder Signals

Along with the 1-best hypothesis, we extract 4 additional signals that are generated by the lattice decoder. For the most likely hypothesis in the N-best set of hypotheses, we extract the sum of the graph cost associated with each word in the hypothesis, the sum of the acoustic cost, and the sum of the word-level posterior confidence scores. The graph cost is the sum of LM cost, transition probabilities, as well as pronunciation cost and the acoustic cost is the negated log-likelihood \[povey12wfst\]. Additionally, we add the number of competing hypotheses to the set of signals. Finally, we apply min-max scaling to scale each feature value into the unit interval $`\lbrack 0,1\rbrack`$.

### 3.3 Large Language Model

Several studies have shown that GPT2 can be adapted to new text generation tasks by providing learnable preﬁxes \[mokady2021clipcap, kim2023prefix, deshmukh2023pengi\]. Therefore, we use a GPT2 (124 million parameters) LLM, as the text generation model for our task. During training, we face the challenge of translating between the audio embedding space of Whisper and the lexical embedding space of the language model. Although both models generate rich and diverse representations, their latent spaces are independent. Since our main concern is not to maintain the expressivity of the language model for text generation but to receive accurate device-directedness decisions, we finetune the language model during training along with the mapping networks.

### 3.4 Mapping Networks

The mapping networks are designed to connect audio features and decoder signals with GPT2. The outputs of the mapping networks are used as prefixes to the text tokens. Each audio feature is translated to $`1 \times {\mathbb{R}}^{768}`$ sized prefixes, where 768 is the channel dimension. Both mapping networks share the same architecture. They consist of 3 linear layers, trained with a dropout probability of 10%.

### 3.5 Baseline

We train a simple linear audio-only classifier on top of Whisper. The classification head consists of a linear layer with 512 hidden units that receives the embedding sequence obtained from the last encoder layer of the Whisper model, followed by mean pooling across the time dimension and a final linear layer. The classifier was trained for 10 epochs with an effective batch size of 32 using cross entropy loss. We used the AdamW optimizer \[loshchilov2018decoupled\] with an initial learning rate of $`2 \times 10^{- 5}`$, and a warm-up phase of 10% of total training steps with a linear schedule. During training, all parameters of the audio encoder remained frozen

To compare the performance of a multi-modal system, we also train unimodal versions (either text-only or audio-only) of our proposed model.

### 3.6 Training Details

We zero-pad the input to the audio encoder, if the audio length is shorter than 30 seconds. The 1024-dimensional embeddings are extracted at the last encoder layer of the pretrained Whisper model. We train for 60 epochs with an effective batch size of 256. For optimization, we use AdamW with an initial learning rate of $`1 \times 10^{- 4}`$, a linear schedule amd a warm-up phase of 10% of total training steps.

## 5 Conclusions

We described a multi-modal classifier for distinguishing device-directed utterances from background speech in the context of interactions with virtual assistants. We provided audio representations and decoder signals as prefixes to an LLM and showed that the system is able to effectively combine audio and lexical information. The proposed model outperformed unimodal systems on the device-directedness detection task and is more versatile than specialized models, as it can be easily extended to other tasks such as audio captioning or acoustic scene classification. Furthermore, our approach reached a satisfying performance, while only using a small portion of the training data that is commonly used to train comparable systems. LLMs play an increasingly important role in human–computer interaction and are deployed on many devices directly, or accessible via cloud services. Utilizing the widespread availability of LLMs to perform tasks such as device-directedness detection that previously required specialized models could be a step towards a more efficient usage of available resources. Future work will focus on combining our approach with additional tasks that can be useful during interactions with virtual assistants such as audio captioning and acoustic scene classification, to analyze their compatibility.
