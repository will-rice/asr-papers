###### Abstract

We introduce STAR (Stream Transduction with Anchor Representations), a novel Transformer-based model designed for efficient sequence-to-sequence transduction over *streams*. STAR dynamically segments input streams to create compressed *anchor* representations, achieving nearly lossless compression (12$`\times`$) in Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) and outperforming existing methods. Moreover, STAR demonstrates superior segmentation and latency-quality trade-offs in simultaneous speech-to-text tasks, optimizing latency, memory footprint, and quality.¹¹1 Codes available at: [https://github.com/steventan0110/STAR](https://github.com/steventan0110/STAR)

## 1 Introduction

Sequence transduction, also referred to as sequence-to-sequence modeling, has shown remarkable success across various domains, including speech translation Liu et al. ([2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib35)); Di Gangi et al. ([2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib15)); Li et al. ([2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib32)) and automatic speech recognition Prabhavalkar et al. ([2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib46)); Li ([2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib31)); Gulati et al. ([2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib22)). Traditionally, these models operate under the assumption of fully observing input sequences before generating outputs. However, this requirement becomes impractical in applications necessitating low latency or real-time output generation such as simultaneous translation (Ma et al., [2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib37); Chang and Lee, [2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib7); Barrault et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib4), inter alia). The concept of streaming sequence transduction Inaguma et al. ([2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib26)); Kameoka et al. ([2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib27)); Chen et al. ([2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib8)); Wang et al. ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib58)); Chen et al. ([2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib8)); Xue et al. ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib61)), or stream transduction, arises to address this challenge. Unlike traditional sequence transduction, stream transduction operates on partially observed input sequences while simultaneously generating outputs. This requires deciding when to initiate output generation, a task inherently tied to identifying critical triggers within the input sequence. Triggers mark moments when sufficient input information has been received to initiate output generation, thus minimizing latency. Consequently, they partition the input sequence into discrete segments, with outputs accessing only information preceding each trigger.

![Refer to caption](https://arxiv.org/html/x1.png)

Figure 1: When yield is triggered, the current segment’s information is compressed into an anchor representation to generate the next output.

Locating these triggers poses a significant challenge. Prior approaches have explored methods that employ fixed sliding windows to determine triggers Ma et al. ([2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib37), [2020b](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib39)), or learning models to predict triggers Ma et al. ([2020c](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib40)); Chang and Lee ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib7)), yet timing remains a complex issue. Beyond reducing latency, another challenge for stream transduction is how to efficiently represent historical information while optimizing memory usage. Prior work (Rae et al., [2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib49); Tay et al., [2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib54); Bertsch et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib6), inter alia) has mostly focused on improving the efficiency of Transformer but does not investigate streaming scenarios. Reducing the memory footprint for streaming systems introduces additional complexity as models must determine when certain information becomes less relevant for future predictions.

In this work, we propose Stream Transduction with Anchor Representations (STAR), a novel approach designed to maximize the benefits of stream transduction, optimizing both generation latency and memory footprint. STAR dynamically segments the input stream into buffers that contain similar levels of information. Then, it introduces the concept of anchors, which aggregate a buffer of information (multiple vector representations) into single-vector anchor representations. Once an anchor representation is yielded, it triggers the generation process to yield another token.

We present a learning strategy to train STAR end-to-end so that the model learns to dynamically select anchor positions with the following objectives: (1) anchor positions are selected such that each segment contains the right amount of information for generating the next output; (2) anchor representation effectively compress the information of its preceding segment. For example, in [fig. 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#S1.F1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ Streaming Sequence Transduction through Dynamic Compression"), the model triggers yield at index 3 (which makes it an anchor position), compressing the information of the current chunk $`{\mathbf{X}} = {(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})}`$ into anchor representation $`z_{1}`$ to generate output $`y_{1}`$. Such a process repeats each time yield is triggered. To summarize, our contributions are as follows: (1) we propose STAR that dynamically segments and compresses input streams, trading-off among latency, memory footprint, and performance for stream transduction; (2) we validate the effectiveness of our approach on well-established *speech-to-text* tasks. Our results show that STAR greatly outperforms existing methods, obtaining better compression ability and excelling in quality-latency trade-offs.

## 2 Methodology

### 2.1 Problem Formulation

In sequence-to-sequence transduction, feature $`{\mathbf{X}} = {({\mathbf{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{x}}_{T_{x}})}`$ is normally first extracted from the raw input sequence. Then the decoder can encode and use such features to generate an output sequence $`{\mathbf{Y}} = {(y_{1},\ldots,y_{T_{y}})}`$. The encoder and decoder can be implemented using various models such as Recurrent Neural Networks Hochreiter and Schmidhuber ([1997](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib23)); Chung et al. ([2014](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib10)); Lipton ([2015](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib33)) and Transformers Vaswani et al. ([2017](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib57)), depending on the input and output characteristics. In the context of streaming sequence transduction, where the input (and their features $`\mathbf{X}`$) is partially observed, a causal encoder and decoder are necessary. The causal encoder processes the partially observed feature $`{\mathbf{X}}_{< \tau}`$ ($`\tau \leq T_{x}`$) to produce their encoding. Suppose the first $`k`$ outputs are already generated, the causal decoder sample the next output $`y_{k + 1}`$ with $`{\mathbb{P}}{(\left. y_{k + 1} \middle| {{\mathbf{X}}_{< \tau},{\mathbf{Y}}_{< {k + 1}};\theta} \right.)}`$, where $`\theta`$ represents the parameter set.

1:Input: Input stream $`\mathbf{X}`$, threshold $`\beta`$

2:Output: Output stream $`\mathbf{Y}`$

3:Initialize: cached repr. $`{\mathbf{Z}}\leftarrow\varnothing`$; buffer $`{\mathbf{B}}\leftarrow\varnothing`$ \While$`y \neq \text{eos}`$

4:$`{\alpha\leftarrow 0};{{\mathbf{B}}\leftarrow\varnothing}`$ $`\rhd`$ clear buffer \While$`{\mathbf{x}}\leftarrow{\text{read}{({\mathbf{X}})}}`$ $`\rhd`$ read new inputs

5:append($`{\mathbf{B}},{\mathbf{x}}`$) $`\rhd`$ add to buffer

6:$`\alpha\leftarrow{\alpha + {F_{seg}{({\mathbf{x}})}}}`$ \If$`\alpha \geq \beta`$$`\rhd`$ yield triggered

7:$`{\mathbf{H}} = {F_{enc}{({{\mathbf{B}} \mid {\mathbf{Z}}})}}`$ $`\rhd`$ encode segment buffer

8:$`{\mathbf{z}} = {\text{compress}{({\mathbf{H}})}}`$

9:append($`{\mathbf{Z}},{\mathbf{z}})`$ $`\rhd`$ embedding for segment

10:$`y\leftarrow F_{dec}{( \cdot |{\mathbf{Y}},{\mathbf{Z}})}`$

11:yield $`y`$

12:break \EndIf\EndWhile\EndWhile

Algorithm 1 High-level overview of STAR

Deciding when to generate (yield) a new token is the core of streaming sequence transduction where a segmenter/predictor Moritz et al. ([2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib41)); Chang and Lee ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib7)) is typically trained to control timing for yield operation. Our approach to tackling stream transduction is outlined in [algorithm 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#alg1 "In 2.1 Problem Formulation ‣ 2 Methodology ‣ Streaming Sequence Transduction through Dynamic Compression"). It involves a learnable segmenter that scores the importance of each input feature to decide if enough information has been accumulated in the current buffer of features. As the segmenter scores input feature in a frame-wise fashion ([algorithm 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#alg1 "In 2.1 Problem Formulation ‣ 2 Methodology ‣ Streaming Sequence Transduction through Dynamic Compression"), line 8), we accumulate the scores $`\alpha`$ until it reaches a pre-defined threshold $`\beta`$. When the threshold is reached, it indicates that enough information has been accumulated in the current buffer $`\mathbf{B}`$ of features. Subsequently, we compress the features into a single vector representation $`\mathbf{z}`$ that we call anchor representation (line 11). $`\mathbf{z}`$ is computed for each buffer and cached into the history anchors $`\mathbf{Z}`$, which is then conditioned by the decoder to generate new tokens (lines 12-13). The details of our segmentation and compression mechanism are introduced in [§ 2.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#S2.SS2 "2.2 Segmentation with Dynamic Compression ‣ 2 Methodology ‣ Streaming Sequence Transduction through Dynamic Compression").

### 2.2 Segmentation with Dynamic Compression

In this section, we provide details of different components in [algorithm 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#alg1 "In 2.1 Problem Formulation ‣ 2 Methodology ‣ Streaming Sequence Transduction through Dynamic Compression"). We first describe how to learn the segmenter $`F_{seg}{( \cdot )}`$ with feedback from the encoder-decoder’s cross-attention. Then we present how anchor representations are obtained through our selection-based compression method.

#### Learning Segmenter with Cross-attention

We propose a learnable segmenter trained with feedback from the encoder-decoder cross-attention. Following [algorithm 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#alg1 "In 2.1 Problem Formulation ‣ 2 Methodology ‣ Streaming Sequence Transduction through Dynamic Compression"), a segmenter is used to evaluate (score) input features as they are read into the system. Such scores $`{\mathbf{s}} = {F_{seg}{({\mathbf{X}})}}`$ are then used to determine if yield is triggered (i.e., whether to segment streams). Effective segmentation is crucial in streaming sequence transduction to avoid sub-optimal transformation due to premature triggering or increased latency from delayed output. Since the ideal segmentation depends on several factors (the input’s information density, the input and output’s modalities, and the task at hand, *etc.,*), we rely on the cross-attention between the encoder and decoder to guide the segmenter (shown in [fig. 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#S2.F2 "In Learning Segmenter with Cross-attention ‣ 2.2 Segmentation with Dynamic Compression ‣ 2 Methodology ‣ Streaming Sequence Transduction through Dynamic Compression")).

Specifically, we follow cross-attention from Transformers Vaswani et al. ([2017](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib57)) to use three projections $`{\mathbf{W}}_{\text{Q}},{\mathbf{W}}_{\text{K}},{\mathbf{W}}_{\text{V}}`$ to generate the query vector $`{{\mathbf{y}}{\mathbf{W}}_{\text{Q}}} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{T_{y} \times d}`$, the key vector $`{{\mathbf{h}}{\mathbf{W}}_{\text{K}}} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{T_{x} \times d}`$ and the value vector $`{{\mathbf{h}}{\mathbf{W}}_{\text{V}}} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{T_{x} \times d}`$ (where $`d`$ is the dimensionality of the representation) and compute cross-attention as:

``` math
{S{({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{y}})}} = {{({{\mathbf{h}}{\mathbf{W}}_{\text{k}}})}{({{\mathbf{y}}{\mathbf{W}}_{\text{Q}}})}^{T}}
```

Then, as illustrated in [fig. 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#S2.F2 "In Learning Segmenter with Cross-attention ‣ 2.2 Segmentation with Dynamic Compression ‣ 2 Methodology ‣ Streaming Sequence Transduction through Dynamic Compression"), we inject segmenter’s scores into it the cross attention:

``` math
{\overset{\sim}{S}{({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{y}})}} = {{S{({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{y}})}} + {F_{seg}{({\mathbf{x}})}}}
```

The updated cross-attention $`\overset{\sim}{S}{({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{y}})}`$ is then used to transform the value vector $`{\mathbf{W}}_{\text{V}}`$ and will be used by the decoder to compute the loss function. Since the segmenter’s scores are injected in [equation 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#S2.E2 "In Learning Segmenter with Cross-attention ‣ 2.2 Segmentation with Dynamic Compression ‣ 2 Methodology ‣ Streaming Sequence Transduction through Dynamic Compression"), *it can be updated with end-to-end back-propagation*. Specifically, suppose the loss objective $`\mathcal{L}`$ is computed, with the chain rule, we have the gradient for the predicted score $`{\mathbf{α}} = {F_{\text{seg}}{({\mathbf{x}})}}`$ as:

$`\frac{\nabla\mathcal{L}}{\nabla{\mathbf{α}}}`$$`= {\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{l}{\frac{\nabla\mathcal{L}}{{\nabla{\overset{\sim}{S}}^{i}}{({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{y}})}} \cdot \frac{{\nabla{\overset{\sim}{S}}^{i}}{({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{y}})}}{\nabla{\mathbf{α}}}}}`$$`= {\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{l}{{\frac{\nabla\mathcal{L}}{{\nabla{\overset{\sim}{S}}^{i}}{({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{y}})}} \cdot \frac{\nabla}{\nabla{\mathbf{α}}}}\left( {{S{({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{y}})}} + {\mathbf{α}}} \right)}}`$$`= {\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{l}\frac{\nabla\mathcal{L}}{{\nabla{\overset{\sim}{S}}^{i}}{({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{y}})}}}`$

where $`l`$ is the number of transformer layer and $`{\overset{\sim}{S}}^{i}{({\mathbf{h}},{\mathbf{y}})}`$ is the cross-attention for $`i^{\text{th}}`$ layer. We observe that the gradient impacting the segmenters is directly proportional to the gradient on the cross-attention logits. Consequently, by injecting cross-attention, we can train segmenters to prioritize positions that are more significant to the decoder.

After training the segmenter, we predict scores $`{\mathbf{s}} = {F_{seg}{({\mathbf{x}})}}`$ for input features and use the scores to segment the input sequence. Note that the predicted scores can be used differently based on the task. In the special case where the whole sequence is fully observed (i.e., regular non-streaming tasks), we do not yield output anymore. Instead, we simply select the top $`k`$ scoring positions as anchors and use their representation for the decoder to generate outputs, as formalized below ($`I`$ is a set of indices):

$`I`$$`= {\text{SelectTop}_{k}{({\mathbf{s}})}}`$$`\mathbf{H}`$$`= {F_{enc}{({\mathbf{x}})}} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{T_{x} \times d}`$$`\mathbf{Z}`$$`= {{\mathbf{H}}{\lbrack I\rbrack}} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{k \times d}`$

The compression rate is then $`r = {T_{x}/k} \in {\lbrack 1,\infty)}`$ assuming $`k \leq T_{x}`$. In a more general case where streaming is enabled, the score is commonly accumulated Inaguma et al. ([2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib26)); Ma et al. ([2020c](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib40)) until a certain threshold is reached. We use a threshold $`\beta = 1`$ throughout experiments. Specifically, we first scale $`\mathbf{s}`$ to $`\lbrack 0,1\rbrack`$ range values $`{\mathbf{α}} = {\text{sigmoid}{({\mathbf{s}})}}`$ and accumulate $`\alpha`$ following [algorithm 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#alg1 "In 2.1 Problem Formulation ‣ 2 Methodology ‣ Streaming Sequence Transduction through Dynamic Compression") (line 8) to yield new output. The accumulation of scores is a natural way to ensure a similar level of information is contained in each buffer. This corresponds to a larger buffer when the sound signal is sparse (see [appendix A](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#A1 "Appendix A Qualitative Examples of Speech Segmentation from Compressors ‣ Streaming Sequence Transduction through Dynamic Compression") for visualization), which gives better latency-quality control.

#### Compression with Anchor Representation

Every time an anchor is predicted by our trained segmenter, the model triggers generation with some buffer $`{\mathbf{B}} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{b \times d}`$ of b features. Subsequently, we transform such features into a high-dimensional representation $`{\mathbf{H}} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{b \times d}`$ with a causal encoder²²2 In practice, we are inspired by BERT Devlin et al. ([2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib14)) to add a special type embedding $`\mathbf{e}`$ to anchor tokens before passing through the encoder. The causality of such an encoder ensures that representations at later positions contain information only from earlier positions. Then, we only select the representation at the anchor position (the last index of the current buffer) $`{\mathbf{z}} = {{\mathbf{H}}{\lbrack b\rbrack}} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{1 \times d}`$ to represent the information of the whole buffer $`\mathbf{B}`$. Selected representations are also called anchor representations/vectors. For example, in [fig. 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#S2.F3 "In Compression with Anchor Representation ‣ 2.2 Segmentation with Dynamic Compression ‣ 2 Methodology ‣ Streaming Sequence Transduction through Dynamic Compression"), yield is triggered at index $`3`$; therefore we first transform the features into representations $`{\mathbf{H}} = {F_{enc}{(\left. {\mathbf{B}} \middle| {\mathbf{Z}} \right.)}}`$, and select $`{\mathbf{H}}{\lbrack 3\rbrack}`$ as the anchor vector $`\mathbf{z}`$ to decode the next output with cached representation $`\mathbf{Z}`$.

![Refer to caption](https://arxiv.org/html/x3.png)

Figure 3: Visualization for the proposed “selection as compression” method. Input features are transformed by the encoder and we only select the encoding at the anchor position (where yield is triggered) as the compressed representation.

### 2.3 Model Training

To train models for streaming sequence transduction, we primarily rely on the conventional objective – negative log-likelihood (NLL) loss:

$`\mathcal{L}_{\text{NLL}}{({\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{Y}},\theta)}`$$`= {- {{\log{\mathbb{P}}}{(\left. {\mathbf{Y}} \middle| {{\mathbf{X}};\theta} \right.)}}}`$$`= {- {\sum\limits_{t = 1}^{T_{y}}{{\log{\mathbb{P}}}{(\left. y_{t} \middle| {{\mathbf{Y}}_{< t},{\mathbf{Z}}_{< t};\theta} \right.)}}}}`$

Note that the loss is defined over $`{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{Y}}`$ as both input and output sequences are fully observed during training. In addition, the loss defined in [equation 6](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#S2.E6 "In 2.3 Model Training ‣ 2 Methodology ‣ Streaming Sequence Transduction through Dynamic Compression") is slightly different than regular NLL in that the decoder can only use representation observed so far ($`{\mathbf{Z}}_{< t}`$) to generate the $`t^{\text{th}}`$ output. This method is also referred to as Infinite-Lookback (Arivazhagan et al., [2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib2); Liu et al., [2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib34), IL) and is used to mitigate the train-test mismatch as future representation cannot be observed during inference. Besides using NLL to update the encoder and decoder, we also follow prior work Chang and Lee ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib7)) to regularize the segmenter so that the number of yield is the same as the output length $`T_{y}`$. Due to page limitations, we refer readers to [appendix B](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#A2 "Appendix B Continuous Integrate and Fire ‣ Streaming Sequence Transduction through Dynamic Compression") for more details.

## 3 Experiments: Non-Streaming Compression

We experiment on the non-streaming ASR task to better demonstrate the effectiveness of our selection-based compression method, since we do not need to consider the quality-latency trade-off as in the streaming scenario. We compare our method with other common baselines like Convolutional Neural Networks (Lecun and Bengio, [1995](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib30); Krizhevsky et al., [2012](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib29), CNN) and Continuous Integrate and Fire (Dong and Xu, [2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib16), CIF).

#### Datasets and Evaluation Metrics

We conduct experiments on the LibriSpeech Panayotov et al. ([2015](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib44)) and LibriTTS Zen et al. ([2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib63)) dataset’s “Clean-360h” section, which contains 360 hours of speech and their corresponding transcriptions. To evaluate ASR performance, we compute the word error rate (Morris et al., [2004](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib42), WER) between reference transcriptions and the generated text.

### 3.1 Training Setup

#### Compression with Anchor Representations

In [§ 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#S2 "2 Methodology ‣ Streaming Sequence Transduction through Dynamic Compression"), we propose a general approach for stream transduction with dynamic compression. Now we instantiate the framework for the ASR task. We first use Wav2Vec2.0 Baevski et al. ([2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib3)) to extract features $`\mathbf{X}`$ from the input speech sequence. We then use a 4-layer decoder-only Transformer³³3 Following the implementation of GPT2 from Huggingface [https://huggingface.co/gpt2](https://huggingface.co/gpt2) as our causal encoder for compression, from which we select out anchor representation $`\mathbf{z}`$. The segmenter is implemented with a 2-layer Feed-Forward Network. For the decoder, we use a 4-layer decoder-only Transformer with an additional linear layer as the language modeling head. For details of hyperparameters, we direct readers to [appendix F](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#A6 "Appendix F Hyper-parameters ‣ Streaming Sequence Transduction through Dynamic Compression").

As described in [§ 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#S2 "2 Methodology ‣ Streaming Sequence Transduction through Dynamic Compression"), we train the Encoder-Decoder model with a segmenter learned through cross-attention feedback. Given the extracted feature $`{\mathbf{X}} = {(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{T_{x}})}`$ and a target compression rate $`r \in {\lbrack 1,\infty)}`$, we select top $`k = {T_{x}/r}`$ scoring positions and use their encodings as anchor representations (following [equation 5](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#S2.E5 "In Learning Segmenter with Cross-attention ‣ 2.2 Segmentation with Dynamic Compression ‣ 2 Methodology ‣ Streaming Sequence Transduction through Dynamic Compression")). We then feed the anchor representation $`\mathbf{Z}`$ to the decoder to generate text tokens. In practice, most input speeches from LibriTTS are less than 10 seconds, corresponding to a feature sequence of length $`T_{x} = {{10 \ast 16000}/320} = 500`$ (with a standard sampling rate 16 kHz and Wav2Vec2.0 has a stack of CNNs that reduce input sequence by 320$`\times`$). Therefore, we chose some reasonable compression rates (i.e., $`r = {12,18,30}`$) to test our compression methods. We now briefly describe two baselines that we compared against: CNNs and CIF.

#### Baseline: CNN

A simple compression component is CNN. After we obtain speech feature $`\mathbf{X}`$, we apply CNNs with pre-defined strides to compress the feature. The encoder (a vanilla Transformer-Encoder module without our selection-based compression) further transforms such compressed features into encoder representations for the decoder to generate outputs. To enhance the capacity of CNNs, we follow Zeghidour et al. ([2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib62)); Défossez et al. ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib18)) to add two CNNs with kernel size $`(5,1)`$ and stride size $`(1,1)`$ as residual connection. More details about CNNs and their configurations are available in [fig. 13](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#A6.F13 "In Appendix F Hyper-parameters ‣ Streaming Sequence Transduction through Dynamic Compression") (in [appendix F](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#A6 "Appendix F Hyper-parameters ‣ Streaming Sequence Transduction through Dynamic Compression")).

#### Baseline: CIF

Continuous Integrate and Fire Dong and Xu ([2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib16)); Dong et al. ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib17)); Chang and Lee ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib7)) uses a neural network to predict scores for each position and accumulates the scores until a threshold is reached, thereafter triggering the generation of a new token (called fire by the original paper). For each segment, CIF averages representations in the segment by directly weighing them with the predicted scores. For a fair comparison with prior work, we adopt the implementation from Dong et al. ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib17)) into our codebase.

There are two major differences between our method and CIF: firstly, STAR segmenter leverages cross-attention between encoder-decoder to interactively update representations, whereas CIF employs a weighted average of representations solely from the encoder side; secondly, STAR pushes information to condense in particular anchor at yield positions and performs explicit selections, whereas CIF’s representations are averaged across each segment. Broadly, these distinctions mirror the differences between hard and soft attention mechanisms Xu et al. ([2015](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib60)); Luong et al. ([2015](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib36)). We refer readers to [appendix B](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#A2 "Appendix B Continuous Integrate and Fire ‣ Streaming Sequence Transduction through Dynamic Compression") and the original paper Dong and Xu ([2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib16)) for more details.

## 4 Streaming Experiments: Simultaneous Speech Recognition and Translation

#### Datasets

For our simultaneous S2T experiments, we use the English-German (EN-DE) portion of the MuST-C V1 Di Gangi et al. ([2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib15)) dataset for speech translation (ST). We also include results for simultaneous ASR using LibriSpeech and LibriTTS. Note that since our method is based on a general Encoder-Decoder Transformer, it is not tailored to ASR by leveraging monotonic alignment or using small character-level vocabulary.

#### Evaluation Metric

To evaluate the quality of generated output, we use WER for the ASR task and BLEU Papineni et al. ([2002](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib45)) for the speech translation task. For simultaneous S2T, latency measurement is essential and we resort to the commonly used metric, Differentiable Average Lagging (Arivazhagan et al., [2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib2), DAL), which was originally proposed for simultaneous text translation and later adapted to speech translation in Ma et al. ([2020a](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib38)). The smaller the DAL, the better the system in terms of latency. We refer readers to [appendix G](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#A7 "Appendix G Differentiable Average Lagging ‣ Streaming Sequence Transduction through Dynamic Compression") for details on the latency metric.

#### Experiment Setup

Our first step is to train an *speech-to-text* (S2T) streaming model without a segmenter. To make Wav2Vec2.0 causal, we add a causal mask and train it jointly with the encoder and decoder until convergence. Once the vanilla streaming S2T model is trained, we freeze the causal Wav2Vec2.0 model as the feature extractor and start fine-tuning the encoder and the decoder with the segmenter.

#### Experimental Results

We show the experiment results in [fig. 5](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#S3.F5 "In 3.2 Results of Different Compression Methods ‣ 3 Experiments: Non-Streaming Compression ‣ Streaming Sequence Transduction through Dynamic Compression") where we plot WER/BLEU v.s. DAL to demonstrate the quality-latency trade-off for each system. In our evaluation, we adapt the wait-$`k`$ policy Ma et al. ([2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib37)) for all systems. Here wait-$`k`$ denotes the number of speech segments we encode first before decoding text tokens. A larger wait-$`k`$ value generally results in higher latency but better S2T performance. In our work, we focus on low-latency scenarios where flexible decision policies like CIF and STAR are most useful; Therefore, we set wait-$`k`$ value to 1 to 5.

We first present the baseline system for simultaneous ASR with a fixed decision policy in [fig. 6](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#S4.F6 "In Experiment Setup ‣ 4 Streaming Experiments: Simultaneous Speech Recognition and Translation ‣ Streaming Sequence Transduction through Dynamic Compression"). We use the vanilla streaming S2T model (no compression) and apply a fixed stride size to slide through the speech and generate text tokens. As shown in [fig. 6](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#S4.F6 "In Experiment Setup ‣ 4 Streaming Experiments: Simultaneous Speech Recognition and Translation ‣ Streaming Sequence Transduction through Dynamic Compression"), using a large stride like 360ms (i.e., each chunk corresponds to a speech feature of length $`{{0.36 \times 16000}/320} = 18`$) or 440ms, simultaneous ASR achieved $`< 20`$ WER. However, the latency is also extremely high (over 2000 DAL). For smaller strides, quality of generated output is suboptimal because not enough information is provided for the text decoder to generate each new token. A flexible decision policy could alleviate such issues and provide better latency-quality trade-off. From [fig. 5](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#S3.F5 "In 3.2 Results of Different Compression Methods ‣ 3 Experiments: Non-Streaming Compression ‣ Streaming Sequence Transduction through Dynamic Compression"), we see that for both CIF and STAR, their output has better quality when the latency is low. For instance, on LibriTTS, STAR achieves about 24 WER with a DAL smaller than 800 while the best-performing fixed decision policy only obtains such performance with a DAL of about 1200.

Comparing CIF with STAR across three datasets (LibriSpeech, LibriTTS, and MUST-C), we find that STAR consistently achieves better performance, obtaining a lower WER (or higher BLEU) score with relatively lower latency across different wait-$`k`$ strategies. This demonstrates that STAR gives a better flexible policy to yield new tokens, and the compressed representation encodes more information for target text generation. In [appendix A](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#A1 "Appendix A Qualitative Examples of Speech Segmentation from Compressors ‣ Streaming Sequence Transduction through Dynamic Compression"), we compare qualitative examples and visualize the difference in the segmentation from CIF and STAR. Overall, we find the segmentation from STAR better corresponds to the target texts, achieving superior simultaneous S2T performance.

### 5.1 Memory Efficiency

![Refer to caption](https://arxiv.org/html/x7.png)

Figure 7: Memory usage and reduction from our proposed method (with compression rates $`r \in {\{ 2,5,10,20\}}`$). More results and detailed setup are provided in [appendix E](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#A5 "Appendix E Memory Usage Benchmark ‣ Streaming Sequence Transduction through Dynamic Compression").

Since STAR condenses information in each buffer into anchor representation, it enhances memory efficiency by caching compressed representation for the decoder to generate outputs. With a compression rate $`r`$, a batch size $`b`$, and input features of average length $`T_{x}`$, and hidden dimension $`d`$, our system compresses the encoder representation from $`bdT_{x}`$ to $`{bdT_{x}}/r`$. Besides memory consumption, note that cross-attention computation ([equation 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#S2.E1 "In Learning Segmenter with Cross-attention ‣ 2.2 Segmentation with Dynamic Compression ‣ 2 Methodology ‣ Streaming Sequence Transduction through Dynamic Compression")) is quadratic w.r.t. encoder representation’s length; thus, our method reduces the cost of its computation by a factor of $`r^{2}`$. Besides theoretical analysis, we benchmark the actual memory usage and the percentage of usage reduction achieved by different compression rates. From [fig. 7](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#S5.F7 "In 5.1 Memory Efficiency ‣ 5 Analysis ‣ Streaming Sequence Transduction through Dynamic Compression"), we show that with a rate of $`r = 10`$ (which achieves nearly lossless compression), STAR reduces the memory consumption by more than 30% when transducing an input feature of length longer than 3,000. For the full details of our benchmark setup and results, we refer readers to [appendix E](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#A5 "Appendix E Memory Usage Benchmark ‣ Streaming Sequence Transduction through Dynamic Compression").

In this section, we evaluate the robustness of streaming models (CIF and STAR) by subjecting them to compression and segmentation conditions different from their training setup. We find that STAR is more robust than CIF, retaining better transduction when operating on context windows not exposed to during training.

#### Various Compression Rates at Inference

As detailed in [§ 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#S3 "3 Experiments: Non-Streaming Compression ‣ Streaming Sequence Transduction through Dynamic Compression"), we trained CIF- and STAR-based models with a compression rate of $`r = 12`$ (denoted as CIF-12 and STAR-12) and tested them under varying compression rates. Both models perform well at $`r \leq 12`$, as expected since they are trained for 12$`\times`$ compression. However, when $`r > 12`$, STAR-12 shows significantly less degradation compared to CIF-12, indicating superior retention of information. This resilience arises from STAR’s design, which focuses information into anchor positions, ensuring each anchor retains substantial information even at higher compression rates. In contrast, CIF’s averaging approach leads to increased interference between representations.

## 6 Related work

#### End-to-end Streaming Speech-to-text

For streaming/simultaneous speech-to-text tasks, learning speech representation and policies for read and yield is essential. Previous methods like RNN-Transducer Graves ([2012](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib20)) and Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) (Graves et al., [2006](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib21)) leverage monotonic alignment for low error rate transcription. Recent work Moritz et al. ([2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib41)); Tsunoo et al. ([2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib55)) further extends transformers for streaming ASR using modified attention and beam search.

For speech translation, Ma et al. ([2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib37)) proposed the Wait-K strategy with a fixed decision policy that read chunks of equal-length text for decoding and Ma et al. ([2020b](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib39)) adapted the wait-k strategy for simultaneous speech translation. Instead of a fixed decision policy, SimulSpeech Ren et al. ([2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib50)) trained segmenters with CTC loss. Zeng et al. ([2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib64)) also use CTC for guidance on word boundary learns to shrink the representation and proposes the Wait-K-Stride-N strategy that writes N tokens for each read action. Dong et al. ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib17)) and Chang and Lee ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib7)) use CIF to learn segmentation for the speech sequences and trigger the yield action whenever CIF fire a new representation. Additionally, Arivazhagan et al. ([2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib2)) and Ma et al. ([2020c](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib40)) support a more adaptive strategy where dynamic read and yield are possible. However, even for such an adaptive strategy, a good decision policy still matters Ma et al. ([2020b](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib39)).

#### Efficient Methods for Transformers

Prior work studied efficient methods to scale Transformers to long sequences (Tay et al., [2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib54)), including sparse patterns (Beltagy et al., [2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib5)), recurrence (Dai et al., [2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib12)), kernelized attentions (Choromanski et al., [2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib9)), etc. Some of them can be applied in the streaming settings, such as Streaming LLMs (Xiao et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib59)), Compressive Transformers (Rae et al., [2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib49)), etc. Moreover, Tworkowski et al. ([2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib56)); Bertsch et al. ([2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib6)) proposed to apply $`k`$NN to the attention to select a subset of past tokens, akin to the segmentation process in this paper. Similar to the residual connection in our paper, Nugget (Qin and Van Durme, [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib48)) trains a scorer to select a subset of tokens to represent texts. More recently, Tan et al. ([2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib53)) and Qin et al. ([2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib47)) also combine context compression with efficient fine-tuning methods like LoRA Hu et al. ([2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib25)) to expand context length for large language models.

#### Speech Representation

Traditionally, acoustic features are extracted by filter-bank features, mel-frequency cepstral coefficients, or bottleneck features Muda et al. ([2010](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib43)); Davis and Mermelstein ([1980](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib13)). More recent work relies on self-supervision to learn speech representations. For example, Zeghidour et al. ([2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib62)) and Défossez et al. ([2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib18)) learn acoustic representation by reconstructing the original audio. To learn semantic representation, masked language modeling, and contrastive learning objectives are popularized by widely used representations from Hubert Hsu et al. ([2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib24)), w2v-BERT Chung et al. ([2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib11)) and Wav2Vec Schneider et al. ([2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib51)); Baevski et al. ([2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib3)). All these models use CNNs as a building block to downsample speech signals/representations.

## 7 Conclusion and Future Work

We introduce STAR, a model designed for dynamic compression and transduction of streams. STAR features a segmenter learned via encoder-decoder cross-attention and employs a selection-based compression approach. Our experiments across multiple speech-to-text tasks confirm STAR’s superior compression performance and latency-quality trade-off relative to established methods such as Convolutional Neural Networks and Continuous Integrate-and-Fire. In the future, we hope to extend this framework to facilitate streaming non-autoregressive generation.

The second approach is inspired by the MaxSim (MS) algorithm used in ColBERT Khattab and Zaharia ([2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib28)), which computes the average of maximum similarity across the compressed representations. Then we measure the quality of our trained speech semantic encoders with metrics widely used in retrieval and ranking–Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (nDCG) and Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR). From the results shown in [table 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#A4.T2 "In Appendix D Similarity Test with Compressed Representation ‣ Streaming Sequence Transduction through Dynamic Compression"), STAR still obtains the best-performing representation, with $`{{\text{MRR}@10} = 0.087},{{\text{nDCG}@10} = 0.453}`$. Note that the performance is not very high as we did not train the model specifically for the sentence similarity task. Rather, we used the similarity task as an intrinsic measurement for the quality of condensed representations to exclude the influence of the text decoder. Comparing the numbers in [table 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#A4.T2 "In Appendix D Similarity Test with Compressed Representation ‣ Streaming Sequence Transduction through Dynamic Compression"), STAR consistently obtains better speech representation (for both MP and MS algorithms) for the similarity task. Interestingly we find that STAR-30’s representation works better in mean pooling compared to STAR-12, suggesting that more condensed information works better for mean pooling. We define the length of $`i^{th}`$ segment (chunk) as $`|X_{i}|`$ (so that $`{|X|} = {\sum_{j = 1}^{|X|}{|X_{j}|}}`$), and we define $`d_{i} = {\sum_{t = 1}^{i}{|X_{t}|}}`$ as the total time that has elapsed until $`i^{th}`$ speech segment $`X_{i}`$ is processed. With the aforementioned notation, DAL is defined to be: ``` math \text{DAL} = {{\frac{1}{T_{y}}{\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{T_{y}}d_{i}'}} - \frac{i - 1}{\gamma}} ``` where $`T_{y}`$ is the length of text tokens and $`1/\gamma`$ is the minimum delay after each operation, computed as $`{1/\gamma} = {\sum_{j = 1}^{|X|}{{|X_{j}|}/T_{y}}}`$ (i.e., the averaged elapsed time for each token is used as the minimum delay). Lastly, $`d_{i}'`$ is defined as: ``` math d_{i}' = \begin{cases} d_{i} & {i = 0} \\ {\text{max}{(d_{i},{d_{i - 1}' + {1/\gamma}})}} & {i > 0} \end{cases} ``` The smaller the DAL, the better the system in terms of latency. For more discussions for DAL and latency-quality trade-off in SimulST, we direct readers to prior work Ma et al. ([2020a](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib38)); Arivazhagan et al. ([2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01172v3#bib.bib2)) for more details.
