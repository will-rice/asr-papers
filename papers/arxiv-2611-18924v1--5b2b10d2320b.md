###### Abstract

Supervisory control of multiple autonomous unmanned aerial vehicles
(UAVs) imposes high visual and cognitive demands on human operators,
particularly when communicating mission objectives and system
constraints. Voice command provides a natural interaction modality that
can reduce operator workload; however, its reliability is affected by
environmental noise, speech variability, and recognition errors. This
paper presents a dependable voice command framework for multi-UAV swarm
supervision using Small Language Models (SLMs). The proposed
architecture integrates an Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) module
with an SLM-based semantic processing layer to transform spoken operator
commands into structured task definitions for downstream multi-UAV
planning and execution. To evaluate robustness, an end-to-end assessment
pipeline is developed using a Probabilistic Context-Free Grammar (PCFG)
to generate synthetic supervisory commands, which are converted into
audio samples with varying operator accents and environmental
signal-to-noise ratios (SNR). The resulting speech signals are processed
by the ASR module, and the SLM performs contextual interpretation and
phonetic correction of transcriptions when required. The evaluation
isolates the resilience of the voice command pipeline under acoustic
degradation and speech variation, demonstrating reliable task generation
for multi-UAV supervision in challenging operational conditions.

## 1 Introduction

Supervisory control of multiple autonomous unmanned aerial vehicles
(UAVs) imposes high visual and cognitive demands on human operators
\[1\]. This is partly related to interacting with the system to monitor
mission progress and to inform the system of mission parameters. While
speech interaction provides a complementary communication modality,
unregulated text-to-speech (TTS) mechanisms often induce excessive
verbosity and cognitive overload \[2, 3\]. Unregulated speech input
relies on Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR), which degrades under
environmental noise and cross-cultural phonetic differences \[4\].
Standard implementations lack the structural constraints required for
dependable task generation in multi-agent environments.

For system input, voice control introduces distinct operational hazards.
Unregulated commands rely entirely on the transcription accuracy of the
ASR engine. In multi-UAV environments, ASR degrades under non-stationary
acoustic masking from rotors and cross-cultural phonetic variations
among operators. Relying on raw ASR transcripts causes referential
ambiguity which might lead to misclassification of target identities.
Static speech-to-text pipelines execute unsafe commands when acoustic
distortions alter the intended instruction.

To resolve transcription degradation, the input architecture must
validate operator intent before execution. This architecture processes
raw ASR outputs through a Small Language Model (SLM), which operates as
a semantic parser. It translates dialect-heavy and noisy transcripts
into task definitions (JSON). It can also adjust for phonetic
similarities that might have affected the ASR (e.g., the word "zoom"
might be transcribed as "soon" in ASR). This isolates the stochastic
uncertainty of speech recognition from the deterministic task allocation
logic of the drone swarm.

These design strategies shift the multimodal interface from reactive
alerting to a regulated, bidirectional communication channel. The input
policies applied to the audio input guarantee a safe task generation in
degraded operational contexts.

The contributions of this work are a semantic interpretation pipeline
based on a Small Language Model (SLM) that enables the extraction of
operator intent from culturally diverse speech inputs and their
transformation into structured JSON-based task representations for
downstream drone allocation. The pipeline further incorporates phonetic
adaptation mechanisms to mitigate applicable ASR transcription errors.
Additionally, this work introduces an end-to-end stochastic evaluation
methodology for quantifying system dependability under controlled
acoustic degradation conditions (SNR variations) and cross-cultural
dialect variability.

## 2 Related Work

In this section, we review multimodal interfaces in UAV swarm
supervisory control, focusing on the use of auditory feedback and speech
input, along with their limitations and challenges.

### 2.1 Multimodal Interfaces in Supervisory Control

Supervisory control of UAV swarms saturates the operator’s visual
channel \[1\]. To mitigate visual overload, multimodal interfaces
utilize bidirectional speech interaction. For system output, auditory
feedback reduces attentional demand \[5\] and decreases reaction times
to system deviations \[3\]. For system input, voice commands enable
operators to direct multiple agents without disengaging visual attention
from spatial displays \[6, 7\]. This interaction modality is
particularly critical in Manned-Unmanned Teaming (MUM-T) architectures,
where a human pilot must issue rapid tactical assignments to autonomous
aircraft while actively managing the leader vehicle \[8\].

However, unregulated speech interfaces introduce operational risks. On
the output side, continuous auditory signals cause alert fatigue and
channel saturation \[2\]. Empirical evaluations of remote vehicle
operators demonstrate that the efficacy of auditory feedback is strictly
bounded by operator workload. Dunn et al. \[9\] showed that while
auditory feedback improves decision-making under low workloads, it acts
as an external stressor and degrades spatial orientation under high
workloads. Uncalibrated auditory volume further deteriorates operator
accuracy \[10\].

On the input side, voice control introduces referential ambiguity and
relies heavily on the accuracy of Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR).
Command execution fails when ASR misclassifies target identities or
action parameters. Consequently, static speech interfaces are
insufficient for multi-agent supervision. Auditory output must be
workload-adaptive to prevent cognitive overload, and speech input must
undergo rigorous semantic validation to prevent unsafe command
execution. The architecture proposed in this paper formalizes this
bidirectional regulation.

### 2.2 Contextual and Cultural Variance in ASR

The operational reliability of voice-driven interfaces depends on the
transcription accuracy of the underlying Automatic Speech Recognition
(ASR) engine. In multi-UAV supervisory control, ASR performance degrades
due to two primary variables: environmental context and cultural
phonetic variance.

Regarding environmental context, outdoor UAV operations subject the
microphone to severe, non-stationary acoustic masking from wind and
rotor noise. Park and Na \[11\] demonstrated that such non-stationary
noise drastically reduces trigger-word detection and command recognition
in multi-UAV environments. While signal processing techniques like
spectral subtraction attempt to filter this noise, they frequently
induce signal distortion that further degrades transcription accuracy
\[11\].

Beyond acoustic noise, ASR exhibits severe performance disparities
across cultural and phonetic demographics. Koenecke et al. \[4\]
established that commercial ASR systems generate significantly higher
Word Error Rates (WER) for non-standard dialects and minority
demographics compared to standard phonetic profiles. These disparities
directly impact human-robot interaction. Bingol and Aydogmus \[12\]
demonstrated that regional dialect variations degrade robotic speech
recognition, necessitating custom acoustic modeling to handle
pronunciation discrepancies. Similarly, Korayem et al. \[13\] showed
that robotic voice command systems trained on native datasets experience
significant accuracy drops when operated by non-native speakers. In a
supervisory control environment, this cross-cultural bias means that
non-native speakers or operators with distinct regional accents face
higher rates of command misclassification.

The compounding effect of acoustic masking and cross-cultural phonetic
variation renders raw ASR output unreliable for critical multi-agent
task allocation. To guarantee system dependability, the interaction
pipeline must include a semantic reasoning layer capable of mapping
noisy, dialect-heavy transcriptions to exact, executable state
constraints.

### 2.3 Language Models in Multi-Agent Systems

Large Language Models (LLMs) currently perform high-level task
allocation and planning in robotic systems \[14, 15\]. These
architectures process natural language commands and generate sequential
robot actions. However, direct LLM-to-robot control introduces
significant operational risks in multi-agent environments. Cloud-based
LLMs exhibit high computational latency, which violates the real-time
constraints of swarm supervision. Furthermore, probabilistic text
generation causes hallucinations, leading to non-deterministic and
potentially catastrophic physical execution \[15\].

Our proposed architecture separates semantic interpretation from control
logic. Direct end-to-end AI planners struggle to scale with the
combinatorial complexity of multi-agent routing, often requiring
human-in-the-loop intervention to escape local minima \[16\]. Therefore,
instead of generating direct flight commands, the system employs a local
Small Language Model (SLM) strictly as a semantic parser. The SLM
translates transcribed speech into a structured format (JSON). This
structured output defines exact operational constraints and target
identifiers. Downstream, these constraints are processed by
deterministic multi-drone planning algorithms, such as cooperative task
allocation and behavior tree execution models \[8\]. This separation
isolates the stochastic uncertainty of language processing from the
safety-critical execution layer, ensuring reliable task generation under
bounded latency.

## 3 Speech Input

Speech input supports higher-level supervisory behaviors, including
contextual references and summary queries that reduce cognitive load and
improve situational awareness. However, it introduces challenges such as
ambiguity, recognition uncertainty, and error handling, particularly in
safety-critical scenarios where mistakes carry real consequences \[17\].
These issues are further compounded by accent variability and
differences in speech patterns, which can increase misinterpretation if
not addressed.

Speech input design must therefore balance efficiency with robustness by
incorporating validation, confirmation mechanisms, and clear feedback.
In practice, speech works best not as a replacement but as part of a
multimodal system that enhances attention management, interaction speed,
and overall operational control \[18\].

### 3.1 Speech Input Policy

Speech input provides an efficient interaction modality for supervisory
control tasks, enabling operators to issue commands without diverting
visual attention. However, speech recognition introduces uncertainty,
ambiguity, and potential recognition errors. To ensure robust
interaction, we define a regulated speech input policy governing command
interpretation, validation, and execution. This model is also presented
in Algorithm 1.

#### 3.1.1 Command Representation

To prevent referential ambiguity and enforce deterministic downstream
execution, the STP outputs a strict JSON payload. The recognized command
$`c`$ is formalized as:

```math
c=(i,t,p,k) \tag{1}
```

where:

- •
  $`i\in I`$ represents the inferred operational intent. The intent
  space $`I`$ is a closed set of 13 valid actions (e.g., INSPECT,
  SURVEY, ABORT, STATUS). Unclear intents strictly map to a fallback
  UNKNOWN state.
- •
  $`t\in T`$ identifies the addressed drone or team. The target space
  $`T`$ is restricted to predefined entity identifiers (e.g., drone one,
  team Red). Spatial locations are explicitly excluded from the target
  space.
- •
  $`p`$ contains an optional array of extracted task parameters (e.g.,
  specific target locations, required camera equipment).
- •
  $`k\in[0,1]`$ denotes the model’s confidence scalar regarding the
  semantic interpretation.

#### 3.1.2 Recognition Constraints

Speech interaction is guided by preferred grammatical patterns to reduce
ambiguity, rather than strictly enforcing predefined command structures.
This allows for flexibility in how users express commands, while
verifying that only inputs with a correctly interpreted intent structure
are accepted:

```math
intent(c)\notin I_{allowed}\Rightarrow reject(c) \tag{2}
```

where $`I_{allowed}`$ is a subset of $`I`$ which represents the set of
intents that the target can perform given its current condition. For
example, a drone which is not flying cannot execute a landing intent.

#### 3.1.3 Confidence-Based Validation

Command execution is conditioned on recognition confidence using an
execution threshold ($`\theta_{exec}`$) and a confirmation threshold
($`\theta_{confirm}`$):

```math
confidence(c)\geq\theta_{exec}\Rightarrow execute(c) \tag{3}
```

```math
\theta_{confirm}\leq confidence(c)<\theta_{exec}\Rightarrow requestConfirmation(c) \tag{4}
```

Low-confidence interpretations trigger clarification mechanisms rather
than direct execution.

#### 3.1.4 Ambiguity Resolution

Commands lacking a clear target or containing multiple possible
interpretations invoke disambiguation procedures:

```math
ambiguous(c)\Rightarrow initiateResolution(c) \tag{5}
```

Resolution strategies may include visual highlighting, clarification
prompts, or contextual inference.

#### 3.1.5 Error Recovery Policy

Recognition errors are mitigated through explicit feedback and
reversible actions. Critical commands (e.g., mission abort) require
confirmation even at high confidence:

```math
criticalIntent(c)\Rightarrow requireConfirmation(c) \tag{6}
```

This mechanism intends to prevent catastrophic consequences of
recognition failures.

## 4 Semantic Task Parser

The system operates as a sequential semantic extraction pipeline, shown
in Figure 1. An Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) module first
transcribes operator speech. A Small Language Model (SLM) performs joint
semantic and phonetic inference on the raw transcripts to mitigate
acoustic and dialectical distortions. The SLM resolves acoustically
confusable terms using domain context and maps the validated utterance
to a structured JSON payload defining exact intents and target
identifiers. This decouples the uncertainty of speech recognition from
the deterministic drone control.

Figure 1: The system pipeline. Raw operator audio is transcribed by the
ASR module. The SLM performs in-context phonetic alignment to resolve
transcription errors and extracts structured state constraints (JSON)
for downstream multi-agent task allocation.

### 4.1 Semantic Task Parser Architecture

Speech input in multi-agent environments amplifies referential ambiguity
and recognition uncertainty. To ensure safe execution, the SLM operates
as a regulated Semantic Task Parser (STP). The STP maps a raw natural
language query ($`q`$) transcribed by the ASR module into a structured,
executable task tuple, bypassing traditional rigid grammar constraints
in favor of in-context probabilistic mapping.

The STP supports multi-intent sequential splitting. For compound
queries, the SLM decomposes the utterance into a set of tuples (e.g.,
$`c_{1}=(\texttt{GOTO},t,p_{1},k_{1})`$ and
$`c_{2}=(\texttt{INSPECT},t,p_{2},k_{2})`$). The complete operational
state space bounding these parameters is defined in Table 1.

| Parameter       | Permitted Vocabulary Space                           |
| --------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| Intents ($`I`$) | INSPECT, SURVEY, SUSPEND, ABORT,                     |
|                 | STATUS, GOTO, LAND, ZOOM, VIDEO_ON,                  |
|                 | VIDEO_OFF, ALTITUDE, RTL, BATTERY                    |
| Targets ($`T`$) | drone one, drone two, drone three,                   |
|                 | drone four, drone five, all drones,                  |
|                 | team red, team blue, team green                      |
| Fallback        | UNKNOWN (Triggers $`k<\theta_{exec}`$ clarification) |

Table 1: Operational State Space for the Semantic Task Parser

#### 4.1.1 In-Context Phonetic Alignment

Raw ASR transcripts contain acoustic distortions. To mitigate these
errors before semantic mapping, the STP executes a mandatory phonetic
normalization process. The SLM identifies acoustic tokens that fail to
match the closed vocabulary spaces $`I`$ or $`T`$.

The STP applies structural context to guide this phonetic alignment. For
example, the system expects a numerical identifier following the keyword
"drone". If the ASR transcribes drone too or drone tree, the STP applies
an internal phonetic confusion rule to map the tokens to drone two and
drone three. The STP executes minimal token edits rather than full
sentence rewrites to preserve the original operational syntax.

#### 4.1.2 Confidence-Based Validation Policy

Command execution is conditioned on a prioritized decision sequence. The
STP applies phonetic normalization, maps the tokens to valid intents
($`i`$) and targets ($`t`$), extracts parameters ($`p`$), and assigns
the confidence scalar ($`k`$).

The STP penalizes uncertain translations to ensure system safety. If the
raw query $`q`$ required phonetic correction to achieve a valid state,
the SLM reduces the output confidence $`k`$ by a minimum penalty of
$`0.1`$. Commands with penalized confidence scalars that fall below the
execution threshold $`\theta_{exec}`$ trigger clarification mechanisms
rather than direct execution, preventing catastrophic consequences from
recognition failures.

## 5 Experimental Methodology

In this section, we present the implemented end-to-end pipeline to
evaluate the proposed architecture. This methodology evaluates the
system’s resilience against ASR uncertainty and referential ambiguity
across varied acoustic and cultural conditions.

### 5.1 Pipeline Implementation and Experimental Baseline

The experimental pipeline (Figure 2) integrates three components: speech
synthesis, speech transcription, and semantic parsing. Voice commands
are generated using Coqui-TTS (version 0.27.5). This engine was selected
due to its capability to synthesize speech conditioned on input sample
audio files, enabling voice cloning and accent variations. The output is
conditioned on 14 sample audio files \[19\] representing 11 distinct
regional accents. For transcription, the Automatic Speech Recognition
(ASR) module utilizes OpenAI’s Whisper model. Finally, the Semantic Task
Parser (STP) operates on Essential AI’s Rnj1 SLM, executed locally
within the LM Studio environment.

Figure 2: Pipeline used for experiments. The output from TTS passes
through the rest of the pipeline twice: (1) clean audio, (2) noisy
audio.

The SLM is evaluated under two distinct configurations to measure the
impact of the proposed phonetic alignment layer:

- •
  Without Phonetic Adjustment (Baseline): The SLM operates as a standard
  semantic parser. It receives the raw ASR transcript and maps it to the
  JSON tuple $`(i,t,p,k)`$ based exclusively on standard natural
  language comprehension and few-shot examples. It contains no explicit
  rules for acoustic distortions.
- •
  With Phonetic Adjustment: The SLM employs phonetic normalization
  rules. It actively executes joint semantic-phonetic inference, applies
  $`0.1`$ confidence penalty for modified tokens, and utilizes spatial
  and numerical context to resolve homophonic ASR errors.

### 5.2 Stochastic Fault Injection

Valid synthetic speech queries ($`q`$) were subjected to stochastic
perturbation vectors to emulate realistic operational hazards in field
deployments:

1.  1.  Acoustic Masking (Noisy Audio): To simulate environmental noise
        (e.g., drone rotors, wind), background acoustic interference was
        injected into the synthesized TTS audio files prior to ASR
        processing. This directly degraded the transcription accuracy.
2.  2.  Referential Omission: To model operator cognitive overload, the
        target parameter was truncated with probability $`P_{omit}=0.15`$
        (e.g., uttering "Return to base" instead of "Drone three, return to
        base"). This forces the SLM to rely on contextual inference or
        correctly assign the UNKNOWN target state.
3.  3.  Intent Corruption: Benign intents were subjected to forced acoustic
        mutations, misclassifying them as critical actions with probability
        $`P_{corrupt}=0.05`$ (e.g., misinterpreting "Report" as "Abort").

### 5.3 Dependability Metrics

System dependability is quantified through objective metrics
highlighting the engineering trade-off between safety and operational
efficiency:

- •
  Intent and Target Accuracy: The percentage of correctly classified
  operational intents ($`i`$) and target identifiers ($`t`$) parsed from
  the generated JSON tuples.
- •
  Catastrophic Failure Rate (CFR): The proportion of erroneously
  executed critical commands (e.g., mapping a distorted transcript to an
  unintended ABORT or LAND intent). The architectural goal is that
  $`\text{CFR}\to 0`$ irrespective of the noise variance $`\sigma^{2}`$,
  forcing highly noisy or uncertain inputs into clarification states
  rather than executing them.
- •
  Spurious Execution Rate (SER): The rate at which non-critical,
  corrupted commands bypass the validation filter (e.g., incorrectly
  assigning a STATUS update to the wrong drone).
- •
  Intervention Request Rate (IRR): The frequency of triggered
  clarification states ($`k<\theta_{exec}`$). This quantifies the
  operational cost of the fail-safe mechanism. A system with near-zero
  CFR but excessive IRR becomes operationally unusable due to constant
  operator prompting.

## 6 Results and Discussion

Table 2 summarizes the performance of the pipeline in terms of intent
and target classification accuracy under noisy and clean audio
conditions, comparing results with and without phonetic adjustment.

The results show a consistent improvement in performance when phonetic
adjustment is applied to the pipeline. This effect is particularly
pronounced under noisy audio conditions, where intent and target
classification accuracies increase from 76.01% to 84.64%, and from
51.27% to 70.43% respectively. The combined intent and target accuracy
also rises substantially, from 42.47% to 62.85%, indicating that
phonetic adjustment significantly enhances robustness in challenging
acoustic environments.

In clean audio conditions, performance is already relatively high, yet
phonetic adjustment still improves the results. Intent accuracy
increases from 84.84% to 91.50%, while target accuracy improves from
57.43% to 75.41%. The joint accuracy metric benefits as well, increasing
from 50.56% to 70.48%. Even in clean audio, the baseline target accuracy
remains relatively low (57.43%) because standard ASR models frequently
mistranscribe target identifiers, specifically numbers and colors into
homophones (e.g., ’too’ instead of ’two’, or ’read’ instead of ’red’).
Illustrating that the unadjusted SLM fails to map the input to valid
system entities. These improvements suggest that phonetic adjustment
assists classification in noise-related degraded audios and also
enhances overall model discrimination even in favorable conditions.

Overall, the results indicate that phonetic adjustment provides a
substantial and consistent benefit, with the largest relative
improvements observed in target and joint classification metrics,
particularly in noisy settings. This suggests that phonetic-level
normalization plays a critical role in improving model robustness and
generalization.

### 6.1 Intent Classification

To provide a more granular understanding of the system’s performance,
Figure 3 presents intent-wise classification accuracy in clean and noisy
conditions with phonetic adjustment. A prominent observation is the
significantly lower performance associated with "zoom" intent compared
to all other categories. Detailed inspection of debug logs suggests that
this degradation is a result of two primary factors: First, the
pronunciation of “zoom” exhibits substantial variability across
different accents, increasing acoustic ambiguity. Second, the term is
phonetically similar to a range of common words (e.g., “soon,” “zone,”
“seen,” “person” ¹¹ 1 In this case the complete sentence was ”Drone
four, zoon”. The ASR transcribed it as ”Drone person” concatenating
”four” and ”zoom”.), leading to frequent misrecognitions at the ASR
level. Indeed, multiple instances were observed where “zoom” was
transcribed as these acoustically analogous alternatives, directly
impacting downstream intent classification.

More broadly, the results indicate that noisy conditions consistently
degrade classification performance in all intents. However, this
degradation is not uniform. Intents with simpler or shorter phonetic
structures, such as "zoom" and "survey", are disproportionately
affected, likely due to increased susceptibility to noise-induced
ambiguity and lexical confusion. In contrast, intents such as "altitude"
demonstrate near-perfect classification accuracy even under noisy
conditions. This can be attributed to their greater phonetic complexity
and distinctiveness, which reduce overlap with other lexical items and
make them more robust to both noise and ASR errors.

Overall, these findings highlight that intent classification performance
is strongly influenced not only by acoustic conditions but also by the
intrinsic phonetic properties of the target vocabulary. Intents with
higher lexical uniqueness and phonetic richness appear inherently more
resilient, whereas shorter, acoustically confusable terms remain a
critical challenge for robust spoken language understanding systems.

### 6.2 Accents and Gender

The experiments are conducted using 14 distinct voice samples
representing 11 different accents, providing a diverse evaluation of
system performance across varied speech patterns. The male voice set
includes speakers from US Arkansas (ARK), Australia (AUS), Canada (CAN),
India (IND), Ireland (IRL), New Zealand (NZL), South Africa (SAF), and
US Virginia (USV). The female voice set includes speakers from Canada
(CAN), Britain (ENG), India (IND), Jamaica (JAM), Malaysia (MAL), and
New Zealand (NZL). This distribution ensures coverage of a broad range
of regional pronunciations and phonetic variations, allowing a more
comprehensive assessment of the system’s robustness to accent diversity.

Figure 4 presents the impact of phonetic adjustment on classification
performance in the aforementioned accent groups under both clean and
noisy conditions. A consistent improvement is observed across all
accents, with gains in clean speech remaining relatively modest and
tightly distributed, generally within a range of approximately +3 to +11
percentage points. In contrast, the improvements in noisy conditions are
larger and more variable, frequently exceeding +10 points and reaching
as high as +15.8 for Jamaican English. This indicates that while
phonetic adjustment provides incremental refinement in ideal acoustic
settings, its contribution becomes substantially more marked when signal
degradation is present.

A clear pattern also emerges: Accents with lower baseline performance,
such as Indian and South African English, exhibit comparatively larger
gains, particularly in noisy environments. Conversely, accents with
already high baseline performance, including American and Canadian
English, show smaller but still consistent improvements, reflecting
diminishing returns in near-ceiling conditions while confirming that the
method provides generalizable benefits across accent groups.

An additional clear observation is that phonetic adjustment yields
disproportionately larger gains for female voices under noisy
conditions. Specifically, the four largest improvements observed in the
noisy setting all correspond to female speakers. This trend becomes even
clearer when comparing gender pairs with the same accent. For example,
in the New Zealand subset, the female voice (NZL-F) shows a performance
increase of 13.2%, compared to 8.9% for the male counterpart (NZL-M). A
similar pattern appears for Indian accents, where IND-F improves by
11.8%, while IND-M shows a more modest improvement of 6.8%.

This disparity is especially interesting given that the baseline
classification accuracy for IND-F is already higher than that of IND-M
(70.7% versus 60.5%). Even with this higher starting point, the relative
improvement for the female voice remains substantially larger,
suggesting that phonetic adjustment may be especially effective for
female speech characteristics in degraded acoustic environments.

More broadly, the results indicate that while phonetic adjustment
consistently improves performance across all speaker groups, its impact
is generally larger in noisy conditions and appears to interact with
gender in a non-uniform manner. This may point to underlying differences
in how noise affects phonetic features in male versus female speech, or
how the adjustment mechanism compensates for these distortions.

Overall, the results highlight the role of phonetic adjustment as a
robustness-enhancing mechanism rather than merely a fine-tuning
strategy. Its disproportionate impact under noisy conditions underscores
its capacity to stabilize performance in the presence of acoustic
variability, particularly for underrepresented or phonetically distinct
accents.

### 6.3 Discussion

#### 6.3.1 Poor TTS Output

A detailed inspection of the application logs revealed that some
misclassifications originate from degraded TTS output, in certain cases
rendering the audio difficult to understand even for human listeners.
While this could be considered a complicating factor, it is
representative of real-world conditions, where speech input is often
affected by factors such as articulation variability, background noise,
or concurrent activities (e.g., eating or drinking). In this context,
imperfect TTS output provides a more rigorous evaluation of the
processing pipeline than idealized, high-quality speech.

#### 6.3.2 Classification Ambiguity

Classification errors can also occur when different commands have
similar meanings in natural language. For example, the system struggles
to distinguish between the intents "abort" and "suspend". In everyday
speech, both terms indicate stopping an action. In drone control, they
execute entirely different behaviors. "Abort" terminates the mission and
forces the drone to return or land. "Suspend" pauses the current task
and commands the drone to hover in place.

When operators use phrases like "stop what you are doing" or "halt", the
SLM may map the speech to the wrong intent. To resolve this overlap, the
SLM requires exact task definitions and few-shot examples in its prompt.
This ensures the model maps ambiguous human speech to the correct
operational state.

#### 6.3.3 Acoustic and Phonetic Ambiguity

ASR transcription errors occur mainly due to phonetic similarity,
different accents, and background noise. Analyzing the system logs shows
specific error patterns that cause standard string-matching and rigid
grammar rules to fail. First, the ASR frequently confuses numbers and
colors with words that sound the same (homophones). For example, it
transcribes "one" as "won", "two" as "to" or "too", "four" as "for" or
"fore", "blue" as "blew", and "red" as "read".

Second, varying accents and background noise break down specific
technical words. The target word "drone" has many variations in the
transcriptions, appearing as "drawn", "drown", "drownd", "roan", "room",
"drum", or "draw". Similarly, the command "zoom" appears as "zum",
"zam", "zom", "som", or "some". Lastly, the ASR often splits compound
words into unrelated text. It transcribes "waypoint" as "weightpoint",
"weight point", or "eight point". Furthermore, the ASR changes action
commands into words that sound similar but make no sense in this
context. For example, it transcribes "show feed" as "Sophie", "show" as
"shoal", and "drone two" as "drone tool".

These failures illustrate why the SLM phonetic adjustment is necessary,
it evaluates the text based on the context of drone operations.
Therefore, it maps a wrong transcription like "Sophie" back to the valid
command "show feed". This contextual correction recovers executable
commands from failed ASR transcriptions by interpreting the intended
meaning within the drone context, as shown in our results.

## 7 Conclusion and Future Work

This paper presented a speech interaction framework for supervising
multiple UAVs, designed to remain reliable under noise and accent
variation. The approach combines a Small Language Model (SLM) with a
regulated input policy to interpret speech and validate commands before
execution, reducing the risks associated with ASR errors. The SLM is
also instructed to take phonetic variations into consideration while
interpreting the commands. The results show that phonetic adjustment
improves both accuracy and system reliability, especially in noisy
conditions. In particular, the system achieves better intent and target
recognition while significantly lowering critical failure rates.
Overall, these findings demonstrate that structured and validated speech
interaction can serve as a practical and safe complement to traditional
control methods in multi-UAV systems.

Future work should focus on evaluating the system with real users in
operational environments, where factors such as spontaneous speech and
unpredictable noise may introduce additional challenges. Incorporating
adaptive mechanisms could allow the system to better handle individual
speaking styles and accents over time. Another promising direction is
integrating speech with other modalities, such as visual context or
gestures, to improve disambiguation and reduce the need for operator
intervention. Finally, refining uncertainty handling and confidence
thresholds could help balance safety and efficiency by minimizing
unnecessary confirmations while still preventing unsafe actions.

## Safe and Responsible Innovation

This work addresses safety-critical human–multi-UAV interaction, where
misinterpretation of speech commands can lead to operational risks. To
mitigate this, the proposed architecture incorporates confidence-based
validation, ambiguity resolution, and mandatory confirmation for
critical actions, reducing the likelihood of unsafe execution. The
system explicitly accounts for bias in Automatic Speech Recognition
(ASR) by evaluating performance across diverse accents and conditions,
promoting inclusivity. Synthetic data is used to avoid privacy concerns
associated with real user recordings. The framework is also designed to
prioritize human oversight, transparency, and fail-safe behavior in
deployment.
